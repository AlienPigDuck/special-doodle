Almost everything in the market right now — the chipmakers, the cloud companies, the power suppliers — has been riding the assumption that AI will keep expanding, endlessly, and that means building data centers, which means buying semiconductors. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index, which tracks America's chip stocks, fell more than twelve percent. Now, let's turn to Japan's own response — because this election in America is reshaping how Tokyo sees the world.

Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi has ordered a review of Japan's Active Cyber Defense Law — that's the law passed just last year to let Japan respond to serious cyberattacks. She wants to strengthen the country's ability to gather information before attacks happen. The government's approved a ten-billion-yen emergency package, and interestingly, it includes partnerships with American AI firms — Anthropic and OpenAI — to spot system vulnerabilities before they become public knowledge.

But here's the tension. The proposed changes would expand surveillance of domestic communications and require more companies to report breaches. And Japan's constitution protects the secrecy of communications. So this runs straight into a civil-liberties question that will need legal clarification before 2027. It's a genuinely difficult balance: how do you defend a country in cyberspace without eroding the privacy of the people you're defending?

And the threat is real. The Nikkei reports that North Korean hackers are now responsible for eighty-nine percent of all cryptocurrency theft in 2026. Takaichi has promised to clarify the details by year-end — including who qualifies for cash assistance, targeting at least thirty percent of workers.

President Putin has signed a decree placing Japan Tobacco's four Russian subsidiaries under state management. Essentially, Russia has seized them. And this isn't a small matter. Normally, Tokyo might lean on Washington for support — but with a new political order taking shape in America, that backing is suddenly far less certain. Experts warn that if trading concessions for protection only emboldens Moscow, other Japanese firms could be next. It's a quiet story, but it tells you something about how a changing America ripples all the way to a cigarette factory in Russia.
