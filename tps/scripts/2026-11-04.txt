Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Wednesday, the fourth of November, 2026. I'm so glad you're here.

We begin this morning with a quiet revolution in your wallet — or rather, the end of something in your wallet. Mitsubishi UFJ Financial Group, Japan's largest financial institution, says it will do away with the physical cash card entirely. When its new digital bank opens in early 2027, there will be no plastic card to carry. Instead, customers will use their smartphones — an app, a passcode, a QR code at the ATM.

Now, to understand why this matters, you have to appreciate how long the cash card has been a fixture of Japanese life. They were introduced here in 1969 — fifty-five years of pulling a card out at the bank machine. And this is the first time a major Japanese bank has eliminated them outright. Nikkei frames it as the start of an industry-wide shift.

The driving force is security. A traditional card needs only a four-digit PIN — one layer, and if someone has your card and that number, they're in. The new system stacks up multiple checks: your phone's own passcode, then logging into the app, then your account PIN. Three locks instead of one. That makes fraud substantially harder. And there's a business logic too — no cards means lower operating costs, and the bank says it will pass some of those savings back to customers in the form of higher deposit interest rates. So: more security, cheaper to run, and a little sweetener for savers. It's a window into where Japanese banking is heading.

And it pairs rather neatly with another banking story on the front page — one about defense rather than convenience. Thirteen regional banks, spread across Kyushu, Okinawa, and Yamagata, have formed a cybersecurity coalition. The idea is strength in numbers. A single regional bank simply doesn't have the staff or budget to fend off today's most sophisticated attacks — and increasingly those attacks are powered by AI. So these banks will coordinate on buying systems, training people, and assessing risk together. Yoshihisa Kasahara of Hyogo Bank put it plainly: these threats require collective defense. They're sharing intelligence through an industry body and running joint security drills. It's the financial equivalent of neighbors agreeing to watch each other's houses.

Now, from the banks to the towns they serve — and here the news is sobering. Across Japan, local governments are quietly confessing to fiscal trouble. In Ehime Prefecture, the city of Seiyo declared a "serious fiscal crisis" last October, and it genuinely shocked residents. We're talking about selling off public assets and cutting staff. Their rainy-day fund — once nearly five billion yen — has run critically low. Up in Saitama, Kuki City made a similar emergency declaration in August, freezing new hires and rebuilding its budget from zero. They project their reserves could run dry by fiscal 2030.

Here's the important context. For years, Japan has had essentially one famous cautionary tale: Yubari, the former coal town in Hokkaido that went bankrupt and ended up under official state supervision. These new declarations are different. They're happening *before* collapse, not after. And experts quoted by Nikkei say that's actually the healthy thing — getting the bad news out early, while there are still choices to make. The hard part is political: no mayor wants to be the one who sells the community center. But early visibility, they argue, is what preserves your options.

Let's turn now to Washington, because the American midterm elections have begun. Voting started yesterday, the third of November, and this is being read as a referendum on President Trump's second term. The Cook Political Report projects Democrats will reclaim the House — picking up 222 or more of the 435 seats, past the 218 needed for a majority. The Senate is tighter, essentially a coin toss, with six seats still in play.

There's a pattern here worth knowing: the party in the White House almost always loses House seats in a midterm. And this time there are extra headwinds — persistent inflation, and the fallout from the American air strikes on Iran back in February.

Which brings us to a fascinating wrinkle. Nine states redrew their electoral maps before this vote — partisan redistricting, what Americans call gerrymandering, after Trump pressed Republican governors to tilt the lines in their favor. But it didn't work the way anyone expected. In Texas, a map designed to flip five seats produced only one safe Republican seat, because some 2024 Trump voters defected — again, over inflation. Meanwhile Democratic California redrew its own map to fight back. So both sides gamed the system, and the result is surprisingly muddy. A reminder that voters don't always behave the way the mapmakers assume.

On Iran itself, a notable signal. President Trump said on the second that he would not immediately resume strikes after the election. Instead he's pointing toward diplomacy — talks brokered by Qatar. Vice President Vance called both sides "optimistic," while keeping military options on the table. That stance reportedly puts distance between Washington and Israeli Prime Minister Netanyahu, who's been pushing for strikes once the votes are counted.

Elsewhere in the world, a few developments. Australia's central bank raised its key interest rate to 4.85% — an eighteen-year high, and its fifth hike this year. Inflation there hit 4.3% in September, driven by Middle East energy shocks and, interestingly, rising technology prices from the AI boom. The pain is landing hardest on housing in Sydney.

In Europe, what started as French high school protests over education in September has grown into a cross-border youth movement — demonstrations in Germany, Italy, and Belgium. Gen Z activists are citing inequality, neglected schools, and military conscription, as governments shift money toward defense. Troublingly, masked far-left groups have attached themselves to the protests with violence, and both political extremes are trying to harness the energy — a worrying backdrop heading into France's 2027 election.

And a striking lapse in Seoul: the personal data of South Korean President Lee Jae-myung and 168 officials — names, birthdates, passport numbers — sat exposed on a malware-scanning website for five months, apparently traced back to his European tour in June. The Foreign Ministry says it wasn't a hack, but they're reissuing every affected passport.

On the corporate side, Apple's latest numbers show growth slowing. Revenue was up 12% to 114.5 billion dollars, but its forecast for the holiday quarter dips to 7-to-10%. And here's the unusual part: Apple has no standard iPhone launching for the crucial October-to-December season. Only the pricey foldable "iPhone Duo" — around 364,000 yen — and the Pro models. Memory shortages are to blame, and analysts are uneasy about that missing middle price tier. The stock slipped 4% after hours.

And that brings me to the one that caught my eye this morning — and it's about something we all take for granted: the parcel at your door. Yamato Holdings, the company behind Japan's famous black-cat delivery service, is making a deliberate choice to chase revenue over sheer volume. CEO Toshiyuki Sakurai says he'll cut 2,700 delivery hubs and raise rates on large corporate customers.

Think about what that signals. For years the story of delivery was *more* — more packages, more speed, more coverage, at ever-thinner margins, often on the backs of exhausted drivers. Yamato is essentially saying: enough. Fewer hubs, higher prices for the heaviest users, and a focus on whether the work actually pays. In a country facing a serious labor shortage, it's a quiet admission that the race to deliver everything, everywhere, instantly, may have reached its limit. Sometimes the most radical business decision is to do less.
