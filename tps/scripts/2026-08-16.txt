Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Sunday, the sixteenth of August, 2026, and yesterday was one of the most solemn days on the Japanese calendar, so that's where we'll begin.

August the fifteenth marks the anniversary of Japan's surrender in the Second World War. Yesterday was the eighty-first of those anniversaries. And the way a Japanese prime minister marks the day — the words they choose, the places they go, and the places they carefully avoid — is always watched closely, both at home and across the region. This year, that scrutiny is sharper than usual.

Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi attended the National Memorial Service for the War Dead at the Nippon Budokan in Tokyo. What she did not do was visit Yasukuni Shrine in person. Now, Yasukuni is the Shinto shrine that honors Japan's war dead — but it also enshrines convicted war criminals, and that makes any prime ministerial visit there deeply controversial with China and South Korea, who see it as glorifying Japan's wartime aggression.

Here's the delicate part. Takaichi has, in the past, committed to visiting Yasukuni. But rather than go herself, she sent party officials on her behalf — including the Liberal Democratic Party's Secretary-General, Toshiichi Suzuki, who made the pilgrimage in her place. It's an unusual arrangement, and it tells you a lot about the box she's in.

According to the Nikkei, there was also a notable shift in her language. In her memorial address, Takaichi left out the phrase "deep reflection" — a phrase her predecessors have used for years to acknowledge Japan's wartime conduct. She offered no explicit apology, emphasizing instead Japan's post-war commitment to peace. And the contrast was striking, because on the very same day, at the very same venue, the Emperor did use those words — "deep reflection." So you had the monarch and the head of government sounding rather different notes on the country's most sensitive anniversary.

The reaction from Beijing was swift. China's Foreign Ministry issued a formal protest over the shrine offering and the cabinet ministers' visits, accusing Japan of resurgent "militarism" and demanding the country "thoroughly break with militarism." Strong language, and it lands at an already tense moment in the region.

Why is Takaichi threading this needle so carefully? The Nikkei points to her numbers. Her support rating has dropped to 58 percent — down ten points from July. And here's the twist: some of that disappointment is coming from her own conservative base, who feel she hasn't delivered on the very promises, like visiting Yasukuni, that endeared her to them. So she's caught between conservative supporters who want her to go, and the diplomatic damage that going would cause. Sending officials in her place is her attempt to satisfy both, and it may end up satisfying neither.

The anniversary echoed elsewhere in the region, too. In Seoul, President Lee Jae-myung marked Liberation Day — that's how Korea remembers the same date, as the end of Japanese colonial rule — by calling on North Korea to open talks toward a permanent peace, and proposing three confidence-building measures to ease decades of hostility.

And there's a Russian dimension. Just two days earlier, on the thirteenth, Vladimir Putin made his first-ever visit to Sakhalin — the island at the heart of Japan's long-running territorial dispute over what Tokyo calls the Northern Territories. The Nikkei reads it as Moscow hardening its stance and signaling closer coordination with China. It's a reminder that Japan's diplomatic room to maneuver is narrowing on several fronts at once.

Let's turn to the economy, where the picture is steadier. Tomorrow, the seventeenth, Japan releases its GDP figures for the April-to-June quarter, and they're expected to show three straight quarters of growth. Private consumption is holding up — helped by strong wage gains and government fuel subsidies, which have cushioned households against supply shocks out of the Middle East.

On that Middle East point: Iran's Deputy Foreign Minister publicly rejected a claim by President Trump that the United States would soon declare the Strait of Hormuz American territory. His response was blunt — that no decree and no aircraft carrier can seize it. The Strait of Hormuz is the chokepoint through which a huge share of the world's oil passes, so this is exactly the kind of tension that keeps energy prices — and Japan's fuel subsidies — very much in the news.

There's a fascinating corporate money story on the front page. Japanese companies have sharply increased their holdings of commercial paper — that's short-term corporate debt — up 47 percent from a year earlier to 4.17 trillion yen. That's a seventeen-year high. The reason is simple and rather telling: with interest rates rising, companies are moving cash out of bank deposits paying half a percent and into commercial paper yielding around 1.2 percent. After decades of near-zero rates, Japanese firms are relearning how to make their idle cash work.

A few quick business notes. Otsuka Holdings wants to grow overseas sales of Pocari Sweat — that's the isotonic sports drink — to 100 billion yen by 2028, betting on record heatwaves to sell hydration as health. TOTO, the toilet maker, is pushing its Washlet — the heated bidet-seat — into the American market, teaming up with the Chicago White Sox and player Munetaka Murakami. U.S. sales have doubled in five years, though penetration is still just three percent, versus eighty percent back home. And Hitachi and Toshiba have delivered new Shinkansen train cars to Taiwan — part of a 124-billion-yen contract that will boost peak capacity by a quarter starting in 2027.

Before I go, the one that caught my eye — and it's a quiet, worrying one, tucked deep in the paper.

Japanese professional sport has never been more popular. Baseball and J-League soccer attendance are hitting all-time highs. But the foundation beneath all that is crumbling. Elementary-school baseball participation has fallen 40 percent in a decade, down to just 170,000 kids. Informal, pick-up playground baseball has withered to just 16 percent of ten- and eleven-year-olds.

The Nikkei lays out why: more than 80 percent of urban parks now restrict ball play. Summers are too hot to be outside. Dual-income parents can't shuttle children to practice. And screens compete for every spare minute. Remarkably, not even Shohei Ohtani's global stardom has reversed it. You have a generation of enthusiastic spectators growing up without ever picking up a bat — a sports culture cheering from the stands while its roots quietly dry out. It says something about childhood in modern Japan that goes well beyond baseball.
