Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Wednesday, the 11th of November, 2026, and we have a paper this morning that's really about one big question: what happens when the machines can do more, but the people are harder to find? Let's get into it.

Our lead story this morning is one of those quiet trends that tells you an enormous amount about where Japan is heading. Nikkei reports that small and medium-sized businesses — the backbone of this economy — are pouring money into digital technology at a pace we've never seen before. In the first half of this year, software investment by these smaller firms hit a record 578.4 billion yen. That's double what it was a year earlier. Double.

And here's why that matters. Japan has a labor shortage that isn't going away. The population is aging, the workforce is shrinking, and for years the worry was that small companies — hotels, construction firms, even rice wholesalers — simply couldn't afford to modernize. Now, facing a world where there just aren't enough workers, they're automating instead. Nikkei forecasts that software investment will jump nearly thirty percent in the next fiscal year.

Why should you care about rice wholesalers buying software? Because small and medium enterprises employ roughly seventy percent of Japan's workforce. For decades, these firms have been the drag on Japan's productivity numbers. If they can finally squeeze more output from fewer hands, that's not a minor efficiency story — that's the thing that decides whether Japan grows at all in the coming years.

Now, that's the optimistic version of the AI story. The front page gives us the complicated version — and it runs straight through American politics.

Start with Anthropic, the American AI company. It's going public — listing on the Nasdaq as soon as next week — but it's had to cut its valuation sharply. Nikkei reports the company now expects to be valued at somewhere between 200 and 220 trillion yen. That sounds enormous, and it is, but it's a markdown of thirty to forty percent from earlier expectations of around two trillion dollars. The reason? Last month's U.S. midterm elections, which the Democrats won convincingly. Investors now worry that a Democratic Congress will tighten regulation on the giant data centers that AI depends on — and that's cooled enthusiasm for anything with "AI" attached.

You can see exactly the same shadow falling over SoftBank Group. Masayoshi Son's company just posted a loss of 768 billion yen for the April-to-September half. A year ago, in that same period, it made a profit of nearly three trillion yen. What went wrong? About 600 billion yen in losses tied to American data-center investments, including a write-down of 72.5 billion yen on a construction project in Ohio.

Here's the context. Son went, in his own words, "all in" on the Trump administration's push for AI. He bet big, and he bet on a particular political climate. Then the midterms changed that climate. Nikkei frames this as a turning point — the moment Son's all-in strategy goes from bold vision to something that has to be tested against reality. When you stake everything on one bet, the bet can turn.

And the ripples from that election reach much further than balance sheets. Nikkei's front page describes the Middle East bracing for what it calls an era "without a patron." With the U.S. seen as pulling back, countries across the region are hedging. Saudi Arabia is leaning into talks hosted by China in Beijing, and even working to improve relations with Iran. The old certainty — that American power would underwrite the region's stability — is fraying, and in its place a kind of patchwork security order is emerging, with each country quietly arranging its own protection.

President Trump himself, returning to office, now says a renewed attack on Iran is "not necessary." He said that after meeting the Prime Minister of Qatar, who's mediating an end to the fighting. Nikkei reads this as Trump absorbing the lesson of the midterms — that high prices and foreign military entanglements helped drive the Republican defeat. But in the same breath, he's casting doubt on the legitimacy of the very election he lost ground in, questioning voting machines and pre-election polls on social media. Before the vote, he'd said he would accept the result. That echo of 2020 is, well, unmistakable. And there's a formal challenge brewing too: Republican Congressman Massie — who's expected to leave the party — has filed an impeachment resolution against Defense Secretary Hegseth over the Iran strikes, arguing he ignored the limits on his authority. Removal would need two-thirds of the Senate, so this is a statement more than a real threat. But it tells you how fractured things are.

Meanwhile, in Abu Dhabi, U.S.-brokered talks between Russia and Ukraine have resumed. On the table: security guarantees now that the fighting has stopped — and, notably, the shape of Europe's broader security arrangements. Ukraine, understandably, is approaching all of this warily.

Let's bring it home to Japan's own politics and economy, because the American turn is reshaping decisions here too.

The government, under Prime Minister Takaichi, is moving to revise Japan's three key national security documents at year's end. The new language will include something called "denial capability" — the idea of being able to stop an enemy invasion before it succeeds, through expanded deployment of drones and long-range missiles. Nikkei reports Japan is coordinating this strategy closely with the Trump administration, with an eye firmly on a possible crisis over Taiwan.

On trade, at the APEC leaders' summit, Japan is pushing for the final declaration to include language on "transparency" in supply chains. The target, unspoken but obvious, is China — its restrictions on rare earths and its industrial overproduction.

There's also a bit of party drama. During a vote on a consumption tax cut bill, four lawmakers — including former Prime Minister Ishiba — walked out, defying the party line. The LDP leadership has reprimanded them.

And a sobering number on the home front: real consumer spending fell 6.8 percent in September from a year earlier — the sharpest drop in six years, since the pandemic. Bad weather and stubborn inflation hit at the same time, and it's now the tenth straight month of year-on-year declines. That's going to weigh on growth.

A few quick corporate notes, and they share a theme — American manufacturing and shifting bets. Mitsubishi Heavy is spending 500 billion yen to expand gas turbine production from fiscal 2027, riding the wave of electricity demand from AI data centers. Honda is building a new plant in Ohio — a 470-billion-yen investment opening in 2029 — but notice this: it's for hybrids, not electric vehicles. Honda has pulled back from its all-EV plans and returned to hybrids as North American demand surges. And NTT has sold its entire 4.9 percent stake in NEC, booking a 189-billion-yen profit and, in Nikkei's words, closing a chapter on the old "Denden family" — the tight-knit group of firms that grew up around Japan's former telephone monopoly.

And now, the one that caught my eye. It's a reminder that in the rush toward automation, the old-fashioned matter of quality still bites. Olympus — the company most of us know for cameras, but which today is a major maker of medical endoscopes — has been hit with a fresh U.S. import ban. The FDA issued a warning letter in October citing problems at six of its sites, and five of its products are now barred from entering the United States. Olympus has already spent more than 80 billion yen trying to fix its quality problems, and it's now conducting a strategic review of its surgical business. It's a striking story because it cuts against the grain of everything else in today's paper. Everyone's investing in the future — AI, data centers, denial capability. But sometimes the hardest thing isn't building the next thing. It's getting the thing you already make reliably right.
