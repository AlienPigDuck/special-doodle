Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Sunday, the eighth of November, 2026, and I'm glad you're with me.

We're going to start this morning with a story about sports — but really, it's a story about money, data, and whether Japan can turn its stadiums and leagues into a much bigger business.

Nikkei leads this morning with the arrival of Red Bird Capital Partners in Japan. If you don't follow international football, the name to know here is AC Milan — the storied Italian club that Red Bird bought back in 2022 for roughly 210 billion yen. Red Bird is the largest sports investment fund in the world, managing some 3.3 trillion yen globally. And now it's coming to Japan, with a notable partner: the Development Bank of Japan. The DBJ will put several billion yen into a Red Bird-managed fund — and here's why that's significant. It's the first time a major Japanese bank has committed money to a sports fund at all.

So why does the government want in on this? It comes down to a number. Japan's sports industry accounts for just 1.83 percent of its economy. In Germany and the UK, it's two percent. That gap may sound small, but Tokyo sees real room to grow — the official goal is a sports market worth 15 trillion yen by 2030. Red Bird's pitch is that it knows how to squeeze more value out of a club or a league: smarter use of intellectual property, data analytics, commercial deals. The proof point they're offering? Since buying AC Milan, Red Bird says it has grown the club's commercial revenues two-and-a-half times over. The question for Japan is whether that playbook travels.

Now, from the business of sport to the business of security — and a sobering story on the front page about Europe, and what it might mean for Japan.

According to the Nikkei this morning, European governments are quietly preparing their citizens for a world where they can't fully count on the United States. In the wake of the US midterm election, and with what Nikkei describes as intensifying hybrid threats from Russia, several European governments are now telling ordinary people to stock up on emergency supplies. The UK is urging households to keep three days' worth of water, canned food, flashlights, and cash on hand. The European Union has set the same 72-hour benchmark. Estonia, closest to Russia, is asking for seven days.

And this isn't about tanks rolling across borders. It's about cyberattacks, disinformation, and sabotage — the kind of threats where the line between war and peace gets deliberately blurred. What makes this a Japan story is a warning from political scientist Glenn Morgan, quoted by Nikkei. He argues that America's structural drift away from Europe mirrors Japan's own situation — and that Japanese policymakers have been, in his word, "naïve" about imagining a future where they can't lean so heavily on Washington. It's a pointed critique, and worth sitting with.

That theme — resilience, and what happens when systems fail — runs through two more stories this morning.

Here at home, the Ministry of Health is designating around thirty advanced hospitals as critical infrastructure, under Japan's Economic Security law, with new cybersecurity rules due by fiscal 2028. At least one hospital per prefecture will be covered. They'll have to notify the government before buying major equipment, and the government can actually block an installation if it sees a security risk — think electronic medical records, surgical equipment, ICU systems. Why now? Because Japan has already been burned. In 2022, a ransomware attack forced the Osaka Acute Medical Center to halt emergency care. In 2024, Kirishima Hospital had to stop taking ambulances. When a hospital's computers go down, people don't get treated — it's that simple.

And on the money side of resilience, Credit Saison — a Japanese lender — has signed on with the Asian Development Bank to mobilize up to 500 million dollars, about 80 billion yen, over three years. The goal is lending to small businesses across emerging markets — Indonesia, the Philippines, and dozens of other ADB member countries. Credit Saison has form here: it's been doing small-business lending in India since 2018, and that portfolio has grown more than four-fold. The bigger picture, Nikkei notes, is that the ADB wants far more private money flowing into development — right now, private lending is just ten percent of what it finances.

Let's turn to the Japanese economy, where the news is genuinely encouraging. Nearly half of Japan's listed manufacturers — 47 percent of 239 companies — have raised their profit forecasts for the coming fiscal year. Nikkei reports that's the highest rate of upgrades since the 2008 financial crisis. What's driving it? Companies have managed to raise prices, there's strong AI-related demand out of the United States, and a weak yen is flattering overseas earnings. Asahi Kasei posted a nine percent profit jump to 173 billion yen — an eighteen-year high. Panasonic is targeting its best results in three years.

But there's a note of caution threaded through this. Strategists worry about what they're calling AI investment fatigue — the concern that the boom in data centers and chips could cool, especially with Democrats now gaining ground in Washington and talking about new regulation.

Which brings us to America, where the political ground has genuinely shifted. Nikkei reports the Democrats surged in the midterms — and strikingly, 109 of the party's winning candidates, around 40 percent, come from its progressive left. Backed by Senator Bernie Sanders, they're calling for a freeze on advanced AI data center construction, a ban on developing superhuman artificial intelligence, a five percent wealth tax, and an end to military aid to Israel. That's a dramatic agenda, and it puts them at odds with their own party's moderates — setting up a real fight heading toward 2028.

President Trump, facing this new Congress, is making his own moves. He's appointed Stephen Miller — the immigration hardliner and a loyalist of sixteen years — as his new Chief of Staff, replacing Susan Wiles. Nikkei's analysts suggest this points toward a more combative White House. Miller, they say, tends to stoke the president's impulses rather than temper them — which could make dealing with a Democratic Congress even harder.

And on foreign policy, Trump faces three paths on Iran: renewed military strikes, a negotiated settlement brokered through Qatar, or simply keeping up sanctions pressure. The clock matters here — come January, the opposition-controlled Congress takes over the budget.

One more international note with a Japanese thread: five lawmakers — from both the ruling party and the opposition, including former Prime Minister Yoshihiko Noda — traveled to The Hague to meet the International Criminal Court's chief prosecutor, Tomoko Akane. She's Japanese, and the court is under US sanctions. Their visit was a show of support, and it puts quiet pressure on Prime Minister Takaichi's government to back the ICC more firmly ahead of a treaty conference later this year.

And finally, the one that caught my eye this morning — and it's about what Japanese families are choosing not to buy.

Aeon, the big retailer, is tripling its rental clothing stores — from 20 to 60 by 2030, under a chain called LULUTI. The inventory jumps from 50,000 garments to 150,000. And what are they renting? Formal wear — the suits and dresses you'd wear to a wedding or a funeral. Here's the quietly telling part. Nikkei notes two things driving this: Japanese families are holding fewer ceremonies altogether, and when they do need formal clothes, more of them would simply rather rent than own. Why buy an expensive black suit you'll wear twice a decade? It's a small business story, but it's really a window into a careful, belt-tightening Japan — a country thinking hard about what it actually needs to keep.
