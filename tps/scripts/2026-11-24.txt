We'll start with the lead, which is a story about how Japan pays for medicine. The Development Bank of Japan — that's the government-backed policy investment bank, often just called the DBJ — has set up a new way of funding pharmaceuticals. And the clever part is in the structure. Normally, when you invest in a drug company, you invest in the company. The DBJ is going to do something different. It's going to invest drug by drug — backing individual projects, and sharing the cost of clinical trials directly with the pharmaceutical firms developing them.

Now, why does that matter? Because of something the industry calls the valley of death. Developing a new drug is wildly expensive, and the most expensive part comes near the end — the big Phase III trials, where a drug is tested on large numbers of patients. Those trials can cost anywhere from hundreds of millions of yen to hundreds of billions. For Japan's mid-sized drugmakers, that's often the point where promising research simply runs out of money. The DBJ wants to fill that gap — targeting roughly five projects, at tens of billions of yen each.

Nikkei notes this mirrors what's worked abroad. The investment firm Blackstone runs a similar fund, and achieved an eighty-six percent success rate at Phase III — the idea being that if you spread money across carefully chosen drugs, you smooth out the risk and keep research moving. For Japan, this is really about competitiveness. The country has deep scientific talent but has been losing ground in bringing new medicines to market. This is an attempt to fix the plumbing.

Now to the front page, and a story that reads almost like a corporate thriller. Jensen Huang — the CEO of NVIDIA, the company at the very heart of the AI boom — is back. You may recall he resigned in October amid infidelity allegations, complicated by the fact that the woman involved was suspected of having ties to Chinese espionage. For a company whose chips are central to American national security, that was no small matter.

As of yesterday, the U.S. government has confirmed there's no national security risk. NVIDIA's board voted unanimously to bring Huang back, after consulting with officials in Washington. President Trump endorsed the move, calling the return "patriotic." The market reaction was striking — NVIDIA's stock surged thirty percent intraday. The board says it will keep investigating whether any information leaked, but for now, the man most associated with the AI era is back in charge of the most influential company in it.

Staying with security, but a very different kind — Nikkei reports that China has demonstrated a serious new nuclear capability. Back in July, China's latest strategic submarine, the Type 096, fired a submarine-launched ballistic missile into the South Pacific. The missile, the JL-3, has a range of more than ten thousand kilometers. That puts most of the American mainland within reach from the South China Sea — waters China treats as a kind of protected sanctuary.

The significance here is dual strike capability: China can now threaten a nuclear response from both land and sea. It operates around fifteen submarines and has expanded production from one shipyard to three. The backdrop, of course, is Taiwan — the aim is to deter the United States from intervening in any future conflict. It's a reminder that the region's balance of power is shifting steadily, and quietly, beneath the surface.

Closer to home, TEPCO — Tokyo Electric Power — is reorganizing itself around Fukushima. By fiscal 2027, it will create a new Fukushima Restoration and Decommissioning Headquarters, pulling together three separate divisions that had been working in fragments. Crucially, it's moving hiring and procurement authority down into that unit, so it can recruit specialized technical talent on its own. The work ahead is extraordinarily difficult — recovering melted nuclear debris — and TEPCO plans to lean on AI-driven robotics and foreign expertise, including firms like Amentum and Cavendish Nuclear. More than fifteen years after the disaster, this is about keeping the momentum going.

Let's turn to politics and diplomacy. In South Korea, President Lee Jae-myung has reshuffled his foreign policy team. Career diplomat Im Woon-soon becomes foreign minister, and — notably — former Justice Minister Kang Geum-sil will head the National Intelligence Service, the first woman ever to lead the agency. The signal from Seoul is continuity: strengthen the alliance with Washington, while keeping the door to North Korea open.

And on North Korea — Kim Jong-un spoke on the twenty-second, saying he'll strengthen ties with countries that "respect sovereignty." It's a carefully coded message. Trump has invited dialogue around APEC, and Kim is signaling he's not closed to it — but on his terms, which include recognition as a nuclear power. He's leaving the door ajar without walking through it.

Here in Japan, Prime Minister Takaichi used her UN General Assembly address to reaffirm Japan's support for the International Criminal Court — a pointed stance, given the Trump administration's open hostility toward the court. And here's a detail I found telling: an AI analysis of all 192 countries' UN speeches found that ninety percent of nations avoided saying anything about the ICC at all — neither for nor against. Nobody wants to anger Washington. Takaichi's clarity stands out against that silence.

On the subject of restraint — or the lack of it — a UN conference on lethal autonomous weapons, so-called killer robots, closed on the twenty-first with no agreement. Around a hundred countries wanted binding rules. But the United States and Russia argued existing law is enough, and because the process requires full consensus, nothing moved. The next deadline isn't until 2028.

And in a story that genuinely gives one pause — German public broadcasters, citing Western intelligence, report that Russia may be preparing to launch a nuclear-armed satellite. It's called "Sputnik S," designed to destroy other satellites by detonating in space. NATO allies warn the launch window could open within sixty days — possibly as soon as December. We should treat intelligence reports with care, but the militarization of space is clearly accelerating.

A few quick business notes. The trading house Sumitomo Corporation has launched a forty-five billion yen private equity fund, targeting thirty to forty mid-sized Japanese manufacturers and retailers — and it'll use its own AI tools to make them more efficient. Anthropic, the American maker of the Claude AI model, says its adoption in Japan has grown tenfold in a year, with clients like Hitachi, Fujitsu and NEC; its Tokyo team has gone from three people to fifty. And Hino Motors is raising its annual holidays to a hundred and twenty-five days — the most in the auto industry — following Toyota, Suzuki and Mazda, as carmakers compete for younger workers in a tight labor market.

And that brings me to the one that caught my eye — tucked deep in the business pages. Isuzu Motors, the truck maker, has teamed up with Toshiba and four partners on a pilot program starting this December to automatically measure how truck drivers spend their time at logistics hubs. Now, that might sound dry — but think about what it touches. Japan is wrestling with a real driver shortage, and a chunk of a driver's day isn't spent driving at all — it's spent waiting, loading, standing around at distribution centers. If you can measure that waiting automatically, you can start to shrink it. It's the kind of unglamorous, behind-the-scenes problem that, solved well, quietly keeps a whole country's goods moving.
