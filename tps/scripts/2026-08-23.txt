Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Sunday, the twenty-third of August. I'm glad you're here.

Let's begin this morning with a warning — a rather grave one — from one of the elder statesmen of Japanese politics.

Tadamori Oshima is ninety-three years old. He served as Speaker of Japan's House of Councillors — the upper house of parliament — for six and a half years, longer than anyone in history. And he sat down with the Nikkei for an extensive interview about the direction of Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi's government. His message, in short: slow down.

Oshima argues that the Takaichi administration is rushing legislation through parliament, cutting short the committee debate where laws are actually scrutinized and shaped. And he frames this not as a matter of parliamentary etiquette, but as something more fundamental. Democracy, he says, requires time — time to deliberate, to compromise across party lines, to talk openly. Abandon that time, and you drift, in his words, toward authoritarianism.

He offered the Prime Minister a Japanese phrase that I rather love: *yukkuri isoge* — "hurry slowly." Go slowly in order to go fast. The idea being that skipping the hard, patient work of persuasion doesn't save you time in the end. It just leaves you with laws that lack legitimacy and consensus.

Now, here's the context that gives this real weight. Takaichi's coalition is a minority in the upper house. It doesn't command an automatic majority. So the temptation to move quickly, to force things through while you can, is real — but so is the risk. When you don't have the votes, deliberation isn't a luxury. It's how you build the support you need. Oshima, having presided over that chamber for years, knows exactly how fragile that balance is.

From the halls of parliament to the classrooms — and a story that says something quietly striking about Japanese society.

You'd expect that in a country with fewer and fewer eighteen-year-olds, getting into university would be getting easier. There are more seats and fewer students to fill them. And yet, the Nikkei reports that the number of *rōnin* — that's the term for students who fail to get into their chosen university and spend a year, or more, studying to try again — has surged for the first time in seven years. Just over seventy-one thousand people sat entrance exams in 2026 as repeat candidates. And the number of people taking the exam for a *second* time — so, on their third attempt overall — jumped a hundred and fifty percent.

Why, when there's more capacity than ever? Because universities have quietly changed how they admit people. They've shifted toward recommendation-based and comprehensive admissions — essays, interviews, portfolios — and away from the traditional general entrance exam. Those exam slots have shrunk to just forty-six percent of all admissions, down twenty percentage points since the year 2000.

So here's the squeeze. Ambitious students aiming for elite universities now compete for a smaller and smaller pool of exam seats. Score thresholds climb. And students who fall just short decide it's worth another year — sometimes two — to try again. It's a reminder that "more supply" doesn't help you if the bottleneck moves somewhere else.

Now to business at home, where the government is thinking hard about the future of Japan's small firms.

The Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry — METI — wants to eliminate inheritance and gift taxes on business succession for small and mid-sized companies, but only for those that raise wages or invest in growth. Right now those taxes are merely deferred, and forgiven only under conditions — which leaves owners with a vague, looming obligation hanging over them. And that uncertainty, METI argues, makes them reluctant to reinvest or hand the business to the next generation early. Make the exemption explicit, and you give owners the confidence to pass the torch and free up capital. It's a targeted fix for one of Japan's slow-burning problems: aging business owners with no clear path to succession.

Staying with the theme of an aging society — the staffing firm Pasona will start helping local governments draft individual evacuation plans for elderly and vulnerable residents ahead of disasters. This is a legal obligation municipalities already have, but the completion rate is just fifteen percent. Pasona will deploy more than three hundred and fifty care professionals and a cloud system that matches residents needing help with local volunteers in real time. The goal is more than fifty municipalities by 2030. In a country as earthquake-prone as Japan, this is exactly the kind of unglamorous groundwork that saves lives.

Let's turn abroad, where trade tensions are flaring in a very old-fashioned way.

The United States has imposed fifty-percent tariffs on Canada — hitting around thirty-two billion dollars of Canadian imports. And here's the historical detail that made me sit up: the Trump administration invoked the 1930 Smoot-Hawley Act to do it. That's the notorious tariff law widely blamed for deepening the Great Depression — not a name you invoke lightly. Negotiations collapsed after Washington shifted its demands at the last minute over autos, dairy, and alcohol rules. Ottawa is furious and has vowed to retaliate on the eighth of September. And it throws the whole USMCA trade pact — the successor to NAFTA — into question.

Closer to home, a sharper kind of pressure. Chinese and Russian warships conducted a coordinated passage in July — from the Arctic, through Japanese waters, down to the South China Sea. The Nikkei's read is that this was a message: in any conflict over Taiwan, Beijing and Moscow want to tie down Japan's Self-Defense Forces on multiple fronts at once, and slow any American reinforcement. Vladimir Putin's visit to Sakhalin on the thirteenth of August underlined the point. And in a related move, a U.S. Navy P-8A patrol aircraft transited the Taiwan Strait on the twenty-first. All of this comes ahead of Xi–Trump talks in September, where Taiwan will loom large.

In corporate news, a couple of quick but telling items. Renesas — the chipmaker whose 2011 shutdown once paralyzed the world's carmakers — restarted production at a plant near last month's earthquake epicenter in just seven days. Reinforced cleanrooms, backup materials, trained staff. Lessons learned, and it shows.

And here's a lovely underdog story. Astar, a seventy-person motor-parts maker in Akita, born out of the 2008 financial crisis, just won a major contract supplying lightweight aluminum coil motors to Anduril — the American defense-drone startup. A big domestic electronics conglomerate had passed on Astar's technology. American capital saw the value the Japanese giants missed.

And that brings me to the one that caught my eye today — and it's a health story with real consequences for patients.

Pharmaceutical companies are delaying the launch of already-approved cancer and diabetes drugs *in Japan*. Eli Lilly's oral breast-cancer therapy and a Novo Nordisk diabetes injection have both cleared for sale — but the makers are stalling on price negotiations. Why? Because Japan's drug prices are low. And under the Trump administration's "most favored nation" rules, if a company sells cheaply in Japan, it could be forced to offer matching discounts back in the far larger U.S. market.

So Japanese patients may end up waiting for approved medicines — not because of anything wrong with the drug, but because of American pricing politics. The Nikkei notes Japan accounts for just five and a half percent of global innovative-drug revenue, despite being a major economy, and METI is now scrambling to overhaul its pricing rules for 2027 to keep Japan attractive. It's a quiet story with a very human edge — a reminder that in a connected world, a policy written in Washington can reach the pharmacy shelf in Osaka.
