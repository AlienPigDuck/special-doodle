We start with SoftBank Group, because the numbers here are genuinely striking. Nikkei reports this morning that SoftBank will issue one trillion yen in corporate bonds aimed at individual investors. That's the largest issuance of its kind ever by a Japanese company. And these aren't bonds for pension funds and big institutions — these are bonds pitched at ordinary people, retail investors, who can buy in.

What's the money for? Artificial intelligence, essentially. SoftBank wants to fund what it's calling "physical AI" — that's robots controlled by autonomous AI systems, machines that can think and act in the real world. Some of the money will also go to repaying bridge loans tied to its investments in OpenAI, the company behind ChatGPT.

Now, here's the interesting part. SoftBank can borrow this much because its credit has actually strengthened, largely thanks to Arm Holdings — that's the British chip-design company SoftBank controls, whose shares have been climbing. So a stronger balance sheet lets SoftBank take on more debt.

But — and this is the catch that connects to nearly everything else in today's paper — interest rates are rising. These bonds are expected to carry rates in the four percent range. And that's expensive. It reflects a world where borrowing costs are climbing everywhere, driven by three things: tension in the Middle East, fierce competition for AI investment money, and fiscal worries across the major economies.

Let me put that in context, because it's the story underneath the story. Japanese ten-year government bond yields have hit 2.945 percent — that's a thirty-year high, the highest since 1996. In the United States, thirty-year yields have reached 5.3 percent. And part of what's pushing rates up is that the AI giants themselves — Google and others — have issued something like 250 billion dollars in debt. When the biggest companies in the world are all borrowing enormous sums at once, they compete with governments for lenders' money, and the price of borrowing goes up for everyone. So SoftBank's trillion-yen bet is happening at exactly the moment that betting big has gotten more costly.

Now, from the cost of borrowing to the cost of getting old — and this is the story on the front page that I'd encourage you to sit with. Nikkei reports that entry fees for private nursing homes in central Tokyo have now passed ten million yen. That's ten million and ten thousand yen, to be precise, as of January — up thirty-nine percent in just eight years. Monthly fees have risen fifteen percent on top of that. Over five years, the median household is looking at 2.79 million yen in costs.

Here's why that matters so much. Japan's national pension pays around sixty thousand yen a month. Sixty thousand. You cannot fund a ten-million-yen entry fee on that. So what's happening is that elderly people are being pushed out of Tokyo entirely, relocating to neighboring prefectures where the average entry fee is closer to 3.3 million yen — a fraction of the capital's.

And there's a quieter, almost aching detail in the reporting. In eighty percent of the households Nikkei surveyed, families simply aren't talking about how they'll pay for elderly care. It's the conversation nobody wants to have, in the country with the oldest population on earth. This is what the paper calls the "final resting place" crisis, and it's not going away.

Sitting right next to that story is a curious contrast. Takashimaya — the venerable department store — is lowering the entry age for its exclusive private concierge service. This is what's called "gaishō" in Japan, a kind of white-glove personal shopping service for wealthy clients. Takashimaya is dropping the eligibility age from twenty-seven to twenty-two — the first change in nineteen years. They're partnering with NTT Docomo to sift through customer data and find young, wealthy shoppers — the "new rich," as the paper puts it, benefiting from a rising stock market. So on one page, the elderly priced out of care; on the next, twenty-two-year-olds courted for luxury. That's Japan in 2026.

And a note of remembrance: Sakane Masahiro, who led the construction-equipment maker Komatsu from 2001 to 2007, has died at eighty-five. He's remembered for taking Komatsu through its first-ever operating loss and turning it into a genuine turnaround — famous for a management philosophy he called "duntotsu," roughly meaning "clearly superior." Under him, Komatsu won the Deming Prize for quality management in 2008. A significant figure in postwar Japanese industry.

Let's turn to politics, and to pressure from Washington. Nikkei reports the Trump administration is leaning hard on Prime Minister Takaichi over defense. The U.S. wants Japan spending 3.5 percent of its economy on the military — Japan is currently at two. Washington also wants more money for hosting American bases in Japan, and it's pressing on that 5.5 trillion yen investment commitment — about 87 billion dollars — that Japan pledged last year. These talks are heating up ahead of the U.S. midterm elections in November.

And Japan isn't alone in feeling that squeeze. Taiwan is boosting its own defense budget to 5.6 trillion yen, aiming for five percent of its economy by 2030. Its 2027 spending will hit three percent — a record — as it faces both Chinese military pressure and those same American requests to spend more.

Meanwhile, at home, the middle class is hurting. Households earning five to six million yen a year have seen their real incomes fall about ten percent since before the pandemic. Part of the culprit is something called bracket creep — as wages rise with inflation, people get pushed into higher tax brackets, but the tax system hasn't been adjusted for that inflation. So you earn more on paper, and take home less in real terms. Families are pulling back — spending on entertainment and travel is down between twelve and twenty-three percent.
