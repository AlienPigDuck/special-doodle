Our lead story this morning is a troubling one, and it touches the very heart of Japan's scientific ambitions. Nikkei reports that Fugaku — one of the most powerful supercomputers in the world, run by the research institute RIKEN — has suffered a cyberattack. And this isn't just a government machine humming away in a lab. Fugaku is used by some of Japan's biggest industrial names: Toyota, Mitsubishi Heavy Industries, Hitachi, Sumitomo Rubber. These companies run their most advanced research on it — simulations, modeling, the kind of work that underpins cutting-edge technology.

Now, according to the Nikkei, the information of as many as four thousand, two hundred and twenty-two users may have leaked. And we're not just talking about names and passwords. The attackers may have made off with authentication credentials and, crucially, program code — the actual building blocks of corporate research. If that's true, it's a direct hit on Japan's industrial competitiveness. Years of proprietary work, potentially walking out the door. It puts enormous pressure on both government and industry to get serious about cybersecurity, fast.

And that pressure is already visible in our next story, which connects neatly. Google Cloud is giving the Japanese government early access to its most advanced AI model — a security-focused system Nikkei calls "Gemini 4 Carbon Four Cyber." The timing is no accident. In October alone, Japan recorded two hundred and thirty-three cyberattacks — the highest monthly figure ever. So Google is stepping in to help government agencies hunt for vulnerabilities. The National Cyber Office and the Digital Agency will both have access, and banks and telecom companies are expected to follow. So you can see the shape of things here: the threat is rising, Japan's own defenses are being tested — Fugaku being exhibit A — and the government is leaning on American technology to shore things up.

Let's turn to diplomacy, because Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi is on the move. She's departed for the Philippines to attend a series of ASEAN-related meetings, and from there she'll head to the APEC summit in Shenzhen. But the real headline hanging over this trip isn't Takaichi's itinerary — it's what's happening on the nineteenth of November. On that day, the United States, China, and Russia are set to hold a trilateral summit. President Trump, President Xi Jinping, and President Putin, all in one room. Nikkei notes this would be the first summit of its kind in history.

And here's why Tokyo is watching nervously. The paper raises the specter of a "new Yalta" — a reference to the 1945 conference where the great powers carved up the postwar world among themselves. The worry in Japan is that these three giants could sit down to reshape the international order — the agenda reportedly includes nuclear disarmament and the Iran question — and simply leave Japan on the sidelines. For a country that depends so heavily on the rules-based order, being left out of the room where decisions get made is a genuine anxiety.

Takaichi isn't arriving empty-handed, though. Nikkei reports Japan is pushing to create what you might call an Asian version of the International Energy Agency — a Petroleum Security Task Force, built with the Philippines, Indonesia, and Thailand. The idea is shared oil stockpiling, so that if trouble in the Middle East chokes off supply, these countries can lean on each other. And that brings us to why the Middle East is so much on everyone's mind.

Iran's Revolutionary Guard says it struck a US air-defense facility in Bahrain — six drones, in what appears to be retaliation for American airstrikes in southern Iran. This is dangerous territory, quite literally, because it's all unfolding near the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow channel through which a huge share of the world's oil passes. Any military clash there sends a shiver through global energy markets — and explains exactly why Japan is so keen to build up those regional stockpiles.

Now, closer to home, let's talk about the Japanese economy, because the news isn't cheerful. Nikkei reports that real GDP for the July-to-September quarter shrank — at an annualized rate of 0.1 percent. That's the first contraction in four quarters. And the culprit is personal consumption, which fell for the second straight quarter. People are simply holding onto their money. Inflation has made households defensive, and pensioners in particular are cutting back. The paper's conclusion is blunt: without price stability, there's no real recovery.

And you can see that squeeze playing out across several stories. Take Japanese beef. The top-grade A5 wagyu hit a record high at the Tokyo meat market — three thousand and fifty-one yen per kilogram. Why? Feed costs have soared, the weak yen hurts, and shipments are down six and a half percent. Meanwhile, nearly a tenth of production now goes overseas, so exports are competing with domestic shoppers. Ranchers are struggling so badly that their numbers have dropped by a fifth in just three years. And ordinary consumers, feeling the pinch, are quietly stepping away from beef altogether.

Housing tells the same story. The average price of a new condominium in Tokyo's 23 wards has reached roughly one hundred and eighty million yen — think somewhere north of a million US dollars. That's pushing middle-income families right out of the market. So the Liberal Democratic Party is considering a new tax measure: heavier taxation on condos resold within five years of purchase, aimed at cooling off the speculation driving prices up. It could land in next year's tax reform package.

A couple of quick notes on money and defense. Individual investors are pouring into corporate bonds — a record three-point-two trillion yen this year, driven by huge issuances from SoftBank Group and Rakuten, with average yields up to 3.9 percent as interest rates climb. And on defense: the government had floated a five-year spending plan in the range of seventy to eighty trillion yen, but Nikkei reports that's now being trimmed toward fifty to sixty trillion — with grumbling from within the ruling party that, once you account for the weak yen and existing contracts, it amounts to a real-terms cut.

Before we go, here's the one that caught my eye — and it's a story about something you probably never think about until you're stuck at one. Traffic lights. Nikkei reports that Tokyo's Metropolitan Police have been running experiments since 2023 on AI-controlled traffic signals — lights that think, essentially, adjusting their timing based on real conditions at the intersection. And the results were good enough that starting in April of 2027, they're rolling it out across roughly eight thousand locations throughout Tokyo. The payoff? Congestion at intersections cut by about ten percent.

I love this one because it's such a quiet revolution. No summit, no scandal — just a smarter traffic light, multiplied eight thousand times, giving millions of commuters a few minutes of their lives back every day. It's artificial intelligence not as a headline, but as the texture of ordinary life. And somehow, in a week full of cyberattacks and great-power summits, there's something reassuring about AI being put to work simply to get everyone home a little sooner.
