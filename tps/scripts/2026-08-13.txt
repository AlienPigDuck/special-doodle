It's Thursday, the thirteenth of August, 2026, and there's a lot moving today — a big shift in how Americans, and now the Japanese, will trade stocks; the passing of two men who shaped the economies of Japan and China; and a story from deeper in the paper about a decoy airplane and a catering truck. Let's get into it.

We begin with a change that sounds technical but could quietly reshape where money in Japan goes. Nikkei reports this morning that five of Japan's biggest online brokerages — SBI Securities, Rakuten Securities, Monex, Matsui, and three others — are preparing to let ordinary Japanese investors trade American stocks during Tokyo business hours, starting in December.

Here's the background. Right now, if you're in Japan and you want to buy shares of, say, an American tech giant, you're mostly doing it in the dead of night — because Wall Street is open when Tokyo is asleep. But in December, the New York Stock Exchange and Nasdaq are moving to nearly round-the-clock trading — twenty-three hours a day. And that means a Japanese investor could buy and sell American shares over morning coffee, in real time, alongside their domestic portfolio.

Now, why does this matter beyond convenience? Because it changes the competition for Japanese savings. Nikkei frames this as both an opportunity and a real risk. The opportunity is obvious — American markets become more accessible. The risk is that Japanese money flows out of Japanese companies and into foreign ones. When U.S. stocks are easier to buy, more liquid, and — in many investors' eyes — more attractive, that's a structural problem for Tokyo. It puts pressure on Japan's own listed companies to prove they can compete globally, not just at home. This is one of those stories where nothing dramatic happens on day one, but the direction of travel matters enormously.

Now to two men whose deaths were announced this week — figures who, in very different ways, built the modern economies of Japan and China.

First, Hiroshi Okuda, the former president of Toyota, who died on the eighth of August at ninety-three. Okuda was a landmark figure. He was the first person to lead Toyota who wasn't a member of the founding Toyoda family — and from 1995 to 2006, he transformed the company. He pushed Toyota to build cars all over the world, opening the company's Kentucky plant. He championed the Prius — the world's first mass-produced hybrid — a bet that looked risky at the time and now looks visionary. Under him, Toyota's sales doubled. And he didn't stop at the company. As the first head of Japan's unified business federation, Keidanren, he became a powerful voice for economic reform, working alongside Prime Minister Koizumi. He was, in short, one of the architects of corporate Japan as we know it.

And on the twelfth of August, China lost Zhu Rongji, its premier from 1998 to 2003, at ninety-seven. They called him the "economic emperor," and the nickname fit. Zhu was the reformer who dismantled the rigid machinery of China's planned economy. He steered China into the World Trade Organization in 2001 — the moment that unlocked China's rise as the world's factory floor. He cut inflation with iron discipline, consolidated bloated ministries, and eliminated some four million state-enterprise jobs. That last figure tells you what kind of man he was — decisive, sometimes brutal, willing to inflict short-term pain. His reforms laid the foundation for three decades of extraordinary growth. Though, as Nikkei notes, critics point out that the same era widened inequality and lit the fuse on China's real-estate speculation. Two very consequential lives, remembered this week.

Speaking of China, let's stay there, because there's a story about who controls the raw materials of the modern economy. Nikkei reports that an Australian company, Arafula Rare Earths, has begun developing something called the Nolands Project — a mine and refinery aiming to supply five percent of the world's neodymium-praseodymium by 2029. If those names mean nothing to you, here's why they matter: these rare earths go into the powerful magnets inside electric-vehicle motors and wind turbines. And right now, China has a near-monopoly on them — which gives Beijing enormous leverage. The Nolands Project, backed by the American and Australian governments, is part of what's being called "friendshoring" — building supply chains among allies to reduce the risk of China turning off the tap.

Which brings us naturally to the White House, where the picture, according to Nikkei's front page, is one of a presidency losing momentum. With roughly forty percent of his term gone, President Trump faces a string of setbacks — his reciprocal tariffs blocked in the courts, his Ukraine ceasefire mediation stalled, his Iran military operations delivering limited results. And with midterm elections looming in November, analysts quoted in the paper warn of a real danger: that domestic frustration pushes Trump toward bolder, riskier moves abroad. They flag possible NATO withdrawal, his ambitions over Greenland, and the frightening prospect of a U.S.-China deal that trades away Taiwan. The concern, as Nikkei puts it, is a president reaching for legacy over prudence.

And that Iran conflict has produced one genuinely astonishing detail — this is my one that caught my eye, so I'll save it for the end.

Here at home, Prime Minister Takaichi is facing pressure on two fronts. First, disaster relief: her government has designated the city of Yatsushiro, in Kumamoto Prefecture, as a severely-stricken disaster area after the July 28th earthquake. That designation matters practically — it lets small businesses borrow reconstruction money on favorable terms.

But politically, the Prime Minister is taking heat from inside her own party. Nikkei reports that senior figures in the Liberal Democratic Party — former party chief Moriyama, former Defense Minister Inada, and former Prime Minister Ishiba — have publicly criticized Takaichi's decision to cut the food consumption tax to one percent for two years. Their complaint isn't just the policy — it's that she did it unilaterally, without consulting the party or laying out how it'll be paid for. And reading between the lines, this is about more than tax. The next LDP leadership election is in September 2027, and her critics appear to be organizing around fiscal discipline as a way to challenge her. Watch this space.

On defense, Takaichi's government plans to deploy artificial intelligence across the Self-Defense Forces' command systems — to keep pace with the U.S. and China, where decisions increasingly move at machine speed. But security experts sound a note of caution: if Japan simply adopts American AI wholesale, it risks handing over its own strategic independence. They're urging Japan to build systems it actually controls.

In business, a major alliance in the semiconductor world. Sony and Taiwan's TSMC — the world's most important chipmaker — are joining forces to mass-produce next-generation image sensors at Sony's factory in Kumamoto, starting in 2029. The total investment approaches one trillion yen, including government subsidies — Sony putting in 465 billion, TSMC 282 billion. Image sensors are the eyes inside your phone's camera, and this deepens Japan's push to rebuild itself as a chipmaking hub.

And now, the one that caught my eye. Remember that Iran assassination threat? The Washington Post, cited by Nikkei, reports that when President Trump left Turkey on the eighth of July, he pulled off an elaborate deception. He boarded an older Air Force plane in full view of the cameras — reporters climbed aboard with him. But then he slipped off, reportedly via a catering truck, and transferred to a smaller military transport for the actual flight across the Atlantic. The plane the press was on? A decoy. U.S. intelligence had judged the assassination threat credible, and Trump himself called the flight "dangerous," claiming he was at the top of Iran's list. A catering truck. Sometimes the real world outdoes the spy novels.
