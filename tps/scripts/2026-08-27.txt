Let's begin there.

The lead story in the Nikkei this morning is about credit unions — and if that sounds a little dry, stay with me, because it matters more than it looks. Credit unions in Japan are small, community-based lenders. They serve local businesses and households, especially in regions far from Tokyo. And they are in real trouble. Nikkei reports that seventeen of them fell into the red in the fiscal year that ended in March — up from just five the year before. That's more than a tripling in a single year.

So what happened? In a word: interest rates. For decades, Japan lived with near-zero rates, and these institutions loaded up on government bonds because there was nothing else safe to buy. But when rates rise, the value of older, low-yielding bonds falls. And now that's coming home to roost. Across the sector, bond-related losses hit four hundred and twenty-four point eight billion yen — nearly two point seven times the year before.

The individual cases really bring it home. The Tajima Credit Union, in Hyogo, posted its first deficit in thirteen years — a loss of nearly twenty billion yen. Even more striking, the Ehime Credit Union recorded its first-ever loss since it was founded in 1951. Think about that — seventy-five years without a losing year, and now this. And there's a bigger number lurking beneath the surface: across two hundred and thirty-seven credit unions, unrealized losses total two point six trillion yen. These are institutions holding up local economies that are already shrinking, with fewer people and eroding deposits. So this is really a test of Japan's rural financial safety net — and it's one to keep watching.

Now, from that cautionary tale of the old low-rate world, to a big bet on the future. Kioxia — Japan's semiconductor memory giant — is going to build a new plant in Iwate Prefecture, in the northeast, with investment topping one trillion yen. President Hiroo Ota will announce it today at the Prime Minister's residence. The factory will make NAND flash memory — that's the storage that lives inside phones, laptops, and, increasingly, the vast data centers powering artificial intelligence. It'll expand Kioxia's existing complex in Kitakami and start operating around 2029. The strategic picture here is clear: AI is driving enormous demand for data storage, and Kioxia wants to compete head-to-head with South Korea's Samsung and SK Hynix. This is Japan trying to hold serious ground in the global chip race.

Staying with the government's economic agenda — Tokyo has just approved an emergency energy package, and the reason is the Middle East. There are fresh risks around the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow chokepoint through which a huge share of the world's oil passes. Japan imports nearly all its oil, so this is existential. The centerpiece: subsidies for companies that source crude oil and naphtha through alternative routes that bypass the strait — using ports like Fujairah in the UAE and Yanbu in Saudi Arabia — with support to build pipeline alternatives too. Prime Minister Takaichi has told her government to bring legislation to the autumn session of the Diet, and she's framing this around a phrase worth noting: "energy sovereignty."

And here's a striking counterpoint from the international pages. Nikkei reports that Iran is considering charging what it calls "service fees" on ships passing through Hormuz — for navigation safety, rescue, refueling. The model is Turkey, which collects tolls on the Bosporus and Dardanelles, where fees have jumped eight-fold in four years. The US Treasury estimates Iran could pull in up to forty billion dollars a year if it went ahead. So on one side, Tehran eyeing a windfall from that waterway — and on the other, Tokyo spending public money to route around it entirely.

Let me stay overseas for a moment, because there are a few stories that speak to a world under strain. In The Hague, the International Criminal Court's prosecutor, Akane, spoke by videoconference to Japanese reporters, pushing back hard against Trump administration sanctions — sanctions aimed at her personally. She called it a "crisis of the rule of law," and said, memorably, that "justice always prevails over its opposite." She warned that dismantling the ICC would usher in an era of "might over law." She even revealed the personal cost — her credit cards have been revoked. What's notable in the Japanese coverage is the domestic criticism: Japan's response has been muted, and members of the ruling party and civil society worry that softness undermines Tokyo's whole diplomatic identity, which rests on international law — the very principle Japan invokes against Chinese coercion.

And the trade wars grind on. Canada is hitting back at Washington with retaliatory tariffs of up to fifty percent, taking effect September the eighth, after talks collapsed at the last hour. The sticking point? The Trump administration reportedly insisted Canada adopt its own tariffs to contain China — and Ottawa refused. Canada's pairing the tariffs with a seven hundred and fifty million Canadian dollar support package for affected workers and small businesses.

Back home, a few quick items worth your attention. The government will buy back one and a half million tons of stored rice in September — because, remarkably, Japan now has too much rice. Premium rice prices in Niigata have collapsed forty percent year-on-year, gutting farm incomes. It's a reminder that food security cuts both ways — this time the threat is overproduction and prices too low to sustain farmers.

Two more forward-looking stories. Japan is roughly eight-fold-ing its H3 rocket budget — requesting twenty-three and a half billion yen — to launch more often. The goal is to go from a launch every two months to one every two weeks. And police budgets are shifting toward AI-driven drones; the government wants eighty thousand domestically-made units by 2030, with the company TerraDrone signing a four billion yen contract for interceptor drones.

And finally, the one that caught my eye — and it comes from the aftermath of the Kumamoto earthquake, one month on. Amid the recovery, two very different uses of technology stood out. University of Tokyo researchers set up what they call "Disaster Support One-Stop Centers" — AI platforms that tell survivors exactly which aid programs they qualify for and which documents they need. That's the kind of bureaucratic maze that, in a crisis, can leave the most vulnerable stranded.

But the one that really got me is grassroots. A beauty salon operator named Kasuga Aiko built a crowdsourced information platform called "Imacoconavi" — roughly, "where-are-we-now navigation." It lets residents post real-time updates: which shops are open, where you can find fuel, what evacuation routes look like. In one week, it reached five hundred thousand users. Think about that — half a million people, drawn to something built not by the government, not by a tech giant, but by a local salon owner who saw a gap and filled it. And that's really the deeper story here. As Japan's workforce shrinks and official disaster response gets stretched thin, it's ordinary people, armed with a phone and a good idea, stepping into the space. There's something quietly hopeful in that.
