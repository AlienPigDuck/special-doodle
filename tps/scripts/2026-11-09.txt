Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Monday, the ninth of November, 2026. I'm glad you're here.

We'll start today with a story that brings together two things you don't often hear in the same sentence — government banking and soccer clubs. The Nikkei reports this morning that Redbird Capital Partners, the American investment fund, is coming to Japan with backing from the Japan Policy Investment Bank. And this is a big deal, partly because of who Redbird is. They own AC Milan, one of the most famous football clubs in Italy. They hold stakes in the Boston Red Sox and in Liverpool. They are, by some measures, the largest sports investment fund in the world.

The Japan Policy Investment Bank — which is government-affiliated — plans to put in tens of billions of yen. And what's striking is why. Redbird's specialty is squeezing more commercial value out of sports. When they bought AC Milan, they tripled the club's commercial revenue in three years, using data analytics and smart management of intellectual property — merchandise, broadcasting rights, the brand itself.

Here's the context that makes this matter. Japan's entire sports sector is worth over ten trillion yen, but that's just 1.83 percent of the country's economy. In Germany and the UK, it's considerably larger. The Japanese government has wanted to grow this to fifteen trillion yen — originally by 2030, now pushed back to 2035. The quiet admission buried in this story is that Japan can't get there alone. It needs foreign capital and foreign know-how to learn how to turn its baseball, its soccer, its sumo into real money. This is the first time a major Japanese bank has invested in a global sports fund. Keep an eye on it.

Now, from one kind of partnership to a very different kind of self-reliance. Across the front page, there's a sobering piece about Europe. After the US midterm elections, European governments are bracing for an America that may simply care less about their defense. And they're telling their own citizens to get ready. The European Union has launched what it calls a 72-hour preparedness campaign — asking ordinary people to keep enough food, water, and supplies to survive three days on their own. Estonia, which sits right on Russia's doorstep, is asking for seven days.

Why? Because Russia's so-called hybrid attacks are intensifying — more than 54 confirmed incidents in six months, mixing cyberattacks, disinformation, and physical sabotage. The Nikkei quotes political scientists who argue that the postwar order — what they call "American Europe," meaning a Europe protected by American power — is collapsing, and this time irreversibly. NATO, they say, must "Europeanize," carry more of its own weight, even as it still depends on the American nuclear umbrella. It's a genuinely historic shift, and it's happening quickly.

That same American retreat threads through several of today's stories. The US midterms handed Democrats control of both chambers of Congress, starting in January. And it's a particular kind of Democrat — 109 progressive candidates won their races, an 89 percent win rate. Figures like Bernie Sanders and Ilhan Omar now have real weight, pushing for wealth taxes, pauses on AI development, and firm pro-Palestinian positions. That puts them at odds with the moderates in their own party, but it also clips the Trump administration's power.

And President Trump is responding by moving right, not left. He's appointed Stephen Miller — the hardline architect of his immigration policy — as White House Chief of Staff, replacing the more pragmatic Susan Wiles. The change takes effect today. Miller is a confrontational figure, and his appointment signals a sharper ideological edge to come.

All of this leaves Trump with narrowing options on Iran. The Nikkei lays out three scenarios: a major bombing campaign against nuclear sites, with a third US aircraft carrier arriving soon; a negotiated deal brokered by Qatar, trading sanctions relief for inspections; or simply holding the current line. Each carries real risk — not least because voters appear to have blamed him for the inflation that followed earlier strikes, and because the new Democratic Congress will soon control the purse strings.

Here's a quiet but telling story in the same vein. A delegation of Japanese lawmakers — from five parties, including the ruling Liberal Democratic Party and Japan Innovation — traveled to The Hague to meet Tomoko Akane, the prosecutor of the International Criminal Court. They went to reaffirm Japan's support for the court, even though the United States has sanctioned it. Japan's government is promising up to 1.5 billion yen as an advance on next year's contributions. The message is subtle but unmistakable: on the rule of law, Japan is leaning toward Europe, not toward Washington.

Closer to home, the Takaichi administration got some uncomfortable news from Japan's Board of Auditors. The annual audit flagged 144.5 billion yen in questionable spending — 250 separate cases. Among them, the Small and Medium Enterprise Agency ran 1.7 times over budget on a system rebuild, and the Housing Finance Agency handed out 30 billion yen in mortgages to properties that weren't even residential. Prime Minister Takaichi has talked up what she calls "responsible active fiscal policy" — spend boldly, but spend well. This audit is a reminder that the second half of that phrase is the hard part.

There's brighter economic news, though. Among roughly 500 listed manufacturers, 47 percent have raised their earnings forecasts since October. That's the highest proportion since the years right after the 2008 Lehman crisis. Companies are successfully passing on price increases, benefiting from the AI investment boom, and from the weak yen. Although — one note of caution the Nikkei flags — Toyota has reset its assumption to 157 yen to the dollar, meaning that currency cushion is starting to thin.

And a story I found genuinely encouraging. Japan's prefectures are doubling their mid-career technical hiring — to 1,040 positions for the coming year. The problem is stark: 46 prefectures report shortages, and 80 percent say civil engineering is the most critical gap, as the country's roads, bridges, and tunnels age. So local governments are getting creative — offering scholarships worth up to 15 million yen to repay student loans, speeding up hiring, dropping exam delays. They're finally competing with the private sector on flexibility and benefits. It's a small sign of government adapting to reality.

Two quick international notes before my favorite of the day. Russia has expanded its seizure of Japan Tobacco's assets — now seven subsidiaries placed under temporary state management, affecting about one trillion yen in equity. Tokyo has sent officials to Moscow demanding a reversal. And in China, a Japanese executive from Astellas Pharma, detained on espionage charges, is expected to be released in December after completing a three-and-a-half-year sentence. Five other Japanese citizens remain imprisoned under Beijing's tightened security laws.

And now, the one that caught my eye. Tucked into the front page, amid all the talk of war and finance, is this: Japanese calligraphy — shodo, the art of brush and ink — is set to be registered by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage. UNESCO praised it for fostering cultural identity and creativity. But what struck me was the timing. This recognition arrives precisely as handwriting itself is fading from daily life, replaced by screens and keyboards. There's a lovely line in the piece about society "rediscovering the warmth and individuality" of pen-and-brush work. In a world where every message looks identical, there's something human about the particular slant of your own hand. It's a quiet form of resistance, really — and a reminder that some things are worth slowing down for.
