It's Friday, the thirtieth of October, 2026, and we've got a day of big structural shifts — in the skies over Japan, in the global bond markets, and inside the Chinese Communist Party. Let's get into it.

We begin with a story that marks a real turning point for Japanese aviation. Nikkei reports this morning that ANA Holdings — the parent company of All Nippon Airways — is moving to take full ownership of two mid-sized regional carriers. Those are Solaseed Air, based in Miyazaki, and AirDo, based in Sapporo. ANA already works closely with both, but under this plan, which takes effect in April 2028, it would fully absorb the holding company that controls them.

Now, why does this matter? Two reasons. First, Japan's domestic air travel market is losing money. There's simply too much capacity — too many seats chasing too few passengers — and that's squeezing everyone. Second, and this is the bigger structural point: until recently, the government capped how much of a smaller airline a major carrier could own. It was known as the "twenty percent rule," designed to protect competition and keep regional airlines independent. In May, the government scrapped that rule, effectively opening the door for the big players to take control of the small ones. This ANA deal is the first of its kind — and Nikkei expects it to kick off a wave of consolidation across the industry. So keep an eye on this one. When the first domino falls in a market this crowded, others tend to follow.

Staying with corporate accountability, but in a very different sector — the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry is preparing to issue a business improvement order to Chubu Electric Power. This follows a data falsification scandal at the Hamaoka nuclear plant. The order, expected in early November, will demand clearer accountability for executives, stronger compliance systems, and a new management structure. Chubu Electric will have until the end of November to submit an improvement plan. Data integrity at a nuclear facility is about as serious as corporate trust gets in Japan, so this is a significant rebuke.

Now let's turn to money and the government's finances, because there's a quietly important debate unfolding over something economists call the "inflation tax." Here's the idea. Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi, in her recent policy address, deliberately dropped the language criticizing what she'd called "excessive austerity." Her government is betting on growth to manage Japan's enormous debt. And the way that works is striking: Nikkei notes that while Japan's real growth rate is around 1.6 percent, nominal GDP — that's growth including inflation — rose 12 percent, and that's what brought the debt-to-GDP ratio down.

But here's the catch, and why bondholders are uneasy. When inflation shrinks the real value of government debt, that's essentially a transfer of wealth — from the people who lent the government money, to the government itself. Investors in Japanese government bonds are watching this closely, because an inflation-led strategy quietly erodes what they're owed. It's a subtle mechanism, but it's at the heart of a growing nervousness in the bond market.

And that connects to another Takaichi decision this morning. The government will begin phasing out its gasoline subsidies starting in December. Right now, that subsidy keeps prices around 170 yen a liter, but it's costing more than ten billion yen a day. So the plan is to raise prices by five yen a month — gradually letting them rise. The reason is simple arithmetic: that daily cost adds up to roughly three trillion yen a year, and it's getting in the way of the supplementary budget and talk of consumption tax cuts. Separate winter support for electricity and gas bills will still go ahead.

Let's cross over to Europe, because there's major news from the European Central Bank. Christine Lagarde, the ECB president, has announced she'll step down early — on the seventh of February, 2027, rather than serving out her full term through October. There's speculation she's headed to chair the World Economic Forum. Her departure is likely to set off a political tug-of-war across Europe over who replaces her — and the timing is delicate.

Why delicate? Because the ECB held its policy rate steady at 2.5 percent this week, balancing persistent inflation worries against a cooling economy. And part of that cooling pressure is coming from France, where government bond yields are climbing toward five percent. Political and fiscal instability in Paris has widened the gap between French and German borrowing costs — and with a presidential election looming, there's a real risk of EU decision-making grinding to a halt. So you have the central bank losing its leader just as the bloc's second-largest economy wobbles. That's a lot of uncertainty at once.

Across the Atlantic, the picture is brighter. The US economy grew at an annualized 3.4 percent in the third quarter, driven by strong consumer spending, with AI investment still booming. Markets now put the odds of a December rate hike at eighty percent.

Here at home, briefly, a few political notes. The ruling Liberal Democratic Party, along with Ishin and the Democratic Party for the People, have agreed to cut thirty-one proportional-representation seats in the lower house. They're adopting a modified Sainte-Laguë formula — that's a method of allocating seats meant to give smaller parties a fairer shake. But here's the interesting detail Nikkei flags: under the new system, the LDP's share of seats would actually rise, from 68 percent to 72 percent. So electoral reform billed as fairer may, in practice, concentrate more power.

And on defense — North Korea fired more than a dozen short-range ballistic missiles into the Sea of Japan, the twelfth such launch this year. Japan's Defense Ministry estimates they fell outside the exclusive economic zone. Relatedly, the Self-Defense Forces are planning to deploy underwater drones for surveillance from fiscal 2027, including detection and attack types — a response to China's fleet of some sixty submarines.

In corporate news, the AI boom keeps rewriting records. Samsung Electronics posted its highest-ever semiconductor operating profit — 106.5 trillion won, about 12 trillion yen — on a wave of demand for AI memory chips, with margins above 72 percent. Though its foundry business is still in the red.

But there's a counterweight worth noting. The big American tech firms — Microsoft, Google, Meta, and Oracle — are beginning to fine-tune their AI spending. Microsoft alone is trimming its 2026 capital expenditure by five billion dollars, prioritizing free cash flow. So even amid the boom, discipline is creeping in.

And a stark contrast in autos: Japanese carmakers saw their China production fall 32 percent, with Honda down a staggering ninety percent. As China's gasoline car market shrinks by sixty percent, Japanese brands are struggling to compete on electric vehicles. Meanwhile Volkswagen posted a 4.6 billion euro quarterly loss, with a six-billion-euro writedown at Porsche, as China sales dropped 39 percent. The company aims to cut fifty thousand jobs by 2030. The old auto order is genuinely shifting.

And now, the one that caught my eye — and it's a hopeful one, tucked deeper in today's international pages. In Serbia, a student movement has forced a strongman government out of power through the ballot box. What's remarkable is how they did it. The so-called "Student List" ran a leaderless, nonviolent protest campaign — and sustained it for fourteen years. Fourteen years. No single charismatic figure to arrest, no leader to co-opt, just a persistent, decentralized movement that refused to give up. In an era when we talk so much about democratic backsliding, here's a case of patient, peaceful organizing actually succeeding. It's a reminder that change doesn't always arrive in a single dramatic moment — sometimes it's the slow accumulation of a generation refusing to look away.
