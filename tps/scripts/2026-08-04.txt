Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Tuesday, the fourth of August, 2026, and there's a lot moving today, so let's get into it.

We begin with something that hasn't happened in fifteen years. On July thirty-first, Japan and the United States stepped into the currency markets together to buy yen — a coordinated intervention to stop the currency's slide against the dollar. Nikkei reports the yen briefly jumped to around 155.20 per dollar, its strongest level in about three months. Now, let me explain why this matters, because the word "intervention" can sound abstract. When the yen falls, imports get more expensive — food, energy, everything Japan buys from abroad. And this time there was a second, scarier layer. Japanese government bonds were falling in price at the same time the currency was falling. Officials call this "simultaneous selling," and it's the kind of thing that keeps finance ministers awake at night, because it suggests investors are losing confidence in Japan itself.

The last time Japan and the U.S. acted together like this was after the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami. So the fact that they're doing it again tells you how serious the anxiety is. Japan's Finance Minister, Katayama Satsuki, said the move was coordinated with U.S. Treasury Secretary Bessent, who signaled he's ready to do it again if needed. And here's the knock-on effect: markets now think the Bank of Japan may speed up its interest rate hikes, with a move in September looking more likely. Because behind all of this is a structural story — years of the central bank keeping rates very low, combined with fragile government finances. The intervention treats the symptom. The underlying condition is still there.

And that condition connects directly to our next story. The Liberal Democratic Party's tax committees approved a plan on August third to cut the consumption tax on food to just one percent for two years, starting in April 2027. The party's tax chief, Onodera Itsunori, called it the fulfillment of a campaign promise for what they've branded "near-zero" tax on food. Now — you can imagine the tension here. On one hand, groceries have gotten expensive, and a tax cut is real relief for households. On the other, this is exactly the kind of fiscal loosening that makes bond markets nervous, at the very moment Japan just spent money defending its currency. Nikkei reports some LDP members raised precisely these concerns about fiscal credibility, but the chairman used his authority to push consensus through. A cabinet decision is expected in early August, with the bill going to the Diet this autumn.

Let's turn to cars, because there are two related stories worth putting side by side. Suzuki announced it will export a lightweight electric vehicle to Europe by 2027. It's an adaptation of its "eSky" kei-car — and if that term is new to you, a kei-car is Japan's category of very small, very efficient minicars, built to strict size and engine limits, hugely popular here for their low cost. Suzuki will build the car at its Lakeside Factory in Shizuoka, and the European version will get about 310 kilometers of range on a full charge. What's notable is the timing: EU regulations are shifting to favor small EVs, and Suzuki sees an opening. This is the first time Suzuki has mass-exported a kei-car since the Cappuccino sports car back in the 1990s — a name that'll make longtime car fans smile.

But Suzuki isn't the only one who spotted this opportunity. China's BYD launched its own kei-EV — the "Laco" — on July twenty-eighth, and it pulled in more than 700 pre-orders within days. BYD is aiming at rural Japan, at towns where gas stations are actually closing down, which quietly makes electric cars more appealing. So watch this space: a Japanese carmaker heading into Europe with a small EV, and a Chinese carmaker moving into rural Japan with the same idea. The kei-car, that most Japanese of vehicles, is becoming a global battleground.

Now to the Middle East, where the news is genuinely better — at least for the moment. President Trump announced on August first that the U.S. will halt its attacks on Iran, on one condition: the "immediate and complete opening" of the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway through which a huge share of the world's oil flows. Secretary Bessent signaled openness to fresh negotiations. But — and this is important — Iran's Foreign Ministry denied any talks are actually happening and said its blockade stance won't change until U.S. military action stops. So we have a declared halt, but no agreement. The markets, though, exhaled: Brent crude fell about ten percent, down to around 81 dollars a barrel, a three-week low, as fears of a supply cutoff eased.

There's a fascinating footnote to Russia in all of this. You might expect Russia's oil revenue to be surging, given Middle East tensions and relaxed U.S. sanctions back in March. But Nikkei reports it hasn't. The reason is Ukraine — its drone attacks on Russian refineries, storage sites, and ports have crippled Russia's ability to export refined petroleum products. So even as crude volumes rise, the money isn't flowing the way Moscow hoped.

Closer to home, Prime Minister Takaichi visited the areas hit by the Kumamoto earthquake, which has now claimed 38 lives. She announced two billion yen in emergency funding and a formal disaster-area designation. And the paper flags a grim complication: nighttime temperatures above 25 degrees, raising the risk of heat illness among survivors. That's part of a wider heat story across the region — South Korea just recorded its highest temperature ever, 42.5 degrees Celsius, with deaths and crop damage prompting new severe-heat warnings.

A couple of quick business notes. Nissan returned to profit in the April-to-June quarter — a 37 billion yen net gain — after eight straight quarters of losses, though sales are still weak globally amid fierce Chinese competition. And copper is climbing toward record highs, driven, of all things, by artificial intelligence: all those data centers need enormous amounts of it.

And that brings me to the one that caught my eye — and it's a quiet story with loud implications. Nikkei reports that Japan's House of Councillors received just 42 written questions during the July extraordinary session. Written questions — shitsumon shui — are a formal tool the opposition uses to demand answers from the government in writing, on the record, with a seven-day deadline to reply. Just a year ago, a similar session drew 352 of them. That's a ninety percent collapse.

Why? Because the opposition was gutted in February's election. With little floor time to question ministers directly, written questions were their main remaining lever — and now even that has gone quiet. Some bureaucrats see relief in the lighter workload. But constitutional scholars see something worrying. These questions create permanent policy records; they force the government to state unified positions. Under Prime Minister Takaichi's supermajority, that scrutiny is simply thinning out. The paper doesn't mince words — it raises the question of accountability in what's become, functionally, a one-party state. A story about paperwork, really. But democracy often lives or dies in the paperwork.
