Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Tuesday, the eighteenth of August, 2026, and I'm glad you're here.

We begin this morning with a story that would have sounded almost unthinkable a few years ago: one of Japan's biggest internet companies is getting into the business of attack drones.

Rakuten — the company most people know for online shopping, mobile phones, and credit cards — has partnered with a German defense startup called Helsing to bring AI-equipped autonomous attack drones to Japan's Ground Self-Defense Force. And the goal isn't just to import them. It's to eventually build them here in Japan.

Now, let me give you a sense of who Helsing is, because this name may be new to you. Nikkei reports that the company is valued at roughly eighteen billion dollars, which makes it one of Europe's largest defense "unicorns" — that's the term for a privately held startup worth more than a billion dollars. Helsing already supplies drones to the German military and to Ukraine, where these systems have been tested under real wartime conditions. So this is not a company working from theory. It's working from the battlefield.

Why does this matter for Japan? A few reasons. First, it fits into a much larger strategic shift. The Takaichi government is racing to modernize Japan's defense posture, and drones are at the center of that thinking. Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi is scheduled to hold talks with German Chancellor Merz in October, and this Rakuten-Helsing tie-up is very much part of a deepening Japan-Germany security relationship.

And second — Japan wants to stop depending on foreign suppliers for critical weapons. Building drones domestically is about self-reliance as much as it's about firepower.

This connects directly to another story inside the paper. Nikkei reports that Japan's Defense Ministry is accelerating a revision of its three core security documents by the end of this year — the foundational papers that guide the country's entire defense strategy. The reason is simple: the world is changing faster than the paperwork. Officials are looking at two things in particular. One is China's military expansion — Nikkei notes that China's combat aircraft have doubled to more than sixteen hundred over the past decade. The other is the war in Ukraine, which has taught planners some hard lessons about long, grinding conflicts and about the growing role of AI on the battlefield.

So keep that framing in mind. When Rakuten partners with a drone maker, it's not a one-off business deal. It's a piece of a national rethink.

There's a companion piece here too, on the civilian side. The government is doubling the staff of its AI Safety Institute to sixty people by year-end, aiming for two hundred over the long term, and developing industry-specific rules for AI. As autonomous AI agents spread — systems that can act on their own — Japan is trying to build the guardrails. The United States and the UK are the benchmark here, and Tokyo is playing catch-up.

Let me turn now to the economy, and the news here is genuinely striking.

Japanese listed companies are on track for their sixth consecutive record year of profits. Nikkei reports that net profit for the fiscal year ending March 2027 is projected to grow fourteen percent. And here's the detail that jumped out at me — about twenty percent of companies have raised their profit forecasts upward, when the normal rate is around ten percent. So this isn't cautious optimism. Companies are surprising themselves.

What's driving it? AI investment demand, semiconductor tailwinds, and strong pricing power among the market leaders. There are clouds on the horizon — a possible correction in the yen, and rising interest rates — but for now, the earnings picture is remarkable.

And that optimism shows up in the markets. Nikkei reports that ten major securities firms now expect the Nikkei average to reach seventy-five thousand by year-end. Citi is at ninety thousand; Okasan, more conservatively, at around sixty-four thousand. For context, the index hit a record of about seventy-two thousand back in June. I'll add my usual reminder here — this is news, not advice, and forecasts are just forecasts.

Foreign money is following the story too. Global equity funds added a record two hundred and eighty-nine billion dollars — about four and a half trillion yen — to Japanese semiconductor and AI plays in the first half of the year. One name stands out: Kioxia, the memory-chip maker, whose presence in foreign portfolios surged twenty-eight-fold. Also drawing money — JX Metals, which makes materials for semiconductors, and, delightfully, Sanrio, the company behind Hello Kitty. Character licensing, it turns out, is a very durable business.

But here's the counterweight to all this good economic news. Nikkei reports that long-term interest rates have hit a thirty-year high. The ten-year Japanese government bond yield climbed to 2.93 percent, and market participants now view three percent as not just possible but imminent. This comes as the Bank of Japan is expected to accelerate rate hikes, following a coordinated yen-buying intervention with the United States. Japan has lived with near-zero rates for so long that a genuine three-percent yield changes the calculation for the whole economy.

Now, out into the world.

The Middle East is casting a long shadow over the rest of 2026. Nikkei frames this around two elections — the U.S. midterms in November, and Israeli national elections. A Republican defeat, the paper suggests, could bring unpredictable shifts in Trump's policy. An Israeli election could reignite military escalation. And running through all of it is the fear of energy insecurity — specifically, the de facto closure of the Strait of Hormuz, the narrow waterway through which so much of the world's oil passes. For Japan and Europe, the worry is a permanent risk premium built into every barrel of oil.

You can already see the human cost of that turmoil in another story. Air India is facing an annual loss of roughly three hundred and eighty billion yen — double the previous year. Part of that stems from a terrible crash in June of last year that killed two hundred and sixty people. But part of it is geopolitics: Pakistan closed its airspace, forcing longer routes, and Middle East turmoil sent fuel costs soaring. The airline is now changing leadership to try to rebuild confidence.

On the Korean peninsula, President Trump has instructed Defense Secretary Hegseth to sharply scale back joint U.S.-South Korea military exercises. The signal is that Trump is open to talks with Kim Jong Un — but for Japan, a quieter alliance and a friendlier tone toward Pyongyang raises real questions about regional stability.

And a quick word on China's carmakers, because the numbers tell a strange story. Exports of Chinese vehicles surged eighty-one percent, with EV exports hitting record highs. But at home, sales fell twenty-four percent — the ninth straight monthly decline. A weak property market and uncertainty about the government's economic direction have Chinese consumers holding back, even as Chinese cars flood the world.

Closer to home for many of us, Europe's wildfires have already cost more than thirty billion euros this year — double the annual average — burning through half a million hectares. The effects reach into everyday life in unexpected ways. Nikkei mentions "smoke taint" damaging grapes in Bordeaux, and low water on the Rhine disrupting cargo. Climate, quietly, is becoming an economic line item.

And now, the one that caught my eye — and it's a very Japanese story, in the best sense.

Nikkei reports that the Transport Ministry is reviewing whether Japan Post can keep delivering mail the way it always has. The reason is simple and a little poignant: mail volume has halved since 2001. Half. As the population ages and shrinks, and as everything moves online, the letters have simply stopped coming. So the government is now openly considering reduced delivery frequency and slower transit times.

I find this quietly moving. The Japanese postal service has been a symbol of reliability for generations — the idea that a letter arrives, on time, everywhere, to everyone. Watching a country decide that this promise may no longer be sustainable is watching demographic change made concrete. Not in a statistic, but in your mailbox.
