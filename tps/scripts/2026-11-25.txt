Our lead this morning is one of those quiet policy stories that could reshape how big business in Japan gets money. The Financial Services Agency — Japan's top financial regulator — says it will create an entirely new kind of lending license, one aimed specifically at loans to large corporations. And here's the key detail. Right now, if you want to run a lending business in Japan, you need at least fifty million yen in net assets. Under this new system, that requirement disappears — provided you lend only to big companies, not ordinary consumers.

Why does this matter? Because it's designed to pull funds and non-bank lenders into the market for financing mergers, acquisitions, and growth investment. For years, Japanese banks have shouldered most of this lending, and Nikkei reports the sense now is that traditional bank lending is approaching its limits. The government wants to diversify who provides that capital. This would be the first new lending category created since the ground-up overhaul of the rules back in 2006 — two decades ago — and the FSA aims to submit the legislation to the ordinary session of parliament next year.

It fits neatly with a broader theme in today's paper, which is how Japan funds its future. Prime Minister Takaichi has signaled the basic direction of the 2027 budget, and she's calling it — her phrase — "the first year of responsible, active fiscal policy." The idea is to concentrate resources on growth investment, and crucially, to move toward multi-year budget management. Japan has long operated on strict single-year budgeting, where money has to be spent within the fiscal year. Takaichi wants to break with that, partly to reassure financial markets that the spending is disciplined even as it expands.

Now, to the story that's gripping the business world. Jensen Huang — the chief executive of NVIDIA, the company at the very center of the AI chip boom — is back. He resigned just eleven days ago, on the twelfth, over an affair with a woman who's under suspicion of being a Chinese spy. And now he has returned. According to the Nikkei, President Trump personally lobbied NVIDIA's board, and the judgment came down that there was "no national security risk."

But — and this is important — the suspicions have not been cleared up. The US Congress plans to hold hearings in December, and there's an ongoing investigation into whether AI chips were being rerouted to China through back channels. So Huang is back in the chair while the questions are still open, and that leaves a real cloud over the independence of NVIDIA's corporate governance. When a sitting president intervenes directly to reinstate a CEO under a security cloud, you have to ask who's really making the call. We'll be watching those December hearings closely.

Staying with the collision of trade and geopolitics, there's troubling news for Japan's automakers. The European Commission is working on a revised version of its Industrial Accelerator Act, and Nikkei has seen the draft. The bad news for Tokyo: it still excludes Japanese-made electric vehicles from the EU's EV subsidy program. For Toyota and the others, this is deeply unfavorable, and both the Japanese government and industry are pushing hard to change it. The obstacle is an agreement between Germany and France, and negotiations are expected to run until the end of 2027.

And it gets worse, because the EU has also triggered an emergency import restriction on hybrid cars. This is a safeguard measure aimed at Chinese-made EVs flooding the market — but Japanese cars are caught in the crossfire. From the first of December through June of 2027, there'll be a common import quota as a temporary measure. So Japan finds itself swept up in Europe's trade defense against China, even though that's not the target.

Speaking of China, the story is everywhere today. Here at home, the Japan Coast Guard is bolstering its fleet with more patrol vessels and drones, responding to a sharp rise in activity by China's coast guard ships, particularly around the Senkaku Islands. And the government's revision of its three key national security documents now puts economic security front and center — checking the vulnerability of energy and infrastructure, establishing what they're calling "AI sovereignty," and strengthening supply chains.

There's a related twist on defense. The Defense Ministry is actually considering cutting the authorized number of Self-Defense Force personnel. That sounds counterintuitive at a moment of rising tension, but it reflects a hard reality — Japan's shrinking population and genuine trouble recruiting. The plan is to bring the official numbers in line with the actual headcount, and make up the difference with AI-based command and control systems, while prioritizing frontline deployment.

Let's step over to the international pages. In the United States, the Democrats have won big in the midterm elections — their largest victory since 1992, Nikkei reports, securing 258 seats in the House. That gives them majorities in both chambers of the new Congress come January, and they're signaling they'll go on the offensive with "corruption" investigations targeting the Trump administration. Which, when you set it next to the Huang story we just discussed, suggests Washington is heading into a very combative new year.

In France, President Macron is weighing whether to write fiscal discipline directly into the constitution — a so-called "golden rule." With the presidential election coming next spring, the strategy is partly to absorb support from the far right, and partly to keep a lid on rising long-term interest rates. And that race is getting crowded: three former prime ministers have now declared. Dominique de Villepin is running on what he calls "left-wing Gaullism." With Marine Le Pen on the far right and Jean-Luc Mélenchon on the radical left both holding strong positions, the worry is a serious splintering of the center-right vote.

A couple more quickly. Laos has graduated from the United Nations list of "least developed countries" — a real milestone. It loses some preferential tariff treatment, but gains international standing. The challenge now is weaning itself off heavy economic dependence on China. And in a striking military development, Ukraine has sunk a Russian warship off eastern Libya — its first attack in the Mediterranean — part of a strategy to check Russia's expansion into Africa.

On the corporate side, a name you'll recognize is making a comeback. Canon is returning to a part of the chipmaking equipment business it left twenty-four years ago — building advanced lithography machines to challenge the near-total dominance of the Dutch company ASML. With ASML's equipment in tight supply, Canon sees an opening, and it starts taking orders early next month.

And now, the one that caught my eye. Tucked into the international pages is a small item about markets across Asia, and it tells a bigger story about nerves over AI. Taiwan — worried that investment in AI has become overheated — is tightening its rules on short selling after its main stock index dropped sharply. South Korea is going the other way, easing rules on share buybacks to support its market. And China is lowering margin requirements on credit trading. Three different governments, three different levers, all pulled in the same week — each trying to steady markets rattled by the same question: has the AI boom run ahead of itself? It's a quiet reminder that for all the excitement, the people watching most closely are getting cautious.
