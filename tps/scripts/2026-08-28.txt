We begin this morning with a story that sits right at the intersection of science and national security — and it's the kind of quiet trend that says a great deal about how the world has changed over the past ten years.

Nikkei reports that Japan's top hundred research institutions have doubled their collaboration with foreign organizations that the government has flagged as security risks. Ten years ago, Japanese researchers co-authored about 3,600 papers with these flagged entities. Last year, that number reached roughly 7,200. It doubled.

Now, how did the Nikkei work this out? They cross-referenced two lists — one kept by Japan's Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, called the Foreign User Risk List, and the other the American Entity List, which flags organizations Washington considers a security concern. And here's the headline within the headline: ninety percent of these co-authored papers involve Chinese institutions. Some of them belong to what's known as China's "National Defense Seven Universities" — schools with direct ties to military research.

Let me give you the honest context here, because it isn't as simple as "Japan letting its guard down." Part of this surge really just reflects China's rise as a scientific power. Chinese universities produce enormous volumes of high-quality research now, and any serious scientist wants to work with the best minds available. That's how science works.

But the worry is about what's called "dual-use" research — work that has both civilian and military applications. Think advanced materials, the kind that can go into a smartphone or a missile depending on who's building it. And when a Japanese lab co-publishes with a Chinese defense university in exactly those fields, the concern is technology quietly leaking across a border. That's why, back in March of last year, the government's science and technology plan explicitly ordered universities to strengthen what it calls "research security." This story tells us that job is far from finished.

Let's turn to some big corporate news, and there are two stories today that both, in different ways, are about Japan tidying up its own house.

The first: Itochu, the trading giant, is buying 38.2 percent of Dentsu Total Solutions for around 250 billion yen — that's the remaining publicly traded shares — and taking the company private, partnering with the Dentsu Group to do it. Now if you're wondering why that matters, it's about something Japanese corporate governance experts have long grumbled about — the so-called parent-subsidiary listing problem. When a parent company and its subsidiary are both listed on the stock market, the interests of ordinary shareholders can clash with the interests of the parent. Taking the subsidiary private cleans that up. For Itochu, there's a business logic too — it wants to strengthen its digital operations by folding in this systems-integration firm.

And the second bit of housekeeping is a happier one, at least if you like roller coasters. Universal Studios Japan, in Osaka, is planning its first major expansion since it opened back in 2001. And it is enormous — an investment of two to three trillion yen, into some sixty thousand square meters of adjacent land. That land, by the way, is currently owned by Osaka City and was once occupied by a Nippon Steel facility. USJ is already Japan's leading theme park, pulling in sixteen million visitors a year, and this expansion — starting around 2028 — is a bet on inbound tourism staying strong. The ambition is to sit alongside the world's top-tier destinations.

Now, I want to slow down for a moment, because this next story is one of consequence for a lot of families.

It's been one month since the Kumamoto earthquake — a magnitude seven, maximum-intensity quake that struck on the 28th of July. And a month on, the human toll is still rippling through the regional economy. Visitor numbers to Kumamoto prefecture were down 62 percent in early August, and even during Obon — the mid-summer holiday when families travel home to honor their ancestors, one of the busiest times of the year — visitor numbers stayed 15 percent below last year. Neighboring Kagoshima was hit too, down 52 percent, largely because shinkansen bullet train service was suspended.

The government has announced relief measures totaling 147.8 billion yen, and it's planning what it calls a "Kyushu Support Discount" — covering sixty percent of travel costs to lure tourists back.

But here's the encouraging thread, and Nikkei draws it out nicely. Manufacturing is recovering far faster than it did after the 2016 Kumamoto quake. And there's a striking example in the business pages: Sony's image-sensor factory. Kumamoto is a hub for these sensors — the tiny chips inside your phone camera. After the 2016 quake, it took Sony three and a half months to get back to full production. This time? They were back to pre-quake output by the 19th of August. Three and a half months, down to three weeks. That's the payoff from years of investment in earthquake-resistant factories, and it's a quiet lesson in resilience.

On to the economy, briefly. Bank of Japan Deputy Governor Ryozo Himino has signaled that a September interest rate hike is very much on the table. He stopped short of a firm commitment, but he said the bank will consider raising rates at every meeting — and the market is now pricing an 83 percent chance of a September move. After years of Japan's famously ultra-low rates, this continues a genuine shift.

And in politics — a merger that isn't happening. The Constitutional Democratic Party has postponed its planned three-way merger with two smaller parties. The reason is grassroots resistance: about eighty percent of the party's Tokyo members opposed dissolving. Talks will continue, but consolidation isn't coming soon.

With the Strait of Hormuz disrupted, crude-derived plastics — the kind used in packaging and countless products — have jumped about 40 percent since February. One expert put it bluntly: "low-cost procurement models are ending."

A brighter note from South Korea, which raised its 2026 growth forecast to 3.3 percent, up sharply, riding an AI-driven semiconductor boom. And speaking of chips — here in Japan, Kioxia and its partner Sandisk are investing five trillion yen over six years in NAND memory, including a brand-new plant in Iwate prefecture, starting production in 2029. The "memory war" with Samsung and SK Hynix is well and truly on.

And finally, the story that caught my eye — though this one caught my heart, too.

Yayoi Kusama has died, at the age of 98. The great avant-garde artist passed away on the 14th of August from multiple organ failure. You may not know her name, but I promise you know her work — those hypnotic polka dots, the yellow-and-black pumpkins, the mirrored infinity rooms that people line up for hours to step inside. She pioneered net painting and soft sculpture. She represented Japan at the Venice Biennale in 1993, had a retrospective at MoMA in 1998, and her installations on the island of Naoshima became a pilgrimage site for art lovers worldwide.

What moves me about Kusama is that she turned a lifetime of obsession and psychological struggle into something universal — dots repeating into infinity, dissolving the boundary between the self and the world. She was ninety-eight, still working, still dotting. A singular life. May she rest.
