Let's begin at the Bank of Japan, because next week is a big one. The central bank meets on the thirtieth and thirty-first, and sources tell Nikkei it will hold its policy rate steady at one percent. Now, that might sound like nothing happening — but standing still is itself a decision. The Bank raised rates back in June, and now it wants to watch. It wants to see how that hike ripples through the economy before it moves again.

And there's a lot to watch. Inflation is still running above one percent. Oil prices are climbing on tension in the Middle East — more on that in a moment. But here's the interesting wrinkle: the Bank is actually expected to raise its growth forecast for this fiscal year, up from the April estimate of half a percent. The reason? Artificial intelligence. AI-related industries are booming, and that's lifting the whole outlook. So the message from the Bank is careful but not timid: rates are on hold for now, but further hikes remain very much on the table. The only question is timing.

Speaking of timing and the yen — the currency has slid to about a hundred and sixty-four to the dollar. That's a thirty-nine-year low. Let me put that in perspective: you'd have to go back to 1987 to find the yen this weak against the dollar. It's cheap for tourists, painful for anyone importing food or fuel, and it's part of why inflation keeps nagging at Japanese households.

Now let me connect two stories that speak to the same anxiety — how ordinary Japanese people should handle their money when prices are rising nearly three percent a year and cash is quietly losing value.

The first is a real shift in policy. Japan's Labor Ministry plans to let financial institutions recommend specific investment products to people who hold something called iDeCo — that's the individual defined-contribution pension, a tax-advantaged retirement account. Until now, that kind of advice was almost forbidden. Starting in fiscal 2027, that changes. And the reason is telling: sixteen percent of iDeCo holders — and twenty-one percent of the corporate version — keep their money entirely in safe, low-yield, principal-protection products. In an era of near-three-percent inflation, that "safe" money is actually shrinking in real terms. The government wants to nudge these savers toward assets that can keep up with rising prices.

It's part of a bigger push. Nikkei reports that capital gains tax discussions are also gaining momentum, as the government wrestles with wealth inequality. And there's a subsidiary bill that would let the Government Pension Investment Fund — the enormous public pension fund known as GPIF — buy more domestic government bonds. That one raises eyebrows among international observers, who worry about using pension savings to prop up government debt. It's a fine line between prudent management and politicizing people's retirement money.

Now, to trade — and to Washington. The Trump administration has imposed a new tariff on Japan: twelve and a half percent, under what's called Section 301. Eighteen other countries got hit with ten percent. Japan's higher rate is officially about insufficient anti-forced-labor measures, though there's some relief built in — a cap keeping the combined burden at no more than that twelve and a half percent, which softens the blow for exporters. The backdrop here is a February court ruling against earlier tariffs, and, frankly, the November midterms. President Trump continues to use trade as a tool to rally domestic manufacturing.

Let me turn to a discovery that could genuinely reshape Japan's future — and it's sitting on the seabed. The government has confirmed that drilling off Minami-Tori Island, far out in the Pacific, has yielded high concentrations of rare earth elements. And not just any rare earths — the medium and heavy kind, which make up fifty-four percent of what they found, including yttrium at nearly thirty percent. Why does this matter? Because China controls roughly seventy percent of global rare earth production, and those elements are essential for everything from electric motors to defense systems. Japan plans large-scale demonstration tests starting in February 2027, with commercialization targeted for fiscal 2028. If it works, it's a real step toward energy and materials independence.

On the international front, the Middle East is where several threads come together — and where that oil worry I mentioned takes shape. Iran's Revolutionary Guards have attacked data centers in the UAE and Bahrain, most recently on the eighteenth of July. Think about that for a moment. The Gulf states are pouring money into AI as their strategy for life after oil — and now the very data centers powering that ambition have become military targets. Foreign tech firms are pausing their ventures there, which threatens the whole vision of the Gulf as an AI hub.

Meanwhile, the energy picture is tightening. The Houthis are blockading the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, and with the Strait of Hormuz also in question, Nikkei describes a "double-blockade" scenario. WTI crude is approaching ninety dollars a barrel, and further escalation could push it past a hundred and ten. Global crude inventories have fallen by five hundred million barrels since February. If this drags into the Northern Hemisphere winter, we could be looking at genuine shortages.

And there's diplomacy stalling, too. President Trump has now conditioned a U.S.–Saudi civilian nuclear deal on Saudi Arabia joining the Abraham Accords — that is, normalizing relations with Israel. Riyadh sees that as nearly impossible given public anger over Gaza. So a deal that once looked promising is suddenly in doubt.
