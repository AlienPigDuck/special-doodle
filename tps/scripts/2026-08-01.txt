Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Saturday, the first of August, 2026, and there's a lot moving today — currencies, chips, and a couple of very human stories from opposite ends of the map. Let's get into it.

We start with the yen — and with a surprise.

On Wednesday, July 30th, Japan's government and the Bank of Japan stepped into the currency markets together and spent somewhere between six and seven trillion yen defending their currency. Now, what made this unusual wasn't just the size — it was the timing. According to the Nikkei this morning, they acted *before* announcing their policy decisions, which caught traders completely off guard. And here's why that matters: currency intervention works partly on surprise. When you move when nobody expects it, you inflict pain on the traders betting against you.

For a moment, it worked. The yen jumped about five cents against the dollar. But then — and this is the sobering part — the market shrugged, and the yen drifted right back to around 160 to the dollar. To put that in plain terms, the yen is very weak. A weak yen makes everything Japan imports — oil, food, raw materials — more expensive, and that feeds inflation.

What's genuinely new here is that this was a *joint* effort with the United States. The US Treasury signaled it's ready to act again. That's a striking bit of coordination, and it tells you both governments now see excessive yen weakness as a shared problem, not just Tokyo's headache.

Meanwhile, the Bank of Japan's governor, Ueda Kazuo, held the policy rate steady at one percent — but he left the door open. He flagged three specific risks that could push inflation higher and force his hand: rising oil prices, surging demand for AI-related semiconductors, and — there it is again — the weak yen. The market read this clearly. The Nikkei reports the odds of a rate hike by October have now climbed above eighty percent.

So that's the backdrop: a currency under pressure, a central bank getting ready to move, and two governments working in concert. Keep it in mind, because it colors nearly everything else in the paper today.

And what it colors most is the great semiconductor boom.

Let's start with the eye-popping number. Kiokushia Holdings — the Japanese memory chip maker — reported quarterly pure profit of 1.27 trillion yen. That's up thirty-one-fold from a year ago. Thirty-one times. The reason is simple: AI data centers need enormous amounts of memory, and that demand has pushed memory prices up fifty to sixty percent. Kiokushia is celebrating with a three-for-one stock split and up to 800 billion yen in share buybacks.

And this isn't just one company. The Nikkei reports that the five biggest memory makers in the world — Samsung, SK Hynix, Micron, Kiokushia, and SanDisk — together pulled in 14.8 trillion yen in free cash flow last quarter. That's ninety-two times more than a year earlier. Samsung and SK Hynix are planning ninety trillion yen in new Korean factories to keep up.

You can see the ripples everywhere. Taiwan's economy grew nearly thirteen percent in the second quarter — an extraordinary figure for a developed economy — driven almost entirely by AI chip exports to the United States. And in Tokyo, the Nikkei 225 jumped four percent yesterday — nearly 2,500 points — led by chip suppliers. Murata rose sixteen percent. Ibiden climbed twenty-two. Over in Korea, the KOSPI surged a historic eighteen percent in a single day.

But here's where I want you to be careful, because the paper is careful. That giddy rally has a shadow, and I'll come back to it at the end.

For now, there's a question hanging over all of this: who's paying for the boom? The answer is the American tech giants. Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft, and Meta together burned through — brace yourself — negative free cash flow of 946 billion dollars last quarter. They spent 2.2 trillion dollars on capital, double the year before, almost all of it on data centers. That's a staggering bet. And the Nikkei notes it's raising real questions about whether this pace of spending is sustainable.

Away from chips, a few more corporate stories worth your time.

Itochu — one of Japan's big trading houses — is investing about 300 billion yen for a fifty percent stake in a US aircraft leasing company, Aviation Capital Group. Combined with what it already owns, that makes Itochu the sixth-largest aircraft lessor in the world, managing around 550 planes. Why now? Because Boeing and Airbus are years behind on production — delays the Nikkei says won't resolve until the early 2030s. When you can't buy new planes, leasing them becomes very valuable.

Sony, meanwhile, raised its profit forecast seventeen percent, to 1.21 trillion yen, thanks to strong PlayStation 5 sales and — yes — that weak yen again, which flatters the earnings of Japanese exporters.

Now to politics, where Prime Minister Takaichi is running into a wall inside her own party.

Takaichi wants to cut the consumption tax on food from eight percent down to one percent for two years — a direct response to households feeling squeezed by rising prices. But the Nikkei reports that at recent party meetings, roughly eighty percent of Liberal Democratic Party members expressed opposition or caution. Their worry is twofold: where does the money come from, and — just as tricky — how do you ever raise the tax *back* to eight percent once people have gotten used to one? That's a real political trap. Tax cuts are easy to give and painful to take away.

On energy, the government has formally approved rebuilding up to five nuclear reactors by the 2040s, and nine more by the 2050s. But there's a catch the paper highlights: nearly seventy percent of surveyed firms in the sector say they simply can't find the workers. You can approve reactors on paper; building them requires skilled hands that Japan doesn't currently have enough of.

And a quieter policy note — Japan is tightening its rules for permanent residency, adding a Japanese language requirement and a civics test.

Turning abroad. President Trump has announced what he's calling a historic agreement with Hamas on disarmament, with a fifteen-point roadmap. But — and this is important — both Hamas and Israel have signaled reservations, and the Nikkei is clear-eyed that implementation remains highly uncertain. Worth watching, not celebrating yet.

In Europe, a heartbreaking and volatile situation on Spain's north African enclave of Ceuta — some sixty thousand migrants crossed from Morocco in just days, and thirty-four drowned. Italy's Prime Minister Meloni condemned the handling and floated the idea of excluding Spain from Europe's open-border zone. And one consumer note from Brussels: the EU's new "right to repair" rules are now in force, requiring makers of phones and appliances to offer spare parts at reasonable prices. Apple, Google, and Nintendo are already redesigning products so you can fix them yourself.

Before I go, I have to return to Kumamoto, in southern Japan, where a magnitude-7 earthquake has now claimed thirty-six lives. Ninety-five hundred people are evacuated, more than fifteen hundred buildings damaged. The Kyushu bullet train has partially resumed, but some sections will stay closed through August. Our thoughts are with everyone there.

And now, the one that caught my eye — and it's buried deep in today's markets pages, which is exactly why I love it.

Everyone's talking about yesterday's spectacular rally — nearly 2,500 points. But the Nikkei points out something the celebration obscures: over the whole month of July, the index *lost* 5,700 points. That's the steepest monthly drop since volatility returned. So the big one-day surge? It didn't erase July. It barely dented it.

And here's the lesson underneath. All that AI optimism — Kiokushia's thirty-one-fold profit, Taiwan's booming exports — is running headlong into a harder reality: higher interest rates, stretched valuations, and nervousness about whether the tech giants are spending too much. That single steadying sentence, tucked away from the headlines, tells you the mood beneath the euphoria is fragile. Sometimes the truest story is the quiet one.
