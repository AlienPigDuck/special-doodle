We begin this morning with money — a lot of it — flowing across the Pacific. Nikkei reports that Citigroup and JPMorgan Chase, two of America's biggest banks, will help finance Japan's planned five hundred and fifty billion dollar investment in the United States. In yen, that's roughly ninety trillion. This is the deal Tokyo and Washington struck last year, and it's now moving into what the paper calls Phase Two — bigger and more ambitious than the first round. We're talking about next-generation nuclear power plants and gas-fired power stations, with a total project cost of up to twelve trillion yen. That's double the size of Phase One.

Now, here's the interesting wrinkle, and it tells you something about how this deal actually works. The financing has shifted away from Japan's own megabanks toward American lenders. Why? Because the fundamental challenge here isn't Japanese money — it's dollars. To build power plants in the United States, you need to pay for them in U.S. currency. And Nikkei's preliminary math suggests Japan's private sector alone will need to secure more than forty trillion yen worth of dollars. That is an enormous amount of foreign currency to source. So the government is now coordinating closely with the Finance Ministry and the Bank of Japan on a medium-to-long-term strategy for procuring all those dollars. And two state-backed institutions — the Japan Bank for International Cooperation, and Japan's trade insurance body, NEXI — will step in to manage the risk through guarantees and expanded lending.

Think about what this represents. Japan is essentially building American energy infrastructure as part of a diplomatic bargain, and the sheer scale of the currency involved has become a policy problem in its own right. It's a reminder that these grand political agreements eventually land on someone's desk as a very concrete question: where do we find the dollars?

And staying with that theme of enormous cross-border sums — the numbers this morning are frankly staggering. Nikkei reports from an AI summit in San Francisco that South Korea and American tech giants announced a combined seven hundred billion dollars in commitments. Samsung and SK Group are the driving forces. Samsung signed a two hundred billion dollar contract with Broadcom running through 2030 — that's for high-bandwidth memory chips and chip manufacturing. And SK announced a joint venture with Nvidia worth over five hundred billion dollars, including data center construction. The story here is that South Korea is positioning itself as indispensable to the global AI supply chain, thanks to its dominance in memory chips. And you'll notice — this is Korea, not Japan, at the center of it. Worth keeping in mind as Japan works out its own place in the AI economy.

Speaking of chips, there's a remarkable financial story on the front page. The American private equity firm Bain Capital has realized about two and a half trillion yen in profit from selling shares in Kioxia — the memory chip maker. Nikkei calls it the largest private equity return ever on a Japanese investment. You may remember the backstory: this company used to be Toshiba Memory. Bain led a group that bought it in 2018, when Toshiba was in deep crisis and breaking itself apart to survive. Bain held around fifty-five percent of the company, and after Kioxia went public in December 2024, those shares became a fortune. Toshiba itself booked two point two trillion yen in gains. And there's a fascinating detail buried here — South Korea's SK Hynix, a direct rival, holds convertible bonds that could turn it into a Kioxia shareholder once regulators approve. So a Korean competitor may end up with a stake in a Japanese chipmaker. The industry is more tangled than it looks.

Let me turn now to matters closer to home, and to Prime Minister Takaichi. She's unveiled what her government calls a "Human Capital Reform Plan" — essentially a rethink of how Japan's national civil servants work. The headline change: by 2030, more government workers will be eligible for shorter working hours. Until now, reduced hours were mainly for people caring for family. The new rules would extend that to health concerns, continuing education, even volunteer work. There's also a push to reward performance rather than pure seniority — bonuses for senior officials, section chief and above, will move to what's called absolute performance evaluation starting this year. In plain terms: your bonus reflects what you actually contributed, not just how long you've been there. For Japan's civil service, long defined by seniority, that's a meaningful cultural shift.

But there's a slightly awkward note for the Prime Minister in the politics pages. Nikkei reports that Takaichi's attendance at Diet Budget Committee sessions during the special parliamentary session came to just thirty-three hours. The five-year average is sixty-eight — so roughly half. Her office says she spent the extra time on policy planning at the official residence. Still, it's raised questions about parliamentary accountability, and it's the kind of number the opposition will remember.

One more domestic story that may interest you if you follow how Japan governs itself: the Furusato Nozei program — that's the "hometown tax" system, where you can donate part of your taxes to a rural municipality and often receive local produce in return. It's popular. But a survey found that seventy-six percent of municipalities think the middleman websites — platforms like Rakuten and Furunabi — charge too much. Their commission runs at eleven and a half percent, and in the last fiscal year, towns paid nearly a hundred and thirty-eight billion yen in fees. The complaint is simple: money meant for struggling rural communities is being siphoned off to Tokyo-based websites. Local governments want the national government to cap those rates.

A few quick items from abroad. China's market regulator has fined Ctrip — the online travel giant, known internationally as Trip.com — the equivalent of about one point two-five trillion yen for antitrust violations. Ctrip controls more than half of China's online hotel bookings, and regulators say it forced hotels to limit dealings with rivals. And Japan's Foreign Minister, Shigeru Motegi, heads to Latin America in early August — Mexico, Ecuador, Colombia, and Panama — to build new economic and trade frameworks, with economic security clearly on the agenda.

And now, the one that caught my eye — and it's a story about the future of the human body, tucked into the markets pages. The global market for obesity drugs is expected to reach two hundred billion dollars by 2030. That's not a typo. The race is on among pharmaceutical giants — Eli Lilly, Novo Nordisk, Pfizer, and Boehringer Ingelheim. And here's the figure that stopped me: Lilly's experimental drug, retatrutide, has shown twenty-eight to thirty percent weight reduction over eighteen to twenty-four months. Nikkei notes that matches the results of weight-loss surgery. Think about that. A weekly injection producing the kind of results that once required an operation. Meanwhile a Chinese startup — MoonShot AI — is among those chasing aggressive pricing. We are watching a genuinely new category of medicine take shape, one that could reshape not just healthcare but food, insurance, even how we think about willpower. Whatever you make of it, two hundred billion dollars tells you the world is betting heavily on it.
