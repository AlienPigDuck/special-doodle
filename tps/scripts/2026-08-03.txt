Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Monday, the third of August, 2026, and there's a lot moving this morning — a rare piece of currency drama between Tokyo and Washington, a record-breaking spending spree on artificial intelligence, and, near the end, a quieter story about rice that I think tells us something big about Japan's future. Let's get into it.

We begin with the yen. On the thirty-first of July, Japan and the United States did something they hadn't done together in fifteen years. They intervened in the currency markets side by side — both governments buying yen at the same moment to prop it up. Nikkei reports this is the first joint intervention since 2011, back when Japan was reeling from the earthquake and tsunami. So this is genuinely unusual.

Here's what prompted it. The yen had slid to a hundred and sixty-four to the dollar. That's a forty-year low. For Japan, a weak yen is a double-edged sword. It makes exports cheaper and helps big manufacturers, but it also makes everything Japan imports — energy, food, raw materials — more expensive, and that squeezes households.

But what makes this story so striking is why Washington got involved. US Treasury Secretary Bessent has been signaling that a weak yen undermines America's high-tariff trade policy. Think about it — if the yen is cheap enough, Japanese goods stay affordable in the US even with tariffs slapped on top. That blunts the whole point of the tariffs. And with American midterm elections coming in November, the politics of that matter a great deal. So this isn't just about stabilizing a currency. It's about America wanting Japan's exchange rate to line up with American trade goals.

The pressure now falls on the Bank of Japan, which is being pushed toward a rate hike in September. Higher interest rates would naturally strengthen the yen. So watch that meeting closely.

Now, from currency to something more forward-looking — and here the theme of the day really comes into focus, because it's all about artificial intelligence.

Nikkei's front page reports that Japan's private sector plans to spend a record thirty-five-point-six trillion yen on capital investment this fiscal year. That's up more than fourteen percent, an all-time high, and the first time in four years that growth has topped two percent. Companies are building. And what they're building is AI infrastructure — data centers, semiconductor plants, the power supply to run all of it. NTT alone plans to spend two-point-four trillion yen, much of it on data centers. Nikkei surveyed seven hundred and fifteen companies, and the appetite is spreading across chipmaking, electrical equipment, and utilities, driven partly by economic security concerns and by data center demand that's roughly doubling every year.

But here's the interesting wrinkle. As money floods into AI, it's flowing away from something else. Environmental investment fell more than five percent — the first drop in six years. Part of that is just timing; JR East, the railway company, had finished a big rail vehicle project, so its green spending dropped by nearly half. But it's a reminder that Japan's "GX," its green transformation push, is competing for the same corporate wallet as the AI boom — and right now, AI is winning.

There's a related story in the services sector, where IT spending jumped more than twenty-four percent to a nine-year high. The big spenders are JR East, ANA, and Japan Airlines, all pouring money into AI for customer service and operations. And Asahi Group — yes, the beer company — is committing nearly forty-two billion yen to rebuild its cybersecurity after a cyberattack last year. A costly lesson.

Now, before we get too swept up in the AI enthusiasm, Nikkei offers a sobering counterpoint from across the Pacific. A Nikkei survey found that forty-five percent of Americans oppose new data center construction, against just twenty-two percent who support it — and that opposition crosses party lines. The reason is bread-and-butter: in swing states like Pennsylvania, these power-hungry facilities have pushed electricity rates up more than ten percent. So the AI build-out has a political cost, and ordinary people are starting to feel it in their utility bills.

Let's turn to the wider world, where the biggest story is the Middle East. President Trump announced on the first of August that the United States will stop its military attacks on Iran — but only on conditions. Tehran must immediately open the Strait of Hormuz and end what Washington calls its nuclear threat. Trump stressed American military readiness and said Saudi Arabia's Crown Prince had asked him to show restraint.

Iran isn't buying it. Its defense ministry called the pause psychological warfare. The heart of the dispute is the Strait of Hormuz — that narrow chokepoint through which a huge share of the world's oil passes. Iran wants full control of the strait; the US insists on open commercial passage. For Japan, which imports nearly all its oil and much of it through those very waters, this is not a distant conflict. It's a lifeline.

Staying in the region briefly — a Houthi-affiliated maritime body in Yemen has denied reports that it plans to charge tolls for ships passing through the Bab al-Mandab strait, the other great gateway to that part of the world. It says passage remains free, except for Saudi-affiliated vessels.

Elsewhere in Asia, South Korea is undergoing a quiet revolution in its justice system. The Democratic Party has pushed through reforms that strip prosecutors of their investigative powers, starting in October. From now on, police will lead investigations, with new specialized agencies handling economic crimes and cases involving senior officials. Supporters say it curbs prosecutorial overreach. Critics warn it could weaken judicial independence, and prosecutors themselves argue the system will simply collapse without their expertise. Either way, it's a historic shift after decades of tension between Korea's prosecutors and its elected governments.

Closer to home, Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi has sent congratulatory letters to Peru's new president, Keiko Fujimori — herself of Japanese descent. Takaichi called Peru a strategic partner and a fellow democracy, and there's real history here: Peru is home to a Japanese community roughly three hundred thousand strong. A small diplomatic gesture, but one that reaches back generations.

A quick note from the business pages, because it speaks to a change we've been tracking. BYD, the Chinese automaker, is launching a "kei" car in Japan — that's the tiny, lightweight class of vehicle that dominates Japanese roads, about four million sold a year. BYD's electric version comes with umbrella storage, redesigned cup holders, and dual sliding doors, all shaped by careful local research. And it's priced to undercut Honda's N-Box. Speaking of Honda — the company is pulling back on its all-electric ambitions and boosting hybrid investment by sixty-six percent, to over a trillion yen. So even as a Chinese EV arrives, Japan's biggest players are hedging their bets on the electric future.

And now, the one that caught my eye — a deep, quiet story buried in today's paper about rice.

Japan's rice farming is in what Nikkei calls a historical crisis. Consider this number: in 1976, Japan had over four hundred million rice-farming households. Today, there are about five hundred thousand. The farmers who remain are aging, and government subsidies are running dry. The 2023 rice shortage — remembered as the "Reiwa rice riots" — exposed just how fragile the system has become. Prices swing wildly, and elderly farmers simply can't absorb the shocks. Yet policy stays frozen.

This isn't just about a beloved staple. It's about food security, about whether rural communities survive at all. When the price of rice collapses, so does something older and deeper. It deserves the urgent attention Nikkei is giving it.
