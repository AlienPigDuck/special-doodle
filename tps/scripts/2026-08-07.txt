Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Friday, the seventh of August, 2026, and there's a lot to get through today, from a massive Gulf investment landing in northern Japan to a quiet financial crisis at the country's most famous university. Let's begin.

Our lead story this morning takes us to Akita, on the northwestern coast of Japan's main island — a region better known for rice and snow than for high technology. That may be about to change. Nikkei reports that Akita is set to host what will become Japan's largest artificial intelligence data center, a facility carrying a price tag of around two trillion yen. That's roughly thirteen billion dollars. And here's the striking part: much of the money is coming from the United Arab Emirates, specifically from Mubadala, the Abu Dhabi sovereign wealth fund. The project brings together the Akita prefectural government, Tokyo, and Japanese companies, with the Gulf providing the financial muscle.

Why Akita? Two words: energy and water. AI data centers are enormously power-hungry, and they run hot — they need vast amounts of electricity and vast amounts of water for cooling. Akita has both. It's building offshore wind farms with more than two hundred megawatts of capacity, and it has water to spare. The new facility itself will draw five hundred megawatts. It's aiming to be up and running in the early 2030s.

Now, step back and think about what this means. Japan has worried for years that it was falling behind in the AI race — behind the United States, behind China. This is a bet that Japan can become a regional hub for AI computing, powered by clean energy, and financed in part by Gulf capital that is looking for a stable home outside the Middle East. It's a genuinely significant moment, and one worth watching as it unfolds.

From that story of ambition, let's turn to one of strain — and it's on the front page. The University of Tokyo, Japan's most prestigious research institution, is heading for its second straight year in the red. Nikkei reports the deficit will reach around forty billion yen this fiscal year. The university's accumulated savings, at this rate, will run dry within a few years.

What's driving it? Several things at once. Personnel costs have climbed ten billion yen over five years, pushed up by civil service pay recommendations. The price of laboratory equipment has soared with inflation. And there are new governance costs, following recent corruption scandals. Without new revenue or government help, the university may have to cut researcher positions — and that has real consequences. Tokyo University is where a huge share of Japan's scientific output originates. If it shrinks, so does Japan's standing in global research. This is a slow-moving crisis, but a serious one.

Staying with corporate Japan, Fujifilm is making a major move. The company will spin off its multifunction device business — the printers and copiers you'll remember as Fujifilm Xerox, now called Fujifilm Business Innovation — and eventually take it public. Fujifilm will cut its stake to around twenty percent over the next two or three years. Now, this business is not small: it brings in thirty-five percent of the company's revenue, some 1.17 trillion yen. But it's a mature market, growth is limited, and Fujifilm wants to pour capital into faster-growing areas — semiconductor materials, biopharmaceutical manufacturing, healthcare. It's a familiar story of a conglomerate trimming itself down to sharpen its focus.

And one more from the front page, on defense. Japan's Defense Ministry will start telling contractors, in advance, how much equipment it plans to buy — ammunition, missiles, drones, warships. Starting in fiscal 2027, suppliers will get those numbers early, under confidentiality agreements, so they can invest in production capacity with confidence. The goal is to expand Japan's domestic defense manufacturing and coax new companies into the field. It's a quiet but meaningful shift in how Japan arms itself.

Let's move to politics, where Prime Minister Takaichi is navigating some sensitive terrain. First, trade. Her government has proposed cutting the consumption tax on food to just one percent for two years — an anti-inflation measure. But Nikkei reports that U.S. Treasury Secretary Bessent has voiced skepticism, preferring other approaches. That's raised eyebrows in Takaichi's own ruling party, with some worried Washington may press Tokyo to change its fiscal plans.

Then there's Hiroshima. At the Peace Memorial ceremony this week, Takaichi stopped short of pledging permanent adherence to Japan's Three Non-Nuclear Principles — the long-standing commitment not to possess, produce, or allow nuclear weapons on Japanese soil. Her choice of words signals possible openness to revisiting that policy in the year-end security review. On the anniversary of the atomic bombing, in that city, those omissions carry enormous weight.

And the security backdrop got a reminder this week: North Korea fired at least one short-range ballistic missile, which landed east of the peninsula, outside Japan's exclusive economic zone. It's the first such launch in about four months.

Turning abroad, there's a sobering figure from the Nikkei this morning. Global investment in companies tied to nuclear weapons has surged thirty percent in a single year, reaching about a hundred billion dollars. For years, institutional investors were pulling money out of this sector on ethical grounds. That trend has now reversed — driven by Russia's war in Ukraine and Middle East tensions, which have reshaped how big investors think about defense. The momentum behind the nuclear weapons ban treaty is, for now, faltering.

Speaking of the Middle East, Iran and Oman have agreed on new navigation coordinates for the Strait of Hormuz — the vital chokepoint through which so much of the world's oil flows. But there's a catch: the new route would still send vessels through Iranian waters, keeping management authority in Tehran's hands. The United States objects, and the durability of the broader regional calm remains uncertain.

A couple of quick ones. In the United States, customs authorities have refunded over a hundred billion dollars in Trump-era tariffs following a February court ruling — around fifteen point seven trillion yen so far, with the eventual total expected to reach a hundred and sixty-six billion dollars. Nikkei calls it the largest tax refund operation in American history. And the United Nations is warning that its peacekeeping operations are near breaking point, as the U.S. withholds some four hundred sixty billion yen in assessed contributions. The UNIFIL mission in Lebanon is set to end at year's end.

Back home, some good news for workers: summer bonuses at major Japanese companies have hit an all-time high. Keidanren, the business federation, reports the average topped one million yen for the first time — and in construction, the average bonus broke two million yen. That's the labor shortage at work, pushing wages up across the economy.

And now, the one that caught my eye — from deeper in today's paper, out of Taiwan. Taiwanese authorities are investigating seventeen Chinese firms for illegally poaching the island's tech talent. We're talking about semiconductor and advanced technology engineers — the people at the very heart of Taiwan's crown jewels. According to Nikkei, companies including the battery giant CATL are accused of using shell entities and consultant fronts to hire these workers without authorization. It's a vivid illustration of just how fierce the competition for skilled minds has become. In the great technology rivalry between China and the wider world, the battle isn't only over chips and factories. It's over people — and who gets to keep them.
