Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Monday, the twenty-fourth of August, 2026, and there's a real thread running through today's paper about the future — how Japan intends to build it, who's going to do the work, and what happens when the old ways of doing things start to give way. Let's get into it.

We'll start with the story leading the Nikkei this morning, and it's a good one to understand what Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi's government is actually trying to do. Japan's state-backed green investment body — it goes by the name GX, for Green Growth Transformation — is putting about thirty billion yen into two Japanese startups. One is called Kyoto Fusioneering, which makes the equipment that goes around nuclear fusion reactors. The other is JEPLAN, which does chemical recycling of plastics. Now, thirty billion is the government's piece. Once you add in private money, the total mobilized comes to around two hundred billion yen.

And here's why that matters. This is a test case for something much larger. Takaichi's flagship economic idea is to channel three hundred and seventy trillion yen — public and private money combined — into seventeen strategic sectors between now and 2040. The goal is net-zero emissions by 2050, but done in a way that doesn't sacrifice energy security or economic growth. That's the delicate balance. Japan wants to decarbonize without becoming dependent on anyone, and it wants to do it while reviving the kind of technological edge it used to be famous for. The competition here is the United States and China, both of whom are pouring money into exactly these fields.

And fusion is the glamorous end of it. Just to give you a sense of the momentum — after this GX backing, Kyoto Fusioneering's valuation jumped sixty percent, to about a hundred and twenty billion yen. That makes it Japan's sixth "unicorn" — a privately held startup worth over a billion dollars. The company already has more than fourteen billion yen in orders from partners in the U.S., the EU, and beyond, and it's aiming for a full-scale demonstration of fusion power in the 2030s. Fusion has been "thirty years away" for about seventy years now, so a healthy skepticism is warranted. But the money moving into it is real.

Now, from the future of energy to the future of work — and here the picture is more complicated. Nikkei reports on the front page that Japan's three megabanks — Mitsubishi UFJ, Sumitomo Mitsui, and Mizuho — plan to hire fewer new graduates next year. Together they'll take on 2,180 new hires in fiscal 2027. That's down almost five percent, and it's the first decline in five years.

At least one of the banks pointed to AI. Automation is quietly doing the work that armies of young employees used to do. But — and this is the interesting part — this isn't the grim story it might have been a decade ago. Back in the 2010s, the banks cut hiring because they were struggling: negative interest rates, closing branches. This time, they're highly profitable. They're simply hiring differently — fewer generalists, more specialists in digital work and asset management. And starting salaries have gone up sharply. A new college graduate now earns about 266,700 yen a month on average, up more than 46,000 yen since 2020. Pay them well, they stay longer, and you need to recruit fewer people. So this is contraction born of strength, not weakness.

And there's a lovely companion story here from Konica Minolta, the imaging company. They've launched what you might call an internal gig economy. Regular, non-management employees can now pick up side assignments in other departments — up to twenty hours a month, for extra pay of up to a hundred thousand yen. In their pilot runs, they filled 24 of 33 posted roles, mostly in software and digital transformation. The idea is to let people broaden their skills and satisfy their curiosity inside the company, so they don't go looking elsewhere. It's a clever response to a fierce war for talent — instead of guarding people in their silos, let them roam a little.

One more from the front page, and this one's about the cars we drive. Japan's transport ministry will require driver-monitoring systems in all new vehicles starting September 2031, extending to existing models by 2033. These systems watch your eyes. If you stare at your phone for three and a half seconds while going over fifty kilometers an hour, or six seconds at lower speeds, the car sounds an alert. This follows UN safety standards agreed in 2024, and the EU has done something similar. The trigger was sobering: 148 distracted-driving casualties in Japan last year. Your car, it seems, is about to start paying attention to whether you are.

Let's turn abroad, where the war in Ukraine continues to reshape Russia from the inside. Nikkei has a striking piece on how Russia is trying to keep its army supplied with men. Regional authorities are now offering enlistment bonuses of up to 8.6 million yen in St. Petersburg — that's forty-five times the average monthly income there. Some regions are forgiving debts of up to ten million yen for those who sign up. Russia did recruit around 200,000 contract soldiers in the first half of this year. But the losses are staggering — more than 30,000 casualties a month. And it's expensive. Russia's fiscal deficit has already hit 6.4 trillion yen this year, blowing past the full-year projection. You can only pay men that much to fight for so long.

Meanwhile, President Putin is rallying his party ahead of parliamentary elections in mid-September, framing the vote as a referendum on national sovereignty. The one anti-war opposition party, Yabloko, has been kept off the ballot entirely, so the outcome isn't in doubt. This is theater — a manufactured show of support for the invasion.

A few more quickly from around the world. In Thailand, after a fourteen-year-old killed six people at a school near Bangkok, the government has suspended new gun permits and is tightening its firearm laws. Nikkei notes, pointedly, that Japan's own gun rules are actually fairly loose by regional standards. In American politics, a Texas Senate race has become a genuine contest — the Democrat, James Talavera, has out-raised the Republican incumbent three to one on small donations, and the race is now rated a toss-up in what should be safe Republican ground. And Iran has warned that any country helping enforce new U.S. sanctions will be treated as an enemy, threatening Persian Gulf oil exports.

That threat connects neatly to a clever bit of corporate problem-solving in today's business pages. Sumitomo Warehouse has validated a new shipping route that avoids the Strait of Hormuz altogether — Japan to Oman's port of Salalah, then overland across the Empty Quarter desert into Saudi Arabia. It costs sixty percent more than normal shipping, but it sidesteps the congestion and risk of the Red Sea. Trading houses are already using it for chemical shipments. When the sea lanes get dangerous, you go around by land.

And that brings me to the one that caught my eye, tucked in the business section. A company called Pocketalk — which makes handheld translation devices — is releasing something genuinely unusual next spring. It's called Pocketalk Z, and it's the world's first offline translation gadget. More than twenty languages, all processed by AI on the device itself. No internet connection. No cloud. Not even a USB port. And here's the kicker — it costs around sixteen thousand dollars per unit.

Sixteen thousand dollars, for a translator. Who on earth pays that? The answer: governments and corporations who cannot risk their conversations leaking into the cloud. In an age where we assume everything is connected, there's suddenly real value in a device that is deliberately, expensively, cut off from the world. Pocketalk hopes to sell 300,000 of them in three years. Sometimes the future isn't about connecting everything — it's about knowing when to unplug.
