Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Sunday, the fifteenth of November, 2026, and I'm glad you're with me.

We begin this morning not in Tokyo, and not on Wall Street, but out in the Japanese countryside — in the fields. The Nikkei leads today with an investigation into something that's been quietly happening for decades, and is now speeding up: Japan is losing its farmland.

Here are the numbers, and they are striking. Over the past fifty years, Japan has lost nearly a quarter of its agricultural land — twenty-four percent. And in just the last fifteen years, three hundred and twelve municipalities have each lost more than ten percent of their farmland. Add it all up, and the land that's vanished is larger than the entire prefecture of Niigata.

Where is it going? Hotels. Housing. Shopping. And the economics here are almost impossible for a struggling farmer, or a cash-strap4 town, to resist. Nikkei reports that a plot of cheap farmland can jump forty-one-fold in value the moment it's approved for development. Forty-one times. That's the figure driving all of this, especially in tourist areas and in the commuter belts around big cities.

Now, this connects to a human story underneath the land story. In 1976, Japan had just over five million people whose main work was farming. Today, that number has fallen below one million. The farmers are aging, the young aren't replacing them, and farming simply doesn't pay enough to compete with the developer's check.

And here's the bind for local government. A town that preserves a rice paddy gets very little tax revenue from it. A town that approves a hotel or a housing estate gets a great deal. When your budget is tight — and most of these towns' budgets are — the incentive points one direction. The Nikkei's experts warn that without strategic intervention from the central government, and without making farming genuinely profitable again, Japan's food security — its ability to feed itself — keeps eroding, acre by acre.

Which brings me to our word of the day, because it's the heart of this story: 農地転用, nōchi tenyo. It means agricultural land conversion — turning farmland into something else. It's tightly regulated under two separate laws, and outside certain zones it requires prefectural approval. So the paper trail exists. The question the Nikkei is really asking is whether the rules are strong enough — and whether anyone has the will to enforce them when forty-one times your money is sitting on the other side of a signature.

Let me turn now to a scandal that's getting worse the longer anyone looks at it. Sony Life Insurance. Back in September, the company reported fraud by four employees, with damages of around five hundred and sixty-four million yen. That was the story. It is no longer the story. Nikkei reports this morning that fraudulent fund transfers apparently continued even while the company was investigating the original fraud. Let that sink in — the misconduct kept going during the probe meant to stop it. Sony Life has now suspended roughly fifty employees. From four to fifty. The Financial Services Agency is inspecting both Sony Life and its parent, Sony Financial Group.

What makes this look especially bad is the contrast the Nikkei draws. A competitor, Prudential Life, went through a similar scandal and responded with sweeping governance reforms and an outside investigation. Sony Life, so far, has done neither — it's postponed finishing its internal probe and hasn't even set up an external committee. In a crisis like this, speed and independence are everything, and Sony Life is showing little of either.

Now to the world stage, and a genuinely historic diplomatic moment. The White House has announced that on the nineteenth of November, Presidents Trump, Xi Jinping, and Putin will meet together — in Shenzhen, China, on the sidelines of the APEC summit. This is the first-ever three-way summit among the United States, China, and Russia. The stated topic: reducing nuclear weapons.

Now, let's be clear-eyed about this, as the Nikkei is. American officials are already lowering expectations — no agreement is expected at this stage. But they're framing it as a first: the first time the leaders of these three nuclear powers have sat down together to talk about how to reduce their arsenals. And read the room. Trump wants a foreign-policy legacy. China gets the prestige of hosting. And Russia is looking for favorable terms on Ukraine. Everyone arrives wanting something, which is exactly why these meetings are worth watching, agreement or no.

On that Ukraine question, there's a related data point today. Russia's economy is slowing — GDP growth came in at just one percent in the third quarter, down from one-point-three. Ukrainian drone strikes on Russian oil refineries are biting, and interest rates are sitting at a punishing fourteen percent. Consumer spending is holding up, but the war is grinding on Russia's output.

Closer to home, two stories about Japan's future. The Defense Ministry plans to consolidate the Ground Self-Defense Force's command structure — merging the Eastern Army headquarters in Tokyo with the Northeastern Army headquarters in Sendai. The goal is better coordination with the naval and air forces, fewer staff tied up in command roles, and more personnel pushed to the front lines, with AI picking up some of the load. The Maritime force already tried this model last year.

And some welcome news for a sector that badly needs it. The Health Ministry is considering raising care service fees in phases starting in 2027 — breaking from the usual practice of freezing them for three years at a time. Why does this matter? Care workers earn about three hundred and fourteen thousand yen a month — roughly eighty-two thousand yen below the national average. The work is essential, the pay is low, and the shortages are chronic. This is an attempt, finally, to fund better wages.

A few quick business notes. NVIDIA is in turmoil — co-founder Jensen Huang has abruptly resigned amid allegations of an affair with a Chinese espionage suspect and separate misconduct. Colette Kress steps in as interim CEO. And even though the company just posted ninety-one percent revenue growth, the stock fell four percent, because as one analyst put it bluntly, "Jensen cannot be replaced by any other CEO." Succession, overnight, has become the story. And here in Japan, Netflix is raising subscription prices eleven to thirteen percent from the fourteenth of December — the ad-supported tier goes to nine hundred and ninety yen.

Which brings me to the one that caught my eye — and it's a story about something that failed, and what that failure should teach us. Last week, Microsoft 365 went down for thirty-six hours. Email, file-sharing, AI tools — all of it, gone, across banks, corporations, and airlines. Japan Airlines and ANA both saw delays.

The Nikkei's editorial takes this and sharpens it into a warning. We've all quietly come to assume the cloud simply never fails. It does. And the paper argues that Japanese companies and the government need to abandon the myth of perfect IT and plan instead for the certainty of downtime — redundancy, backups, even old-fashioned analog procedures. Consider this: modernizing just the American aviation system is estimated at eight trillion yen. In an age of AI-driven cyberattacks, resilience isn't paranoia. It's prudence. The question isn't whether these systems go down. It's whether you're ready when they do.
