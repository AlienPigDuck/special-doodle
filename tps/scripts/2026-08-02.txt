Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Sunday, the second of August, 2026, and there's a lot moving this weekend — starting with the yen.

Let's begin with the story that leads the paper this morning: a rare and rather dramatic show of financial force. On July 31st, Japanese and American authorities carried out a coordinated currency intervention, buying yen together to halt its slide. Now, Japan intervening in its own currency is not unheard of. What makes this striking is that the United States joined in. The New York Federal Reserve sold euros for yen, alongside Japan's own dollar-selling operation. And the US Treasury did something extraordinary — it announced the intervention to markets beforehand. That's a signal. It tells traders: don't bet against the yen, because Washington is standing behind Tokyo.

And that's really the heart of this story. This is a public gesture of support from the Trump administration for Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi's government. The yen rebounded to about 157 to the dollar — its strongest in months. Japan even posted on X that it can draw dollars from the New York Fed using its US Treasury holdings as collateral. The message there is: our firepower is essentially unlimited. Don't test us.

But — and there's always a but — this support comes with a price tag. Treasury Secretary Bessent is pushing Japan to raise interest rates faster at the Bank of Japan, and he wants that discussed at the G20 in August. He's also pressing Tokyo to speed up 55 billion dollars in investment it pledged to the United States. So America helps hold up the yen, and in return, it wants Japanese rate hikes and Japanese money flowing westward. This is the shape of the alliance right now — cooperation, with conditions.

Which makes our buried story this morning all the more interesting, and I'll come back to it, because it complicates that picture.

Now, to a very physical crisis. The magnitude-7 earthquake that struck Kumamoto on July 28th is still rippling through Japan's industrial machine, and the damage is spreading well beyond the epicenter. Honda's Kumamoto plant — which, remarkably, is Japan's only two-wheeler factory — will stay idle until at least August 17th. But the real story is the supply chain. Toyota, Nissan, and Mitsubishi have all halted production at factories on the main islands, hundreds of miles away. Why? Because critical component makers — Aisin, Astemo, their subsidiaries and partners — were hit, and when a few key suppliers go down, the whole line stops. It's a vivid reminder of how tightly wound modern manufacturing is. One region shakes, and the entire country's car production stutters.

And speaking of Toyota, there's a quieter but clever piece of news. Starting this month, Toyota will let buyers retrofit used vehicles with advanced safety features — collision-avoidance radar and the like — for somewhere between ten thousand and a hundred thousand yen. That's roughly seventy dollars to seven hundred. It's a smart move on two fronts: it makes Toyota's used cars more competitive against cheaper rivals, and it opens up revenue after the sale. Toyota wants to grow used-car sales from 350,000 to 550,000 units a year by 2030.

Let me stay in the domestic economy for a moment, because two stories here touch ordinary households. First: labor. Foreign worker intake in Japan is bumping up against its limits in key sectors. Food services is at 95 percent of capacity. Food manufacturing, 72 percent. Construction, 67. Employers are scrambling for contingency plans, and there's pressure on the government to loosen the caps on the Specified Skills visa — that's the program that lets foreign workers fill labor shortages. But Prime Minister Takaichi's administration is cautious about expanding it. So the tension is building: the economy needs workers, the politics resist.

And second, on healthcare. As of August 1st, the ceiling on out-of-pocket medical expenses has risen — four to seven percent. That doesn't sound like much, but there's a further 38 percent increase planned for next year. The worry, quite bluntly, is that some people will simply stop seeking treatment because they can't afford it. The government is trying to soften the blow with annual caps for long-term care patients, but this is a real strain on Japanese families, and one worth watching.

Let's step out into the world. In the United States, the political weather is turning stormy ahead of November's midterms. Both chambers of Congress are genuinely competitive. Republicans hold a slight edge in the Senate; Democrats lead in the House, but that lead is narrowing amid infighting between their moderate and progressive wings. President Trump's approval sits at just under 40 percent, weighed down by public skepticism over the conflict with Iran and by inflation.

In Ukraine, a brutal night. Russia struck Kyiv with 35 missiles and 185 drones overnight into August 1st. Nine civilians were killed, and the Lithuanian embassy building was damaged. Here's the number that tells the grim story: of 27 ballistic missiles, Ukraine intercepted just one. That reflects an acute shortage of air-defense ammunition — the interceptors are running low, and it shows.

And in the world of football, a rare defeat for FIFA's president. Gianni Infantino had proposed spinning off a World Cup subsidiary and selling a 20 percent stake to outside investors — a deal valued at 4.2 billion dollars. UEFA and other confederations rebelled. Fifty-five European federations voted to boycott, and the plan collapsed. A reminder that even football's most powerful man has limits.

On the corporate front, the big theme is Chinese ambition in technology. A firm called Moonshot AI has released a new model, Kimi K3, which apparently outperforms Anthropic's Claude on web-development tasks — at a quarter of the cost, though it's twice as slow. Its parent company just listed at what Nikkei reports is the largest valuation on mainland China. Alongside that, the memory-chip maker CXMT has climbed to fourth place globally in DRAM, with about 8 percent of the market. There's still a two-to-three generation technology gap versus Samsung and SK, but with heavy state backing, it's a long-term threat — particularly to Japan's own Kioxia. And Beijing is now preparing export controls on AI training data and semiconductor designs — a sign it feels confident enough to play offense.

Meanwhile in Europe's old economy, BMW is cutting 5 percent of its workforce — 8,000 jobs by the end of 2027 — after profit fell 28 percent, driven by a 20 percent collapse in China sales. The German giants are struggling; the French, curiously, are doing better by staying out of China's cutthroat market.

Now, the story that caught my eye — and it circles right back to where we started. Deep in the paper, the "Wind Gauge" column notes something quietly remarkable: Prime Minister Takaichi has made no calls to foreign leaders since June 1st. Two months of near-silence. And this at a moment when Japan's interests with Trump on trade, Taiwan, and the abduction issue are on the line.

Compare this to Shinzo Abe, who called Trump constantly — even about routine matters — precisely to keep a channel open for the crises. The column warns that without that steady input, Japan risks fading from Trump's ear, especially as he plans three more summits with Xi Jinping. And there's a poignant human detail: the piece suggests Takaichi is inward-focused and low on energy, reportedly sleeping between zero and three hours a night. So while America backs the yen, the phone line to Washington sits quiet. A striking contrast.
