Let's start with money — specifically, the money the Japanese government is trying to find.

Nikkei's lead story this morning lays out a fiscal squeeze that's going to define Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi's next year in office. As her administration puts together the budget for fiscal 2027, it's facing roughly ten trillion yen in new spending pressures. And here's the catch — it wants to do all of this without leaning on deficit bonds. In other words, without simply borrowing the money.

So where's that ten trillion going? A few places. Takaichi wants to cut the consumption tax on food — that's the tax you pay at the register on groceries. She wants to keep raising defense spending. And there's a long list of other priorities besides. Meanwhile, the ministries themselves are already asking for far more than they got last year.

Now, here's the part that makes this genuinely difficult. The Finance Ministry has quietly raised the interest rate it assumes it'll have to pay on government debt — from three percent up to three point eight percent. That may sound like a small technical adjustment. It isn't. It could push Japan's debt service costs — just the cost of paying interest — to a record thirty-six point six trillion yen. Japan carries an enormous national debt, the largest relative to its economy in the developed world, and for years low interest rates made that debt cheap to carry. Those days are ending. As rates rise, the bill comes due.

The government's plan hinges on one hopeful number: tax revenue of ninety trillion yen next year. If the economy grows and revenues come in, the math holds together. If not, all that talk of avoiding deficit bonds becomes very hard to keep. And Nikkei notes this isn't just a domestic story — Japan's fiscal position ripples outward, even nudging US Treasury yields, because Japanese investors are among the largest holders of American government debt. When Tokyo's finances wobble, the world notices.

Staying with government spending, a few of those budget requests are worth pulling out.

The Foreign Ministry is asking for about one point two trillion yen — the first time it's ever crossed the trillion-yen line, and a forty percent jump from last year. Roughly two hundred billion of that goes to crisis management and cultural promotion. It fits Takaichi's stated theme of a "Strong and Prosperous Japan" — the idea being that geopolitical influence needs funding, not just words.

The Defense Ministry, meanwhile, wants to build artificial intelligence into its command systems for faster decision-making, create what it calls a high-security "sovereign cloud" for classified data, and keep developing hypersonic missiles for counterstrike capability. And the Interior Ministry has unveiled a new plan for responding to chemical and biological terrorism — the sort of scenario where police, hospitals, and fire departments have historically worked in silos. The new protocol lets them share information in real time, and interestingly, it prioritizes rapid decontamination over the traditional triage of sorting the wounded — the thinking being that you stop the contamination first, to prevent a second wave of casualties.

There's also a delicate foreign policy note. One week after Russian President Putin visited Etorofu — one of the disputed Northern Territories, islands Japan claims but Russia has held since the end of the Second World War — the Takaichi cabinet has chosen restraint. No recalling the ambassador, no new sanctions. Part of that caution is uncertainty about where the Trump administration stands, and part is very practical: Japan still relies on liquefied natural gas from the Sakhalin 2 project in Russia's far east, and with the Middle East unsettled, Tokyo can't afford to jeopardize its energy supply.

Let me turn to a couple of stories about how Japan does business — and how it's trying to change.

Nikkei reports that the Financial Services Agency has registered a company called SmartRound as the country's first broker for a secondary market in unlisted shares. Now, what does that mean, and why should you care? When a startup raises money, its early investors and employees hold shares that they usually can't sell until the company goes public. That creates enormous pressure to rush to an IPO — Nikkei calls it the "IPO goal" problem, where going public becomes the finish line rather than a milestone. A secondary market lets people sell those shares privately, to long-term institutional investors, without that rush. The gap here is striking: America's secondary market hit about thirty-eight trillion yen last year. Japan's was one trillion. This is Tokyo trying to grow its own unicorns — those billion-dollar startups — with patience instead of panic.

And in a nod to anyone who's ever tried to cancel an online subscription: the Consumer Affairs Agency plans to regulate what are called "dark patterns." These are the sneaky design tricks websites use to manipulate you — the pre-checked box that signs you up for something, the cancellation process buried five clicks deep, the free trial that quietly becomes a paid subscription. The agency estimates the damage at up to three point two trillion yen a year. Soon, those tactics could trigger administrative penalties. A small, welcome win for anyone with a phone.

On the corporate front, a genuinely remarkable medical story. A Japanese company called Oncoris has launched what Nikkei describes as a world-first: a viral treatment for esophageal cancer. It's called Telomelysin, and it's a genetically modified virus — an adenovirus — that attacks tumors. In mid-stage trials it eliminated tumors in half of patients, with minimal side effects. It's aimed at people who can't have surgery or chemotherapy, and doctors are calling it a possible "fifth modality" of cancer treatment. It isn't cheap — about nine point three million yen per patient — but the global market for these so-called oncolytic virus therapies is expected to quadruple over the next decade. This is the frontier of medicine, and Japan is planting a flag on it.

Quickly, from abroad. Samsung Electronics announced a record shareholder return — up to twelve trillion yen in 2026, roughly five times its previous record, fueled by soaring demand for AI memory chips. And on the other end of the spectrum, the fast-fashion giant SHEIN has delayed its Hong Kong stock listing and slashed its valuation to around two hundred sixty billion dollars. Remember, that company was once talked about at a trillion dollars. US tariffs, changing customs rules, and regulatory scrutiny have taken the shine off.

One more international note that caught me by surprise. Switzerland — famously, eternally neutral — is heading to a referendum next month on whether to write "perpetual armed neutrality" directly into its constitution. It would bar cooperation with NATO and forbid joining international sanctions unless the UN mandates them. And early polling suggests Swiss voters will reject it — fifty-four percent opposed. Every major party except the People's Party is urging a no vote. In a Europe reshaped by war in Ukraine, even Switzerland is reconsidering what neutrality should mean.

But here's the one that caught my eye this morning, tucked into the domestic pages — and it's about water. Bottled water.

Trial Holdings, a Japanese discount retailer, has just opened a factory in Tochigi Prefecture that produces a 500-milliliter bottle of water for forty-one yen. That is now, Nikkei reports, the lowest floor price in Japan — undercutting nearly everyone, and just one yen above its rival Pan-Pacific's forty. Think about that. We are watching a price war fought over a single yen, on a bottle of water.

But there's a real story underneath the penny-pinching. In an era of stubborn inflation, when the cost of nearly everything has been creeping up, this is a company betting that shoppers are counting every yen — and building its own factory to control its supply chain and hold that price. When companies start manufacturing their own bottled water to shave off pennies, you're seeing how deeply cost-consciousness has settled into Japanese daily life. Sometimes the biggest economic story is hiding in the smallest bottle.
