We begin this morning in Kumamoto, on the southern island of Kyushu, where two of the biggest names in technology are placing a very large bet on Japan's future. Nikkei reports that Sony and Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company — TSMC, the world's most important chipmaker — plan to start producing next-generation image sensors in Kumamoto by around 2029. The combined investment: roughly one trillion yen. The two firms will set up a joint venture this fiscal year, with Sony holding sixty percent and TSMC forty, to develop advanced chips for smartphones, artificial intelligence, and self-driving cars.

Now, if you've been following this podcast, you'll know Kumamoto has become the beating heart of Japan's chip revival. TSMC already runs plants there. And here's what makes today's news striking: just last month, Kumamoto was hit by an earthquake. You might expect that to give investors pause. Instead, both companies went out of their way to reaffirm their commitment to the region — citing its abundant fresh water, which chipmaking devours in enormous quantities, and its cheap renewable energy.

The strategic picture matters too. Image sensors are the tiny eyes inside every camera and phone, and Sony has long been the global leader. But Chinese and South Korean rivals are closing in. This investment is Japan's answer — a way to defend a crown it doesn't intend to lose.

And that theme — Japanese corporate strength — runs right through this morning's front page. Nikkei reports that consolidated net profit at 956 companies on the Tokyo Stock Exchange's Prime market jumped sixty-eight percent in the April-to-June quarter, with sales up fourteen percent. Semiconductors and automakers led the way. Most remarkable of all: the profit margin reached nine percent — the highest in thirty-one years, since the financial turmoil of 1995. A weaker yen and the global rush to build AI infrastructure have lifted Japanese manufacturers across the board.

Related to this, an interesting figure from the paper. Berkshire Hathaway's Greg Abel — Warren Buffett's chosen successor — visited Japan as CEO, signaling that Berkshire is holding on to its stakes in Japan's five great trading houses. Those are sprawling conglomerates that deal in everything from energy to convenience stores. Their combined market value has quintupled since 2019. Nikkei describes how they're reinventing themselves — cutting unprofitable projects, using AI to sharpen investment decisions, behaving less like traders and more like portfolio managers.

But — and there's always a "but" — this rosy corporate picture sits alongside some sobering national numbers. Japan's current account surplus hit a record 17.4 trillion yen in the first half of the year. That's the broadest measure of money flowing in from abroad. And yet the yen still weakened by ten yen against the dollar. Why? Because those overseas earnings aren't coming home. Japanese companies are reinvesting profits abroad rather than repatriating them, and foreign investment into Japan remains weak — a quiet verdict on the country's low growth potential and its shrinking workforce.

And the debt keeps climbing. National debt reached a record 1,346.7 trillion yen as of June, and this year's supplementary budget is expected to push that toward 1,493 trillion by year's end. Numbers so large they almost lose meaning — but they frame every policy choice the government makes.

Speaking of the government, a revealing detail about how Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi runs her administration. Nikkei has analyzed her private meetings and found that only nine percent involve individual company executives — while thirty-nine percent are with industry associations. In other words, Takaichi prefers to hear from groups rather than from individual chief executives. Read into that what you will about her governing style: coordination over one-on-one lobbying.

Her government is also developing an income-linked cash benefit system for lower-income workers, starting in fiscal 2027 — the kind of targeted support that becomes harder to fund when the debt looks the way it does.

Now, a contrast that jumped out at me. American military recruitment surged twelve percent last fiscal year, to over 160,000 soldiers — the strongest growth since the end of the Cold War. The driver? Pay. Monthly salaries climbed from around 1,700 dollars to over 2,400. Meanwhile, Japan's Self-Defense Force fell thirty percent short of its recruitment target — despite plans to restructure salaries. It's a stark illustration of a demographic squeeze that shapes everything here, from defense to the economy.

Let's turn abroad, where tensions are running high in the Persian Gulf. Iran has appointed a hardliner, Mohsen Rezai, to lead its Supreme National Security Council, and has issued six conditions for reopening the Strait of Hormuz. That strait is the chokepoint through which a fifth of the world's oil passes. Iran's demands include an end to threats against it, lifting the US naval blockade, paying reparations, removing sanctions, and unfreezing assets. These are not the terms of a country looking to compromise — and a sixty-day negotiation deadline from June's ceasefire is fast approaching.

In Asia, a notable diplomatic shift. Thailand, Vietnam, and Myanmar have all added explicit support for China's "peaceful unification" of Taiwan to recent bilateral statements. For countries that have long tried to balance between Beijing and Washington, that's a meaningful tilt — bought, Nikkei suggests, with Chinese investment and military cooperation.

And Taiwan itself is preparing for the worst. It carried out its first large-scale drill to throttle mobile networks — deliberately cutting service — affecting seventeen million people. The scenario: a Chinese strike on communications infrastructure. A sobering exercise in imagining the unimaginable.

There was also a natural disaster overnight. A magnitude 7.4 earthquake struck western Colombia, killing at least twenty people, injuring hundreds, and collapsing buildings in the cities of Pereira and Manizales. Our thoughts are with everyone affected.

Midea's portable unit — the PortaSplit — has already sold 200,000 units through June, double all of last year, as Europe bakes in record heat and many buildings ban the outdoor units traditional air conditioners require.

And here at home, Rakuten's mobile business posted its first operating profit in seven years — 504 billion yen in the first half. Good news, hard-won. But there's a catch: rival KDDI is phasing out the roaming agreement that lets Rakuten borrow its network, starting in October. So Rakuten now has to spend heavily, and fast, to build out coverage on its own.

Before we go, the one that caught my eye — and it's a story about the very people paid to see the future. Buried deeper in the paper is a warning that Japan's consulting industry is heading for a bubble and a reckoning. Major firms now expect the market to peak around 2030, then contract. Deloitte Tohmatsu itself predicts the industry will shrink. The culprit is artificial intelligence, which is quietly erasing the bread-and-butter work of consulting — the research, the slide decks, the document preparation. Japan has over 100,000 consultants, and Nikkei suggests many in the middle ranks may soon face retrenchment.

What I find fascinating is the question it raises. The work that AI can't touch, the paper argues, is the work requiring human judgment and ethical reasoning — the harder, higher kind of advice. So the challenge isn't just layoffs. It's whether an industry built on producing documents can reinvent itself around producing wisdom. That's a much taller order — and one worth watching, because if it's happening to consultants, it's coming for a lot of us.
