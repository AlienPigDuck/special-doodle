Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Friday, the twentieth of November, 2026, and we have a front page today that historians may end up quoting for years.

Let's begin in Shenzhen, where something happened yesterday that had never happened before. The leaders of the United States, China, and Russia — Donald Trump, Xi Jinping, and Vladimir Putin — sat down together for the first-ever trilateral summit between those three powers. They discussed nuclear arms reduction, the war in Ukraine, Iran, and, in the words Nikkei uses, "reshaping the international order." And when they were done, they issued a joint "peace declaration," pledging dialogue on reducing nuclear risk and on non-proliferation.

Now, on paper, that sounds reassuring. Three nuclear-armed giants talking instead of threatening each other. But the reaction among Western allies has been something closer to alarm. And the word you'll see again and again in this morning's coverage is "Munich." Nikkei reports that observers are drawing a direct line to the 1938 Munich Conference — the moment when major powers carved up a smaller nation's territory in a backroom deal and called it peace. The worry here is the same: that when the three strongest countries in the world meet to redraw the map among themselves, the nations not in the room — and the rules that are supposed to protect them — get traded away.

The deeper anxiety, and Nikkei is quite direct about this, is that President Trump appears increasingly willing to cut deals with authoritarian leaders at the expense of democratic values and the rule-of-law order that the postwar world was built on. For Japan — a democracy that has staked its security on exactly that order — this is not an abstract concern. It's personal.

And it connects to the second big story on the front page. During his one-on-one with Xi Jinping, Trump told the Chinese leader that the United States does not support Taiwan independence. Now, that's actually consistent with what previous American administrations have said for decades — so in one sense, nothing new. But the timing matters. It comes amid rising tension over Taiwan, and amid Chinese criticism of Japan's own defense policies. When you say the familiar thing at an unfamiliar moment, the meaning shifts.

Which brings us to how Japan is responding — and the answer is: by building friendships elsewhere. Nikkei reports that Japan and India have agreed, in a 2+2 ministerial meeting, to let Japan's Self-Defense Forces conduct missile launch drills on Indian soil by 2027. Let that sink in — Japanese forces test-firing missiles in India. Both countries are feeling Chinese military pressure: Japan around the Senkaku Islands and the South China Sea, India across the Indian Ocean. So they're drawing closer. If the big powers are making deals over everyone's heads, the middle powers are quietly linking arms.

Now, Prime Minister Takaichi has her own version of this balancing act playing out at home. Nikkei reports that Japan's defense budget negotiations are tense. Takaichi is insisting that any spending increase be based on genuine strategic need — not on hitting some arbitrary number. The figures being thrown around are striking: initial estimates of seventy to eighty trillion yen, now being pressed down toward fifty to sixty trillion. She wants to strengthen Japan's defenses without picking a fight with Beijing, and without blowing a hole in the budget. That's a very narrow path to walk.

And Takaichi is walking it on another front too. Despite everything, she met Putin at the APEC summit. Why? Two reasons, both concrete. First, to press for the release of Yuki Imai, a Hokkaido Shimbun reporter who's been detained in Russia. And second — this is the one that affects corporate Japan — to push back on a Putin decree that seized control of Japanese company subsidiaries.

Because here is where the Ukraine war lands directly on a Japanese balance sheet. Japan Tobacco — JT — announced this morning a staggering write-down: one-point-three-eight-six trillion yen. That's the largest loss in the company's history since 2004. It happened because JT lost control of its Russian subsidiaries after Putin placed them under temporary government management. So JT has simply pulled those operations out of its books entirely. And the warning signal here is loud: Nikkei notes that more than a hundred Japanese firms still have operations inside Russia. JT's trillion-yen lesson is one they'll all be studying.

Let me turn to a very different front-page story, but a historic one. The International Court of Justice has ruled, for the first time ever, that a nation committed genocide. The nation is Myanmar, and the victims are the Rohingya minority — systematic killings and forced displacement that began in 2017. The court has ordered Myanmar to compensate victims and to conduct investigations within two years. And Nikkei points out why this matters beyond Myanmar: it sets a legal precedent, at a moment when other cases — including proceedings over Israel's actions in Gaza — are facing similar scrutiny. A line has been drawn.

Now, before we leave the heavy news, a quick but important one for anyone who runs a website — which, these days, is nearly everyone. A serious security vulnerability has been discovered in Nginx, server software used by roughly sixty percent of Japanese websites. It was found, interestingly, by Japanese white-hat hackers working with researchers from a Google subsidiary. Japan's National Cyber Security Center has issued an urgent alert: patch now. And this fits a worrying trend — Nikkei reports a surge in North Korean cyberattacks, increasingly using AI to find weaknesses faster than humans can close them.

Let's shift to business, where the headline number is almost hard to say with a straight face. The AI company Anthropic went public on Nasdaq. It opened at eighty-three dollars a share, climbed past a hundred and eleven, and reached a fully diluted valuation of around one-point-five trillion dollars. What makes this fascinating isn't just the size — it's the tangle of relationships underneath. Amazon, Google and other tech giants collectively own roughly thirty percent of Anthropic, and they're also the ones providing its computing power. So they profit when it grows, and it depends on them to grow. Nikkei raises the obvious question: who, exactly, is governing whom?

And the appetite for AI is quietly reshaping the physical economy. A survey by the supply-chain firm Resillia found that eighty-six percent of manufacturers suffered what they call "buying defeat" — simply unable to get the semiconductors they wanted at the price they wanted. The reason? AI data centers are hoovering up the chips. Average fulfillment hit just under eighty percent.

And finally, the one that caught my eye — tucked deep in the paper. After thirty years of dormancy, Japan's rare earth industry is waking up. Back in the 1990s, Chinese competition drove Japanese producers out of the business entirely. Now, with China restricting exports, they're coming back. Proterial — the company formerly known as Hitachi Metals — is expanding its smelting capacity with a sixteen-billion-yen investment, half of it government-subsidized. Shin-Etsu Chemical is building a thirty-five-billion-yen facility in Fukui.

Why now? Two minerals — dysprosium and terbium — that go into the motors of electric cars. Nikkei reports that Chinese suppliers cut volumes eightfold, and in one case the price rose a hundred and seventy-four-fold. That's not a typo. So Japan is choosing resilience over profit margin — deliberately accepting a less efficient supply chain for a more secure one. It's the whole philosophy of "economic security" in a single investment: sometimes the point isn't to be cheap. The point is to not be cut off.
