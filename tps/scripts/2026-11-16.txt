Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Monday, the sixteenth of November, 2026. I'm so glad you're here.

We begin this morning with a ship. A single container ship, really, but one that carries a lot of meaning. According to the Nikkei this morning, Ocean Network Express — that's the Japan-backed shipping line known as ONE — has sailed through the Bab al-Mandab Strait and into the Red Sea for the first time in three years. On November fifteenth, the vessel reached the anchorage at the Suez Canal, carrying cargo bound for Europe.

Now, why does that matter? For three years, shipping companies have been avoiding the Red Sea entirely. Houthi attacks on vessels in the region made the route too dangerous, so ships were rerouted all the way around the southern tip of Africa — around the Cape of Good Hope. That detour adds ten to fifteen days to a voyage. For a country like Japan, this is not a small thing. Japan moves ninety-nine percent of its imports and exports by sea. Think about that for a moment — almost everything that enters or leaves this country travels on a ship. So when the sea lanes are disrupted, the cost of nearly everything rises. Economists call this cost-push inflation — prices going up not because people are buying more, but because it simply costs more to move goods around.

So ONE's return to the Red Sea is a hopeful sign. But — and here's the sober note — it may not bring relief at the checkout counter anytime soon. Nikkei reports that war-risk insurance premiums remain high, and ports along the route are still congested. An analyst at the shipping consultancy Drewry says the savings from the shorter transit time are, for now, largely eaten up by those extra costs. The global shipping market is expected to grow twenty-seven percent in 2027, but the path back to normal is going to be gradual, not sudden. One ship through the strait is a beginning, not a conclusion.

Let's turn now to something closer to home — and to the kitchen table, so to speak. The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare is taking aim at what you might call parental leave in name only. Here's the picture. In fiscal 2025, the share of Japanese fathers taking some form of parental leave hit a record — fifty-point-nine percent. More than half. On paper, that's a milestone. But look closer, and the picture changes. Only about ten percent of those fathers took leave lasting longer than six months. Compare that to mothers, where over ninety percent take extended leave. So the fathers are technically taking leave — but often just a few days, a token gesture, while the real, sustained burden of childcare still falls on women.

So the Ministry wants large companies — those with a thousand or more employees — to disclose not just whether men took leave, but for how long. The idea is that sunlight does the work. If a company has to publish how many of its men took a month or more, that's harder to fudge than a simple yes-or-no. The government will also adjust the standards for its "Kurumin" certification — that's a seal given to family-friendly workplaces, a kind of badge companies like to display. The thinking is straightforward: if you want credit for supporting families, show us the real numbers.

And staying with corporate Japan responding to its environment — there's a quietly interesting story about wood. Daito Kentaku, a major homebuilder, plans to double its use of domestic Japanese lumber by fiscal 2027, up to forty thousand cubic meters a year, sourced from a Sumitomo Forestry subsidiary in Fukushima. Here's what's driving it: the weak yen. For years, imported Canadian timber was simply cheaper — about twenty percent cheaper than Japanese wood. But as the yen has weakened, that gap has narrowed to just ten percent. Add in the memory of pandemic supply shocks and the disruption from Russia's war in Ukraine, and suddenly buying local looks a lot smarter. It's good news for Japan's long-neglected forestry industry, too. The country's timber self-sufficiency has climbed back to forty-two and a half percent, up from a low of under nineteen percent back in 2002.

Now, overseas, where there's a great deal moving this morning.

Let's start in Southeast Asia, where ASEAN held its summit on November fifteenth — and the most notable thing may be who wasn't there. Myanmar's military leader, Min Aung Hlaing, was not invited to participate fully. The regional bloc remains deadlocked over Myanmar's descent into violence. Years ago, ASEAN agreed on what's called the Five-Point Consensus — essentially a demand that Myanmar's junta stop the killing and open dialogue. Progress has been nearly nonexistent. And the member states are split: some want engagement, others insist on strict compliance before any restoration of status. Thailand's deputy prime minister, Seehasak, called a full return for Myanmar's leadership premature, suggesting any thaw should come gradually, through informal meetings first.

That same summit brought another disappointment. ASEAN has given up on its goal of finishing a Code of Conduct with China for the South China Sea by year's end. About eighty percent of the draft is agreed — but the hardest twenty percent remains: whether the code would be legally binding, and exactly which waters it covers. Those are, of course, the questions that actually matter.

Over in Washington, the political winds have shifted. In the 2026 U.S. midterm elections, Democrats won governorships in twenty-four of the thirty-six states that held races — including Alaska, which elected a Democratic governor for the first time in twenty-eight years. Gains came even in traditionally Republican territory.

And in China, the central bank has made a notable move. The People's Bank of China raised its foreign-currency deposit reserve requirement from four percent to six percent — its first increase in nearly five years. The goal is to cool off the yuan, which has been strengthening. A strong currency sounds flattering, but for an export-driven economy it makes your goods more expensive abroad. With China running a trade surplus topping a trillion dollars, Beijing wants to keep its exports competitive. Still, Nikkei notes the yuan stayed near four-year highs even after the move.

A few more briefly. France saw its fifth straight week of major protests — over a hundred thousand people demanding education funding and relief from the cost of living, with clashes in Paris, Bordeaux, and Lyon. And in Portugal, demonstrations across more than thirty cities took aim at a housing crisis that has pushed costs up nearly threefold in a decade, driven in part by foreign investment.

And a word for the space enthusiasts: SpaceX pulled off a first on November fourteenth, catching the Starship's upper stage with its "Mechazilla" mechanical arms — the first recovery of a reusable upper stage. A real engineering milestone.

And now, the one that caught my eye — buried, fittingly, in the business pages.

It's a story about a university and a tuna. Kindai University, near Osaka, managed something almost unheard of in Japan: it broke into the top of the rankings in a system famous for being rigid and nearly impossible to climb. And it did it with a fish. Back in 2002, Kindai's researchers achieved the first complete farm-raising of bluefin tuna — breeding the fish from egg to egg entirely in captivity, a genuine scientific breakthrough. And then, for about five years, that achievement just... sat there. Dormant.

Until someone realized it could be a brand. Nikkei reports the university took inspiration from Kobayashi Ichizo — the legendary founder of Hankyu Railway, who built the Takarazuka theater troupe to give people a reason to ride his trains. Kindai did something similar with its tuna, turning a laboratory success into a cultural identity, even opening restaurants serving the farmed fish. By 2014, Kindai had the most applicants of any private university in Japan. In a country facing a shrinking population, where universities are fighting to survive, it's a lovely example of how a single idea — patiently, cleverly used — can upend what everyone assumed was fixed.
