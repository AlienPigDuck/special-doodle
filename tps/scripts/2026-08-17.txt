Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Monday, the seventeenth of August, 2026. I'm glad you're here.

We begin this morning with money — big money, and who's chasing it.

Japan's biggest banks are quietly changing what they are. Nikkei reports this morning that Sumitomo Mitsui Financial Group and Mizuho are pouring resources into wealth management, and specifically into serving the ultra-wealthy. Sumitomo Mitsui plans to double the number of advisors it dedicates to that top tier — up to four hundred by 2028. Mizuho wants to grow its own wealth staff by half over the next couple of years.

Now, why does this matter? Because it tells you something about the future of Japanese banking. The old model — the one most of us picture, with a branch on every corner and modest interest on modest savings — that business just doesn't earn much anymore. So the banks are following the money, and in Japan the money is remarkably concentrated. There are about a hundred and twenty thousand ultra-wealthy households in Japan — that's households holding more than five hundred million yen in assets, which is roughly three and a half million dollars. And those households, small in number, control about a hundred and thirty-five trillion yen. That's ten percent of all the financial assets in the country.

The banks are looking abroad for the template. Nikkei points to Morgan Stanley, which now earns nearly half its revenue from wealth management, overseeing more than seven trillion dollars for clients around the world. Japan's mega banks want a slice of that same high-margin business — advising the rich rather than simply holding the savings of everyone else. It's a pivot with real consequences for ordinary customers, and one worth watching.

And it connects directly to our next story, because both are really about the same question: is Japan's financial system finally growing up?

The Nikkei Average — the main Tokyo stock index — hit a record in June. Seventy-two thousand yen and change. That's higher than even the optimists predicted. Foreign investors have bought more than ten trillion yen of Japanese shares. On the surface, a triumph.

But the commentary in this morning's paper delivers a warning, and it's a sober one. Japanese companies are still, in many cases, not very efficient with the capital they hold. A third of major Japanese firms — thirty-three percent — trade below book value. That means the market values the company at less than the sum of its parts. In the United States, that figure is two percent. Two. And return on equity, a basic measure of how much profit a company squeezes from shareholder money, sits at around ten percent in Japan, versus twenty-one percent in America.

Here's the memory that haunts this story. During the Abenomics years, foreign investors came rushing in on optimism — and then, when the returns disappointed, they sold and left. Nikkei's worry is that history could repeat. The record highs are real, but they rest on a promise of reform: companies selling off businesses they don't need, sharpening their profitability, treating shareholders' money with more respect. If that reform stalls, the enthusiasm could drain away just as it did before.

Now, from finance to the factory floor, and a couple of stories about Japan trying to move faster.

Honda is turning to artificial intelligence to speed up how it designs for crash safety. The company has built custom AI agents that comb through engineering data and suggest design changes — and Honda says this can accelerate that safety development by more than forty percent. Human engineers stay in the loop, choosing among the options. The target is 2030. And there's a clear motive here: Chinese carmakers have been developing new vehicles at a startling pace, and Honda is racing to keep up.

Speaking of the weather that all that logistics depends on — the Japan Meteorological Agency is upgrading its forecasts. By 2030, its one-month outlooks will be broken down week by week — rain, snow, sunshine — instead of being lumped into vague longer stretches. It sounds technical, but for retailers deciding what to stock, for farmers, for shipping companies, more precise forecasts are genuinely valuable, especially as extreme weather grows more common.

There's also some intriguing corporate news at home. Japan's two biggest convenience store chains — Seven & i and FamilyMart, which is backed by the trading house Itochu — are cooperating on ATMs. Over the next four years, FamilyMart locations will switch over to Seven Bank machines. These are fierce rivals, so any cooperation is notable. And it reflects the pressure of a shrinking population: with fewer customers to go around, even competitors are finding places to share costs.

Now to the wider world, where an election is shaping up as a proxy battle.

Brazil votes for a president in October, and Nikkei frames the contest bluntly as "Lula versus Trump." The leftist incumbent, Lula, faces the right-wing candidate Flávio Bolsonaro. And the Trump administration is openly backing Bolsonaro. The paper places this in a larger story — a revived American assertiveness in its own hemisphere, an echo of the old Monroe Doctrine, pressuring governments and championing the right. Brazil matters here because it's a heavyweight of the BRICS group and has stubbornly refused to line up behind Washington. So this election could signal a real shift.

Elsewhere in the world: Israel's economy grew at an annualized fifteen point four percent in the second quarter, a remarkable surge that Nikkei attributes to the June ceasefire with Iran. With security restrictions lifted, exports jumped more than twenty-five percent, and the central bank has nudged up its growth forecast — all contingent, of course, on the peace holding.

The war in Ukraine, meanwhile, saw one of its largest drone assaults yet. Ukraine sent roughly six hundred unmanned aircraft toward Moscow over the weekend. Russia claims it intercepted more than eight hundred drones across its territory and says it struck weapons facilities in Kyiv in response.

A couple of quicker notes. A powerful earthquake — magnitude seven point seven — struck eastern Indonesia, centered on Flores Island, killing at least fifty-one people, with thousands displaced. And here's a small but rather lovely one: Liechtenstein has changed its succession law so the eldest child inherits the throne regardless of gender. It was the last constitutional monarchy in Europe to do so — meaning every one of them now allows a woman to reign.

And now, the story that caught my eye — buried deeper in the paper, and quietly unsettling.

Japan's literary awards are drowning in submissions. The Hayakawa science fiction contest went from about four hundred entries to over a thousand. Kadokawa's light novel award rose forty percent. Why? Generative AI. It's now possible to produce a polished, publishable-looking manuscript with very little effort — and people are, by the thousands.

The problem for publishers isn't just the cost of reading it all. It's deeper. These contests have long been how Japan discovers and nurtures new writers — the pipeline through which talent emerges. If that system collapses under the sheer weight of machine-generated manuscripts, what happens to the human writers it was built to find? And underneath it all sits a genuinely hard question: what, now, does it even mean to be an author?
