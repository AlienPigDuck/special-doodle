Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Sunday, the thirtieth of August, 2026. I'm glad you're here.

Let's begin with something close to the ground — literally. This morning's Nikkei takes a hard look at bicycles, and at how badly Japan has fallen behind on making room for them. Now, this might sound like a small story, but stay with me, because it's really about safety, and about how cities decide who the roads are for.

Here's the striking number. Tokyo has less than a tenth of the cycling infrastructure that Paris has. In Paris, for every square kilometer of city, you'll find more than ten kilometers of cycling paths. In Tokyo? Less than seven-tenths of a single kilometer. And this matters right now, because Japan has been tightening its traffic laws for cyclists — pushing them off sidewalks and onto the roads — without first building the safe roads to put them on. The result is grim. Cyclists now account for more than fifteen percent of all traffic deaths in Japan.

To be fair, the government hasn't been idle. Nikkei reports that total cycling space has grown more than fivefold since 2017, reaching nearly ten thousand kilometers by March of last year. But here's the catch: most of that isn't protected bike lane. It's what's called "mixed-use" space — painted lines on roads where cars still drive right alongside you. The truly safe, separated paths are still rare. The government wants to expand them twenty percent by 2030, but in the dense heart of Japan's cities, where you'd need it most, land is scarce and construction is expensive. So the ambition runs straight into the price of Tokyo real estate. It's a quiet crisis, but a real one.

Now let's move to the front page, where the theme this morning is Japan building for the future — in money, in defense, and in the ruins at Fukushima.

First, the money. The Tokyo Stock Exchange is setting an IPO record — but not in the way you might expect. The average company going public this year is worth around 309 billion yen at listing. That's a record, up sixty percent from last year, and six times higher than the long-run average. Why? Because Tokyo deliberately raised the bar. Last year, the exchange changed its rules: to list now, a company has to reach a hundred billion yen in market value within five years. The old rule was far gentler. In effect, Tokyo is squeezing out the tiny, premature IPOs and telling founders to stay private longer, grow up, and come to market as a serious business. And you can see it working — this year's big debuts include the ride-hailing app GO, worth over 1.5 trillion yen, and the autonomous-vehicle firm TierIV. Fewer companies, but bigger, sturdier ones.

Now to defense — and two stories that share a very large price tag. In both cases, the government is requesting roughly 400 billion yen.

The first is about the wires that carry information. Japan's Defense Ministry plans to build NTT's next-generation network — called IOWN, an all-photonics system that moves data using light — into the Self-Defense Forces' command and control by 2028. The promises here are extraordinary: over a hundred times more data capacity, delays cut to a fraction of today's, and power consumption slashed dramatically. Why does the military care? Because modern warfare is a race against the clock. Near-zero delay means AI can analyze a battlefield and help commanders decide in an instant — crucial for missile defense and contested airspace.

The second 400-billion-yen request is for Fukushima. The government and Tokyo Electric Power are forming a public-private consortium by year's end to send in what Nikkei calls "physical AI" — robots that can go where humans simply cannot. Inside reactors one, two, and three, radiation is lethal. So these robots will survey, collect data, and cut contaminated piping. This is the hardest part of the whole decommissioning, and the timeline is sobering: large-scale debris removal isn't planned until 2037. This is a decades-long undertaking, and machines will have to do what people can't.

That defense spending, though, is colliding with an uncomfortable reality. Nikkei reports that the prices of defense equipment have surged — F-35B fighters up thirty-five percent since 2023, P-1 patrol aircraft up fifty percent — driven by the weak yen and inflation. Which means Japan may end up buying fewer of the things it planned to buy.

And speaking of spending: Prime Minister Takaichi is leaning hard into price relief. She's been posting on X, and she's surprised her own coalition partners by extending gasoline subsidies indefinitely — while they'd hoped to phase them out. More broadly, there's a real shift underway. Japan's budget process, unchanged for twenty-seven years, is dropping its hard spending caps under a new "Strong and Prosperous Japan Investment Framework." The old ceilings are gone. This is a bet that markets will trust Japan to spend for growth without losing fiscal discipline. We'll see.

Overseas now, and three stories about power and control.

From Estonia, a stark warning. The commander of Estonia's defense force, Andrus Merilo, told Nikkei that Russia — even with its setbacks in Ukraine — is rebuilding and likely preparing new operations. The Baltic states, he says, face the imminent threat of hybrid attacks: drones, cyberattacks, infiltration. And he worries NATO could fracture if Russia tests its resolve with a small, limited land grab. His phrase stays with you: "We are no longer in peacetime, but not yet in war."

In Venezuela, President Trump has announced a deal giving U.S. firms control over more than sixty-five billion barrels of Venezuelan crude — potentially a fifth of the world's reserves. Venezuela's interim government is calling it a historic pact, touting a hundred billion dollars in investment. But experts urge caution: many of these deposits are abandoned, would need enormous infrastructure, and may require congressional approval and even Venezuelan constitutional changes. A grand announcement; a very long road to reality.

And in Hong Kong, a quiet but historic shift. On September sixteenth, Hong Kong will publish its very first five-year plan — the kind of centrally-planned framework you'd expect from Beijing, not from a free port. Remember, Hong Kong's Basic Law promised that socialism would not be practiced there, and capitalism would stay unchanged for fifty years. One expert in Nikkei put it vividly: Hong Kong is turning from a natural fishing ground into a fish farm.

A quick tour of business. China's EV giant BYD saw first-half profit fall twenty-one percent, with domestic sales cratering forty percent — even as overseas sales surged. In the U.S., the new Fed chair, Waller, struck a hawkish tone at Jackson Hole, and the dollar climbed to as much as 163 yen. And here's a notable Japanese pairing: Honda and Nissan have agreed to build a shared software platform for their next-generation vehicles by 2029 — pooling their scale to cut development costs in half and take on Tesla and China's software-first carmakers.

And now, the one that caught my eye — a story about watches, and about India.

Casio, Japan's watchmaker, is planning to expand its retail presence in India by seventy percent — to a hundred and thirty stores — and, for the first time, to build watches locally. Here's what I love about this: it's a window into how the world's supply chains are quietly rearranging themselves. India charges tariffs of over forty percent on imported watches, so Casio is choosing to make them there, aiming for local production to reach thirty percent of its Indian sales. It's even opening design centers to create watches for Indian tastes. And the prize is enormous — India's watch market has grown nearly threefold since 2015, to over 227 billion rupees. A Japanese company, building in India, designing for India, to beat Chinese rivals. That's the shape of the decade to come, told through a wristwatch.
