Let's get into it.

We begin with the story that leads the Nikkei this morning — and it's a big one. Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi announced yesterday that Japan will cut the consumption tax on food from eight percent all the way down to one percent. That reduction kicks in next April and runs for two years. And here's the clever part: that remaining one percent will be handed back to lower and middle-income households as cash benefits — so that, in the Prime Minister's words, the effective tax on food reaches zero.

Now, let me give you the context, because this matters. Japan's consumption tax is a sales tax you pay on almost everything, and it's been a political third rail for decades — governments have fallen over raising it. So cutting it, even temporarily, is a serious statement. Takaichi is framing this as relief from the cost-of-living squeeze that Japanese households have been feeling, and as a bridge to something more permanent — an income-linked benefit system she wants to launch in fiscal 2029.

But here's the catch, and it's a real one. She's promised the tax will snap back to eight percent after two years. And she's insisted the government won't fund this with deficit bonds — no new borrowing. The trouble is, nobody quite knows where the money is coming from. The specific funding sources remain unclear, and that has rattled market participants and stirred unease even within her own party. Cutting a tax is popular. Explaining how you'll pay for it — that's the hard part, and that answer is still missing.

And this fiscal boldness doesn't stop at food. The Nikkei reports that the government has scrapped its traditional spending ceilings for the 2027 budget. In Japanese these are called the "shīringu" — literally the ceiling — the caps that limit how much each ministry can request. Those caps have been part of the fiscal furniture since 1960, built to keep deficits in check. Now they're gone, at least for spending on artificial intelligence, semiconductors, defense, and crisis management. This is a genuine historic shift — a government stepping away from the deficit-control framework it built more than sixty years ago, and betting instead on growth and security spending.

It comes at an awkward moment, though. The Cabinet Office has just revised Japan's growth forecast downward — real GDP growth for fiscal 2026 now expected at just zero-point-nine percent, cut by four-tenths of a point. The reason? Higher oil prices, driven by tensions in the Middle East. And notably, that forecast doesn't even include the effect of the new food tax cut. So the fiscal picture is fluid.

Which brings us naturally to the yen — because the currency had a dramatic day. In New York trading, the yen surged to 157 to the dollar, a five-month high. But look at how it happened: a five-yen jump in just fifty minutes. That kind of sudden, sharp move has traders whispering one word — intervention. When a currency snaps that hard, that fast, it often bears the fingerprints of coordinated action by the government and the Bank of Japan. Nothing's confirmed, but the pattern is familiar, and markets noticed.

Now, some heavier news from home. The death toll from the Kumamoto earthquake has risen to thirty-four. This was a magnitude seven quake that struck Kumamoto Prefecture on Monday. Twenty-five of those deaths are now confirmed as directly earthquake-related. And there's a particularly tragic detail: seven people died at the Aeon Mall in Kumamoto, following an explosion at the shopping center. The government has set up an on-site disaster response headquarters — a hundred and twenty personnel, led by Deputy Minister Atsushi Tsushima. Our thoughts are with everyone in Kumamoto this morning.

Let's turn abroad, where the news is tense. The United States launched fresh strikes on Iranian facilities on the twenty-ninth, responding to an Iranian attack on a base in Jordan the day before. What's striking here is that both sides had recently signaled they wanted to de-escalate — and now they're back in direct combat. The Nikkei's reading is that Iran senses the Trump administration is constrained ahead of November's elections, and is escalating deliberately to strengthen its hand at the negotiating table. A dangerous calculation.

And in Europe, an incident that could carry enormous weight. Poland has confirmed that a Russian cruise missile — a Kh-101 — struck near its eastern border yesterday. If that holds up, it's the first documented strike on NATO territory. NATO and Polish forces are coordinating their response, and there's already speculation about whether this could trigger the alliance's collective defense provisions — the principle that an attack on one member is an attack on all. That's the kind of sentence that changes history, so watch this one very closely. On a more hopeful note, Ukraine's President Zelensky met Polish Prime Minister Tusk to mend a relationship strained by old historical disputes, and to restart stalled defense cooperation on aircraft and drones.

Now to business, and a milestone for Japan. After two failed rounds of bidding and some hard price negotiations, a consortium of Hitachi and Toshiba has shipped twelve trainsets — a hundred and forty-four cars — to Taiwan High Speed Rail, for a hundred and twenty-four billion yen. These are based on the N700S Shinkansen, and here's why it matters: this is the first-ever production export of Japanese bullet trains. Japan has long dreamed of selling its famous high-speed technology abroad, and this delivery could unlock years of service and modernization contracts to come.

Elsewhere in tech, a fascinating split at Samsung. The memory chip division just posted its best-ever quarterly profit, driven by that voracious demand for AI chips. But at the very same time, Samsung's smartphone division swung to its first loss since 2010 — undone by rising component costs. Two divisions, same company, wildly different fortunes. And closer to home, Sony has proposed acquiring the camera lens maker Tamron — Sony already owns about fifteen percent — aiming to cut costs and push into what's called physical AI. Tamron's shares jumped twenty-six percent on the news.

And now, the one that caught my eye — buried deeper in the paper, and quietly revealing. In Cambodia, the deep-water port at Sihanukville just posted a fifty-two percent surge in operating profit. Why? Because the border between Thailand and Cambodia has closed, and all that trade which used to move overland is now diverting through this single port. Container traffic jumped forty percent. A windfall.

But here's the twist. Japanese companies investing in Cambodia are frustrated — deeply so. The state-owned port operator charges above-market rates and slapped on punitive storage fees last year. With the overland routes to Thailand blocked indefinitely and no clear political will to reopen them, that inefficiency is biting. Mitsubishi Corporation has actually begun withdrawing its operations. So you have a port getting rich off a crisis, while the very companies it depends on quietly head for the exits — a reminder that a monopoly's good fortune and a country's economic health are not always the same thing.
