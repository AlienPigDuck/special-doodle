Let's begin with the story that runs underneath so much of what's happening in Japan's economy right now — and that's the yen.

The Nikkei reports this morning that Japanese households and companies are moving their money out of yen and into foreign currencies at a record pace. In just the second quarter of this year, foreign currency deposits jumped by four trillion yen. According to the Bank of Japan, the total now sits at 26.1 trillion yen held at domestic banks — an eighteen percent increase from a year ago. Individuals alone are holding 6.7 trillion yen in foreign currency. Companies, 19.3 trillion.

Now, why does this matter? Here's the thing that makes this so striking. Usually, in a currency crisis, you'd expect people to rush toward the safe thing. But what we're seeing here is the opposite. Japanese savers and businesses look at the weak yen, decide it's only going to get weaker, and move their money out — into dollars, into other currencies. And every time they do that, they sell yen. Which pushes the yen down further. Which convinces the next person to do the same thing.

It's a self-reinforcing cycle, and it's happening even after July's emergency intervention, when the government and the central bank stepped in to try to defend the currency. What this tells us is that ordinary people — not just hedge funds in London or New York, but families and firms right here in Japan — have stopped believing the yen will recover. And that's a much harder problem to solve, because you can't intervene your way out of a loss of confidence.

Keep this in the back of your mind, because it connects to a couple of the government's moves this week. The Finance Ministry and the Financial Services Agency are planning to propose tax breaks for individual government bonds in next year's budget — possibly inheritance tax exemptions, possibly making them eligible for tax-free investment accounts. Why? Because the Bank of Japan is unwinding its enormous holdings of government debt, and someone has to buy those bonds. Increasingly, the government is hoping that someone is you — the ordinary household investor. So there's a real tension here. The state wants people to hold yen assets, at the very moment people are voting with their wallets to do the opposite.

Let's move to the front page, where the theme is power — literally. Electricity.

Mitsubishi Electric has announced its largest acquisition ever: it's buying an American energy software company called PCI Energy Solutions for 1.4 billion dollars, about 220 billion yen. And here's the eye-catching detail — PCI's systems manage sixty percent of all electricity generation in the United States. As renewable energy spreads and power grids get more complicated, the software that manages all of it becomes enormously valuable. Mitsubishi Electric wants to double its smart energy business by 2030, and this is how it's buying its way in.

Alongside that, the trading company Itochu says it will invest several hundred billion yen through 2030 to build around ten data centers across Japan. These are big, fifty-megawatt facilities for the cloud giants and tech firms. And here's the clever part — Itochu is in talks with the railway operator JR East. Why a railway company? Because JR East owns land all over Japan, and it has power connections. And in Japan, the two things holding data centers back are exactly that: not enough space, and not enough power. So a trading house and a railway may turn out to be the perfect pair.

That same hunger for computing power runs through this morning's international pages, where the story is South Korea and artificial intelligence. Samsung Electronics has announced the largest shareholder return in Korean history — one hundred trillion won, about eleven trillion yen, in buybacks. Its chip rival SK Hynix announced a forty-trillion-won program of its own. Both companies are swimming in profits from AI-driven demand for semiconductors, and they're handing money back to shareholders partly to calm nerves about just how much they're spending on AI infrastructure.

And Hyundai — yes, the carmaker — is investing around a trillion yen to build something it's calling "Seomangeum AI Valley" in southwestern Korea. Fifty thousand Nvidia chips, hydrogen production, and factories to build robots. Hyundai is trying to become something more than a car company — a maker of what people are calling "physical AI." Put these together and you can see Korea placing an enormous national bet on the AI era.

Now, two sharper stories from abroad. In China, the founder of the property giant Evergrande, Hui Ka Yan, has been sentenced to life in prison for massive financial fraud, and all his personal assets have been confiscated. Evergrande, you'll remember, is the company whose collapse nearly took China's financial system down with it. This is Beijing drawing a very hard line under the property crisis.

And on the Korean peninsula, North Korea fired around ten short-range ballistic missiles on August twentieth. They fell outside Japan's economic waters, but the timing matters — the tests come as President Trump is openly signaling he'd like to negotiate, and as Seoul now estimates the North holds somewhere between eighty and a hundred and twenty nuclear warheads. A reminder, if we needed one, of how the diplomacy and the weapons move together.

Back home, a bit of good news for workers. Thirty-two of Japan's forty-seven prefectures have decided to put this year's minimum wage increases into effect in October. That may sound dull, but it's a real reversal. Last year, many of these hikes were pushed back — some as far as March — which meant companies got more time to prepare while workers waited months for the raise they'd been promised. That drew a lot of criticism. This year, after political pressure, the raises come sooner. For a lot of families, that's real money, on time.

And here's a sign of the times worth pausing on: ransomware. A survey from Trend Micro finds that nearly seventeen percent of major Japanese firms have now suffered ransomware losses of more than 500 million yen. Three years ago, that figure was under five percent. It's tripled. As companies digitize everything, criminals are going after the backups and the critical systems — and the damage is climbing fast.

A quick one before we close, because it made me smile. Unicharm — a company you might know for diapers — has launched premium frozen dog food. It's called Freschais, made with human-grade ingredients, and it sells for 498 yen for a fifty-gram pack. That's three times the price of ordinary dog food. Why? Because Japanese owners increasingly think of their dogs as family, and they're spending forty percent more on pet nutrition than they did five years ago. In a country with a shrinking human population, this is a real and growing market.

And now, the one that caught my eye — from deeper in the paper.

A Japanese shipbuilder called Meimura is building the country's first new large dry dock since 2017. And that quiet little fact tells a bigger story. For years, Japan's shipbuilding industry was in retreat, losing ground to China and South Korea. But the government now wants to double the nation's shipbuilding capacity — to eighteen million gross tons by 2035 — because in an anxious world, being able to build your own ships has become a matter of national security. Sumitomo Heavy Industries is ramping up its giant crane production by half. After years of decline, an old industry is being asked to come back — not for profit alone, but for strategy. When a country starts building docks again, it's telling you something about how it sees the future.
