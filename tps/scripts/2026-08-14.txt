We begin this morning with a story that goes right to the heart of one of Japan's oldest and most stubborn grievances. Russian President Vladimir Putin has visited Etorofu Island — one of the four islands Japan calls the Northern Territories. It's his first such visit as president, and Nikkei reports that he toured fish processing plants, hospitals, and schools. Now, if you've never followed this dispute, here's what you need to know. These four islands, off the northeastern tip of Hokkaido, were seized by the Soviet Union in the final days of the Second World War. Japan has claimed them ever since. To this day, Tokyo and Moscow have never signed a formal peace treaty ending that war — because of these islands.

So when Putin walks around Etorofu inspecting a school, he's not just visiting. He's making a statement — that Russia controls these islands, and that Russia intends to keep them. Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi called the visit "absolutely unacceptable." She warned it will deepen anti-Russian feeling in Japan and set back any hope of repairing relations for years to come. And here's the wider context: with the war in Ukraine still grinding on, and Western sanctions squeezing Moscow, Putin has no interest in compromise. He's signaling he'll give Japan nothing. For Tokyo, it's a cold reminder that the door on this decades-old question has, for now, been firmly shut.

Let's move from geopolitics to something closer to home — quite literally, the corner shop. Nikkei has a striking front-page piece this morning: the mobile phone store is vanishing from rural Japan. The three big carriers — NTT Docomo, KDDI, and SoftBank — now have no retail presence at all in more than forty percent of Japan's municipalities. That's around 750 out of roughly 1,800 towns and cities. Store numbers peaked at 11,500 back in 2013; they've fallen to about 9,900, and they're expected to keep dropping.

Why does this matter? Think about who relies on a physical shop to set up a phone, or to get help when something goes wrong. It's often older people — and rural Japan is aging fast. So the closures widen what's called the digital divide, the gap between those comfortable online and those left behind. To their credit, the carriers aren't simply walking away. They're experimenting with new roles — smartphone classes, disaster support — trying to stay useful in communities where they no longer sell many devices. Because that's the real driver here: people keep their phones longer, and sales have slumped.

Staying with the economy, two other front-page items caught my attention. First, Risona Holdings is launching what's called cloud factoring — and let me unpack that. Factoring means a business gets cash now against invoices it's owed but hasn't been paid for yet. Risona plans to use AI to approve these short-term loans — typically between 500,000 yen and 10 million yen — on the same day, instead of the usual two weeks. For a small business waiting on a customer to pay, that speed can be the difference between survival and going under. Risona is working with a firm called OLTA and reaching out through regional banks, hoping to serve nearly three million eligible businesses.

And second — a rare piece of good news on Japan's birth rate. In the first half of 2026, births topped the same period a year earlier — for the first time in eleven years. Just over 282,000 babies. That upturn seems to be driven by rising marriages, which have now increased two years running. But let's keep our feet on the ground. The total is still at what Nikkei calls wartime lows — 2025 saw fewer than 672,000 births, down more than two percent from the year before. Economists caution that lingering financial anxiety is still holding families back. So: a flicker, not a turnaround.

Now to the government's own agenda, and it is ambitious. The Takaichi administration has pledged 68 trillion yen in public-private investment by 2040, spread across 17 strategic sectors. And one-sixth of that — a full sixth — is going to AI semiconductors. At the center of this is Rapidus, Japan's flagship chipmaker, which will receive 2.4 trillion yen in research subsidies through this year. The language here is telling: the government talks about rebuilding a "sovereign industrial base." In plain terms, Japan doesn't want to depend on anyone else for the most advanced chips. It's watching South Korea pour some 90 trillion yen into chips and Taiwan run the world's most advanced factories — and it doesn't want to be left behind.

Alongside that industrial push, Nikkei reports Takaichi is preparing a party reshuffle in October, as leadership terms expire. The name to watch is Deputy Prime Minister Taro Asoh — a veteran figure who helped Takaichi rise to power, and who's expected to keep a strong hand in the appointments. That said, her position isn't unassailable. Her cabinet's internal support rate slipped to 58 percent in July, down ten points in a single month. When a leader's numbers fall, so does her room to maneuver.

Let's cross overseas, and here's a story that says a lot about where the car industry is heading. In Australia, China's BYD has all but caught Toyota. Back in late 2022, BYD sold zero cars there. This past June, it sold 19,000 — nearly matching Toyota's 19,243. What happened? Fuel prices jumped sharply — from around 190 yen a liter to 247. The government offered incentives for electric vehicles. And EVs went from about seven percent of the market to nearly 22 percent. Toyota has held its ground at 20 percent market share, but this is a genuine shot across the bow — after 23 years at the top, its dominance is being tested by an affordable Chinese newcomer.

Two quick notes from the wider world. In the Middle East, Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and Pakistan have signed a mutual defense pact in Mecca — a NATO-style agreement where an attack on one is treated as an attack on all. Nikkei frames it as part of a broader "bloc formation" trend, driven by worries that the United States is stepping back from the region. And in Britain, Prime Minister Barnum has unveiled a historic plan to hand income tax and business rates to regional authorities by spring 2027 — reversing decades of power concentrated in London. Interestingly, Nikkei notes this is exactly the kind of decentralization Japan tried, and struggled with, in its own "Trinity Reform."

And that brings me to the one that caught my eye this morning — and it's about money, specifically the price of brilliance. A Japanese real estate developer called Kasumigaseki Capital is offering roughly 14 million yen a year to about ten exceptional new graduates joining next spring. That's a monthly base salary of one million yen for people fresh out of university. And who are these hires? Nikkei lists Cambridge University students, mathematical modelers, fintech competition winners, AI developers. This matters because Japan has long been known for flat, seniority-based pay, where everyone starts at roughly the same rung. A package like this breaks that mold entirely. It's a sign that Japanese firms have realized the "superstar" talent they need is being aggressively poached by tech companies abroad — and that if they want to keep it, they'll have to pay global prices. Whether the rest of corporate Japan follows suit is one of the quiet, fascinating questions to watch.
