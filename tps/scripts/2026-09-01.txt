Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Tuesday, the first of September, 2026, and there's a single number at the heart of today's paper that tells you almost everything you need to know about where this government is heading. That number is one hundred and forty-three trillion yen.

That's the size of Japan's budget request for the coming fiscal year — the wish list government agencies submit before the spending is finalized. And it is a record. To give you a sense of the jump, last year's request came in around a hundred and twenty-two trillion. So we're talking about an increase of more than twenty trillion yen in a single year. That doesn't happen by accident.

Here's what's behind it. Under normal circumstances, Japan's ministries operate under something called a spending ceiling — a cap on how much they can even ask for. Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi's administration has removed that ceiling for investments tied to growth and crisis management. Nikkei reports the government is framing this as "responsible aggressive fiscal policy" — the idea being that Japan should spend boldly now to drive economic growth later.

But here's the tension, and it's a real one. The moment that request landed, financial markets got nervous. The yield on Japan's ten-year government bond rose to two point nine five percent — a thirty-year high. When bond yields climb like that, it's a signal that investors are worried about two things: whether Japan can sustain all this debt over the long term, and whether the Bank of Japan may have to raise interest rates. And there's a further wrinkle. A lot of this request is made up of what the Japanese call "unspecified items" — categories where agencies haven't yet named a figure. Which means the real number could climb even higher.

You can see this playing out in the Defense Ministry's request — nearly nine trillion yen, but loaded with those unspecified items for things like unmanned aircraft, long-range missiles, and AI-enhanced systems. Nikkei suggests the final defense figure could push past ten trillion once those blanks are filled in. So the headline number, as big as it is, may understate what's coming.

Now, at the same time this government is spending aggressively, it's also cutting taxes. There's a plan to slash the consumption tax on food to just one percent for two years, starting in April of 2027, with income-linked cash support to follow. That's meant to ease the pressure of inflation on households. But it raises the obvious question — if you're spending more and taxing less, where does the money come from? The government says it'll offset the lost revenue through treasury transfers to local governments, but Nikkei is clear that the fiscal mechanics are still being worked out.

And there's a small, telling detail here that speaks to the mood. The Takaichi administration is backing what's being called a "Japan version DOGE" — a review aimed at cutting government waste. One of its first targets is a gift tax break for wedding and childbirth funds, which is being eliminated. The thing is, only about two hundred and nineteen households used that measure each year. So this is symbolic more than anything — a signal of fiscal discipline running right alongside all that aggressive spending. Discipline for show, expansion for real. It's a delicate balancing act, and the bond market is watching closely.

Let me turn to something that shows the other side of Japan's economy — the money flowing in. Nikkei's front page reports that Japan GLP, a major logistics operator, is setting up the country's largest-ever real estate fund focused on logistics — warehouses, distribution centers, that kind of infrastructure. It's raised around six hundred billion yen, and with borrowing, the total deployment reaches one point seven trillion.

What's striking is who's putting up the money. Foreign investors are at the center of it — Canada's pension fund alone is investing a hundred and fifty billion yen, alongside Gulf state sovereign funds and pensions from across Asia, Europe, and North America. The fund plans to build thirty to forty large facilities across Tokyo, Nagoya, and Osaka. And this tells you something important: while markets fret about Japan's debt, global investors see Japan's logistics sector — warehousing, data centers, the physical backbone of e-commerce and AI — as a stable, long-term bet. In an uncertain world, they want steady yield, and Japan is offering it.

Not every business story is about expansion, though. House Food Group is looking to sell its stake in Coco Ichibanya — and if that name isn't familiar, Coco Ichibanya, or "CoCo Ichi," is Japan's beloved curry chain, the kind of place you find in train stations and suburbs alike. House Food took it under its wing back in 2015, hoping the two could grow together — better ingredient buying, joint overseas expansion, especially in China. The dream was to export Japanese curry culture to the world.

It didn't quite work out. Rising inflation forced repeated price hikes, customers thinned out, and the synergy never materialized. Now investment funds are circling, hoping to take the chain private, and Coco Ichibanya's stock jumped nearly sixteen percent on the news. It's a small story, but it captures a bigger corporate mood in Japan right now — a retreat from grand ambitions toward capital efficiency.

On the trade front, there's an interesting bit of positioning. The Japanese government wants to host the permanent secretariat for the CPTPP — that's the Pacific trade pact — right here in Japan. It'll formally propose this at a ministers' meeting in November, with funding already tucked into that budget request. The strategy is clear: as the Trump administration pulls the United States away from multilateral trade, Tokyo wants to plant itself firmly at the center of regional economic cooperation. Japan as the hub, not Washington.

Speaking of the wider world — the Shanghai Cooperation Organization summit brought Xi Jinping and Putin together in Kyrgyzstan, deepening their alignment just weeks before Xi is scheduled to visit Washington on the twenty-fourth of September. And in a sign of the times, Nikkei reports the U.S. nuclear weapons budget is swelling to four point three trillion yen for 2027 — roughly triple what it was a decade ago. That's the Trump administration accelerating tactical nuclear development to counter China, Russia, and North Korea. It marks a genuine break from the post-Cold War instinct toward disarmament — a return, in a sense, to an older logic.

A couple of quicker notes. In Europe, Iceland has voted against reopening EU membership talks — fifty-two point eight percent said no. Iceland has long thrived outside the union through its trade arrangements, and populists across Europe are already holding it up as proof that sovereignty and prosperity can coexist. And on China's economy, its manufacturing activity contracted for a second straight month, with youth unemployment among sixteen-to-twenty-four-year-olds hitting seventeen point nine percent in July. Compare that to India, which just posted seven point eight percent growth. Two very different trajectories in Asia right now.

And now, the one that caught my eye — tucked into the international pages. Russia and China have been negotiating a major new gas pipeline, "Siberia 2," designed to carry fifty billion cubic meters of gas a year to China. And it's stalled. Why? Because Beijing won't agree to Moscow's pricing. Russia, cut off from European markets after Ukraine, badly needs Chinese buyers — and China knows it. So Beijing is simply refusing to commit beyond its existing contracts, letting Russia wait.

What I find fascinating is what this reveals about that much-touted "no limits" partnership. On paper, Xi and Putin are the closest of allies. But when it comes to gas prices, China holds all the cards, and it's using them. Dependence, it turns out, isn't friendship. The stronger partner sets the terms — even among friends.
