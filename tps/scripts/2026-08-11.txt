Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Tuesday, the eleventh of August, 2026, and I'm glad you're here.

We begin this morning in Kumamoto, on the southern island of Kyushu — and if that place name rings a bell, it might be for two very different reasons. Just last month, Kumamoto was hit by an earthquake. But over the past few years, it's also quietly become the heart of Japan's semiconductor revival. And this morning, the Nikkei leads with a story that ties both of those threads together.

Sony and TSMC — that's the giant Taiwanese chipmaker, Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company — are joining forces to build next-generation image sensors in Kumamoto. The combined investment is enormous: around one trillion yen. They'll set up a joint venture this fiscal year, with Sony holding sixty percent and TSMC forty, and they aim to be mass-producing by 2029.

Now, why does this matter? A couple of reasons. First, image sensors are one of the things Japan genuinely leads the world in. If you've ever taken a photo on an iPhone, there's a good chance a Sony sensor captured that light. And that's exactly where these new chips will go first — into Apple's iPhones — before the companies look toward AI-driven robotics and self-driving cars.

But here's the shift the Nikkei highlights. Until now, Sony designed the sensors and simply handed the manufacturing off to TSMC. This new venture brings them together, side by side, in Japan. That's a much deeper partnership. And notably, both companies are pressing ahead in Kumamoto even after last month's earthquake — a signal of long-term confidence in the region, tremors and all.

That story connects to something bigger on the front page. Japanese companies are having a remarkable moment. Net profit for firms on the Tokyo Stock Exchange's Prime section jumped sixty-eight percent in the April-to-June quarter, with sales up fourteen percent. Profitability, the Nikkei says, is at its strongest since the 2008 financial crisis. Two engines are driving this: a weak yen, which flatters overseas earnings, and a global stampede of investment into artificial intelligence.

Here's a number that really captures the moment. Six of Japan's big semiconductor-related companies together made 1.24 trillion yen in net profit — that's nearly as much as Toyota, all by itself, at 1.48 trillion. Let that sink in. Chips are catching up to cars. And the benefits are spreading — to component makers like Murata, whose capacitors go into the data centers powering AI, and out into the automotive and industrial sectors too.

There's a related story with a familiar name attached. Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway invested in Japan's five great trading houses — Mitsubishi, Itochu, Mitsui, Sumitomo, and Marubeni — seven years ago. Since then, their combined market value has multiplied roughly fivefold. And now Berkshire's new CEO, Greg Abel, will make his first visit to Tokyo as chief executive in September. These trading companies, the Nikkei notes, have reinvented themselves — less about shipping raw materials, more like portfolio managers, pruning weak businesses and using their global reach. Buffett saw that early.

Not everyone's sharing in the boom, though. The Financial Services Agency is planning to loosen the rules for non-bank lenders in 2027, to get more money flowing to startups. And the problem here is stark. Startup lending in Japan came to about 220 billion yen last year — less than a tenth of what it once was. So the regulator wants to cut, or scrap, the current one-billion-yen minimum capital requirement, and ease staffing rules too. The hope is a healthier ecosystem for young companies.

Let's turn to politics. The Nikkei ran a rather clever piece of analysis this morning — 286 days into Prime Minister Takaichi's administration, they went through her schedule to see who she's actually meeting. And the finding is telling. Individual company executives make up just nine percent of her non-government contacts. For her predecessors, that figure ran anywhere from fifteen to twenty-three percent. Instead, she leans heavily on industry associations — group representatives rather than individual CEOs. Whether that reflects style or substance, it hints at a more distant relationship with the private sector.

On policy, Takaichi has approved disaster assistance of up to three million yen per household for Kumamoto earthquake victims, including help rebuilding homes and stabilizing jobs. And she reaffirmed her signature economic pledge: a one-percent cut in the consumption tax on food, paired with cash transfers, starting April of next year.

That promise sits against a sobering backdrop. Japan's national debt hit a record 1.346 quadrillion yen as of June. That's quadrillion, with a q. It grew by nearly three trillion yen since March. And it feeds a real anxiety in the bond market — more on that shortly.

Abroad, a few developments worth your attention. Thailand has, for the first time in seven years, explicitly backed China's position on unification with Taiwan, in a joint statement. And the Nikkei frames this as part of a broader Southeast Asian drift toward Beijing — Vietnam and Myanmar echoing similar language — as China leans on economic carrots and diplomatic pressure.

In the Middle East, Iran has named a hardliner, Mohsen Rezaei, a former Revolutionary Guard commander, to head its Supreme National Security Council. And Iran has issued six conditions for access to the Strait of Hormuz — including a U.S. military withdrawal, sanctions relief, and reparations. These are terms the Trump administration is very unlikely to accept, especially before November's elections. So: rising tension in a waterway that carries much of the world's oil.

And a natural disaster to note. A magnitude 7.4 earthquake struck western Colombia, killing at least twenty people, with hundreds injured and heavy damage in cities like Pereira and Manizales. Our thoughts are with them.

Back to markets, briefly — and here's the thread that runs back to that record debt. Japan's two-year government bond yield has climbed to 1.62 percent, a thirty-one-year high. The market is now pricing a two-in-three chance that the Bank of Japan raises rates in September. After decades of near-zero interest rates, that's a genuinely significant shift. Meanwhile, gold hit a two-month high, around 4,371 dollars an ounce, as central banks — China's especially — keep stockpiling it.

A quick word on a couple of corporate stories, because they point to the future. NEC has created what may be the first corporate department staffed entirely by AI — seventeen AI "agents" holding management positions, with humans overseeing their decisions. And the consulting world is bracing itself. Executives at both Accenture and Deloitte in Japan told the Nikkei that old-style consulting — gathering information, writing reports — is becoming obsolete as AI does that work in seconds. They're predicting the market could peak around 2030. A striking case of an industry warning about its own reinvention.

And now, the one that caught my eye — buried deeper in the paper, but I think it's the most quietly important story of the day.

Japan just recorded a current account surplus of 17.4 trillion yen. That's a record. By all the old rules, that ought to lift the yen. Instead, the yen fell — about ten yen against the dollar in the first half of the year. Why?

Because the money isn't coming home. Japanese companies keep about forty percent of their overseas profits invested abroad, chasing better returns in faster-growing economies. And foreign investment into Japan? Less than a third of what flows out. So even a record surplus can't prop up the currency.

What the Nikkei is pointing to here is profound. It suggests that Japan, even with a shrinking, aging population, has become a permanent exporter of capital — its own companies quietly voting for opportunities elsewhere. And it means the usual tools of monetary policy may not work the way the textbooks say. That's a structural story, not a headline one. But it may explain more about Japan's future than anything else in today's paper.
