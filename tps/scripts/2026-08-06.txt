Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Thursday, the sixth of August, 2026, and today the Nikkei is dominated by a single, remarkable decision — a tax cut that undoes three decades of Japanese fiscal orthodoxy.

Let's start there.

Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi's government has approved something Japan has never done before. It's going to cut the consumption tax on food. Not raise it — cut it. The rate on groceries is dropping from eight percent all the way down to one percent, and it takes effect next April, in 2027, for a two-year window. Alcohol and eating out are excluded — this is about the food you bring home. And the Nikkei says the typical household stands to save around 360,000 yen a year. That's real money, at a time when the cost of living has been squeezing Japanese families hard.

Now, to understand why this is such a big deal, you have to know the history. Japan introduced its consumption tax — its version of a sales tax — back in 1989, at three percent. And ever since, the story has been one direction only: up. Three percent, then five, then eight, then ten. Every increase was fought over, every increase was justified the same way — Japan is aging, someone has to pay for the pensions and the healthcare, and the consumption tax was the pillar holding it all up. So for a government to cut it, for the first time ever, is a genuine reversal.

And here's the catch. Nobody's entirely sure how it will be paid for. The Nikkei puts the fiscal hole at around ten trillion yen. The government is reportedly eyeing surplus funds from something called the Foreign Exchange Special Account — essentially the pot of money the state uses to manage the yen — but no firm source has been confirmed. Ten trillion yen, and the funding is still a question mark.

The Nikkei's own editorial board is not impressed. Their editorial this morning carries the headline "Can Aging Japan Overcome This?" And the argument is pointed. They say Takaichi is undermining social security financing at the exact moment Japan faces peak spending on its aging population. And they single out the Liberal Democratic Party — Takaichi's own party — for abandoning its long-held position. For thirty years the LDP defended the consumption tax as essential. Now, the editorial says, they've traded that long-term responsibility for a short-term political win.

And there was a fight to get here. The Nikkei reports that Takaichi pushed this through a Cabinet meeting on the thirtieth of July, overriding the conservative wing of her own party — the faction led by Deputy Prime Minister Taro Aso. Her weapon was the election pledge. She'd promised this to voters, and she used that mandate to silence the internal dissent. So this is Takaichi flexing her authority, and betting that relief at the checkout counter matters more to voters than warnings about the pension system a decade from now.

Now, is that squeeze on households actually easing? The wage data offers a mixed answer. Real wages — that's pay adjusted for inflation — rose 1.6 percent in June, the sixth straight month of gains. Summer bonuses hit a record, averaging over a million yen. That's genuinely good news. But the Nikkei adds a caveat worth hearing: some of that improvement is because food price inflation has cooled, which flatters the numbers. And energy prices are expected to climb this autumn. So the recovery is real, but it's fragile.

And here's a striking companion figure. Household financial assets have hit an all-time high — the Nikkei reports 2.4 trillion yen — with families holding more in stocks and investment funds than at any point since the 1990s bubble. But look closer and you see a divide. The median household holds 7.2 million yen. The average is 19.4 million. When the average is nearly three times the median, that tells you the wealth is concentrated at the top. The rising markets are rewarding asset-rich retirees. Working-age families with mortgages are watching their interest burdens go up instead. So a booming market, but the gains are not being shared.

Let's turn abroad, because there's a fascinating thread from Washington. US Treasury Secretary Janet Yellen gave the Nikkei an interview, and she said something blunt about the yen. She said recent coordinated yen-buying intervention was driven by fear — fear that a rapidly weakening yen would set off a chain reaction, competitive currency devaluations across Asia. She reached back to the 1990s Asian financial crisis, when a weak yen helped accelerate regional turmoil. And she went further, declaring that in the view of the Trump administration, Japan's "Abenomics phase has ended" — a reference to the era of ultra-loose money under the late Shinzo Abe — and she urged Japan to normalize its monetary policy. That's a foreign finance minister telling Japan, quite directly, that an era is over.

Yellen also featured in another story. She said a deal to reopen the Strait of Hormuz — that vital oil chokepoint at the mouth of the Persian Gulf — could be reached "today or tomorrow." The plan involves 60-day provisional measures, with Iran and Oman managing separate shipping lanes. Though the Nikkei notes both Washington and Tehran dispute how final any of this really is.

Two more from overseas, quickly. In Europe, extreme heat is doing extraordinary damage. The Danube River has fallen to record low levels. Romania's only nuclear reactor is running at half capacity. Hungary's sole nuclear plant may face its first full shutdown since 1982. And automakers — including Ford-Otosan and Dacia — have halted production through mid-August. The Nikkei puts the losses from heat and wildfires across five Eurozone countries at 31 billion euros. A reminder of how directly the climate now touches the economy.

And in Australian politics, a jolt. Pauline Hanson's right-wing One Nation party has doubled its support to 30 percent, now neck-and-neck with the governing Labor Party, riding a backlash over immigration — some 570,000 arrivals a year, up 22 percent over a decade.

Closer to home, two corporate stories tied to Japan's own disaster. That magnitude-7.0 Kumamoto earthquake on the twenty-eighth of July is still reverberating. The Finance Ministry has actually suspended its economic outlook for the whole Kyushu region while it assesses the damage. And Nippon Paper — Japan's largest paper producer — has halted its Yashiro plant indefinitely after chimneys collapsed in the quake, killing nine workers. The company has pulled its earnings guidance entirely. And here's a detail that stopped me: that plant supplies half of Kyushu's newspaper paper. So a disaster is threatening the physical supply of the papers themselves.

On a brighter note for Japanese industry, Honda has sharply raised its profit forecast — to 650 billion yen, up from an initial 260 — thanks largely to the weak yen. But buried in that good news is a warning: Honda's sales in China collapsed 47 percent in the second quarter. The forex tailwind is masking a real problem in the world's biggest car market.

And that brings me to the one that caught my eye — and it's about the man who never leaves the headlines for long. SpaceX, Elon Musk's company, fresh from its June IPO, just posted a second-quarter loss of 541 million dollars. Now, revenue grew 92 percent — nearly doubled — to 7.8 billion dollars. That's not the problem. The problem is spending. Capital expenditure hit 18.3 billion dollars in a single quarter. That's 2.3 times the company's entire revenue. Musk is pouring money into AI data centers and chip manufacturing, and he's promising 1 trillion dollars in sales by 2030 and 27,000 robotaxis by 2027. Meanwhile, today — the sixth of August — the IPO lockup expires, letting insiders start selling. The shares have already fallen 40 percent from their June highs. A company spending more than twice what it earns, on a promise. It's a bet on the future, quite literally, at the speed of a rocket.
