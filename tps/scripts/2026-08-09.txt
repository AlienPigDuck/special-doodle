Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Sunday, the ninth of August, 2026, and I'm glad you're here.

There's a thread running through today's paper — from the front page all the way to the markets pages — and it's about how fragile the world's supply lines have become. Money, minerals, missiles. Everything, it seems, is being stockpiled against the day it can't be had. So let's start there.

Our lead story this morning is about Japan's three biggest banks — Mitsubishi UFJ, Mizuho, and Sumitomo Mitsui. According to the Nikkei, they've quietly built up an enormous war chest of foreign currency. As of June, they were holding around 198 trillion yen in dollar and other foreign-currency liquidity. That's about 1.25 trillion dollars — and it's up eighteen percent in just three months. It's the largest such accumulation in more than a decade.

Now, why would banks do this? The short answer is fear about dollars. When tensions flare in the Middle East — and right now the United States and Iran are in open military conflict — global markets get nervous, and dollars can suddenly become hard to get. Japanese companies do enormous business in dollars: they import oil, they pay overseas suppliers, they run factories abroad. If the dollar taps tighten, a Japanese firm could find itself unable to pay its bills even while it's perfectly healthy. So the banks are getting ahead of that. Mitsubishi UFJ alone boosted its reserves by thirty-two percent, to nearly forty-seven billion dollars. And Nikkei reports that corporate demand for emergency credit lines has jumped six trillion yen since January. In plain terms: Japan Inc. is asking its banks to promise the money will be there in a crisis, and the banks are stocking the shelves.

And that same anxiety about scarcity — the fear of not being able to get what you need — shows up in an even sharper form when we turn to China and rare earths.

Back in January, Beijing put export restrictions on seven rare-earth elements — things with names like dysprosium, terbium, and yttrium. These aren't household words, but they are essential to the tiny, powerful magnets inside electric-car motors. And the numbers this morning are striking. Nikkei reports that rare-earth exports to Japan fell fifty-one percent in the first half of the year. Compare that to the United States, which saw only a fourteen percent cut. Japan is being singled out. In June, shipments were down eighty-one percent from a year earlier, and exports of dysprosium and terbium to Japan have been at zero since January. Zero.

Why Japan, specifically? The paper connects it to Prime Minister Takaichi's statements in the Diet about planning for a possible Taiwan contingency — comments Beijing did not appreciate. So this is economic pressure with a political point behind it, and it's landing directly on Japan's automakers, who need those magnets to build electric motors.

If you want to know where this road leads, look at gallium. Three years ago, in August 2023, China banned exports of gallium and germanium. This morning the Nikkei reports gallium prices have risen ninefold — to over three thousand dollars a kilogram. China controls very nearly all of the world's production. Mitsubishi Corporation has managed to line up some supply from Kazakhstan, but there's simply no easy substitute. It's a preview of what dependence really costs.

And there's a fourth kind of shortage in the paper today, and it's a sobering one. The United States, the Nikkei reports, is running low on missiles. After weeks of escalating clashes with Iran, American Patriot air-defense stocks have fallen from roughly 2,330 units before the conflict to somewhere between 759 and 827 by late July. That's about a third of what they started with. Tomahawk cruise missile inventories have been cut in half. President Trump has acknowledged the shortage and authorized emergency production, but you can't manufacture these things overnight. And the ripple effects are real — depleted American stockpiles mean less to spare for Ukraine, and less for deterrence here in the Indo-Pacific. When we talk about supply chains, we usually mean gadgets and groceries. But it turns out even deterrence has a supply chain, and it can run dry.

Let me bring us home, though, because the front page has a very Japanese story about a very universal problem — heat.

Japan is warming, and its rice is suffering for it. Nikkei reports that heat-resistant rice varieties now cover just eighteen percent of the country's rice acreage. That's tripled over a decade, which sounds like progress — but the adoption is wildly uneven. In eastern producers like Akita and Iwate, uptake is below one percent. Miyagi is at nine. Farmers there are attached to their famous brands — Koshihikari and Akitakomachi — names consumers know and pay for. But here's the stark math from the record heat of 2023: in Niigata, only four percent of the beloved Koshihikari crop reached top grade. The heat-resistant variety, Shinnosuke? Ninety-five percent. That's the difference between a good year and a ruined one, and it's why the paper worries about a repeat of the rice shortages that hit Japan in 2024 and 2025.

On the politics pages, Prime Minister Takaichi's plan to cut the consumption tax on food is drawing a note of caution. Starting in April 2027, for two years, she wants to reduce the tax — at a cost of around five trillion yen a year. The Nikkei points to history here: past temporary tax cuts, like Prime Minister Hashimoto's in 1998, ended in political confusion and, eventually, deficit bonds to fill the hole. The paper calls it "see now, pay later," and it's watching closely.

Also in politics, a fascinating little item: Japan launched a National Intelligence Bureau last month, and Britain's former MI6 chief Richard Moore has given it a public endorsement. He called Japan's expertise on China, North Korea, and the Middle East genuinely valuable — but he offered a word of advice. Don't become obsessed with joining "Five Eyes," the intelligence-sharing club of the US, UK, Canada, Australia and New Zealand. Intelligence, Moore said, is an equal exchange. Japan brings its own to the table, membership card or not.

A quick turn to business. Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway made its first net stock purchase in fifteen quarters — nearly twenty billion dollars in the second quarter — with a ten-billion-dollar bet on Alphabet, Google's parent, and its AI infrastructure. Chevron, meanwhile, dropped out of Berkshire's top five holdings. Read into that what you will about where the smart money thinks the future is.

And closer to the ground, a genuinely hopeful story: Toyoda Gosei, a Toyota supplier near Nagoya, has opened Japan's first full-scale line for recycling automotive plastics. Old bumpers and trim, sourced from local demolition specialists, are melted down at two hundred degrees and turned back into new, virgin-quality parts. With rules coming that will require up to twenty percent recycled content by 2040, this is the circular economy, quietly becoming real.

And now, the one that caught my eye — tucked into the front page, and I love it.

A renewable-energy company called Looop is going to give electricity away for free. Between seven and nine in the evening, two hundred trial customers will pay nothing for their power — just a three-hundred-yen monthly fee to take part. How? Looop stores its own solar energy in batteries during the day and releases it at night. The trial runs into 2027, with a wider rollout to follow.

Think about what that inverts. For a century, electricity was cheapest in the dead of night, when nobody wanted it. Solar and batteries are turning that logic upside down — making evening power, the power we actually use, the free part. It's a small trial. But it's a glimpse of a genuinely different energy world.
