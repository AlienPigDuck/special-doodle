We begin today with a story that lands uncomfortably close to home for anyone who's ever handed their personal details to a company — which is to say, all of us. According to the Nikkei this morning, Japan is in the middle of a surge in data breaches. Since September, more than a hundred and thirty companies have discovered that personal information may have leaked out through unauthorized access. And the scale is staggering. At the high end, the exposed data adds up to something like two hundred and six million records — more than the entire population of Japan.

Now, what's driving this isn't a single break-in. It's a new generation of attacks, more sophisticated than what came before. The Nikkei points to chained attacks that exploit vulnerabilities in PHP — that's one of the most common programming languages used to build websites — and, more worryingly, attackers are now using artificial intelligence to make these intrusions smarter and faster.

But here's the detail that really deserves our attention. Investigators found that many companies were still holding onto personal data long after they had any reason to. They'd finished using it for its original purpose, and they simply… kept it. Under Japan's personal information protection law, companies have a duty to make an effort to delete data they no longer need. The key phrase there is "make an effort." It's not a hard requirement — it's an aspiration. And this wave of breaches is raising a pointed question: does a rule with no teeth actually protect anyone? When data sits unused on a server for years, it's not an asset. It's a liability waiting to leak.

Let's turn now to the diplomacy, because there's a remarkable scene unfolding in southern China. The APEC leaders' summit has opened in Shenzhen, and Nikkei reports that Chinese President Xi Jinping personally met President Trump at the airport. He also sat down with Russia's Vladimir Putin, a very deliberate show of unity between Beijing and Moscow. And today, the nineteenth, a three-way summit between the United States, China, and Russia is expected to take place.

Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi was there too, and she met Putin face to face. She used that meeting to press two points directly. First, she demanded the immediate release of a Hokkaido Shimbun journalist being held by Russia. And second, she urged Moscow to withdraw administrative measures it's imposed on the local operations of Japan Tobacco.

As for China — well, Takaichi and Xi did shake hands. But that was essentially it. No substantive meeting took place, just that handshake, and Nikkei reads this as further evidence that relations between Tokyo and Beijing remain frozen. A handshake can mean warmth. It can also be the polite minimum two leaders perform when there's nothing else to say.

Staying with foreign influence, the Takaichi government has unveiled plans for a new "foreign agent" registration system. Anyone lobbying on behalf of a foreign government — contacting politicians, running information campaigns, or handing over financial benefits — would be required to register with the National Public Safety Commission. Break the rules, and there are penalties. The Nikkei notes this mirrors systems already in place in the United States and Australia, and the government frames it plainly as a way to shed Japan's reputation as a "spy's paradise." The bill is aimed at the ordinary Diet session in 2027.

Now, the government is also reaching deep into your grocery bill. Nikkei reports that officials are preparing to cut the consumption tax on food from eight percent all the way down to one percent. That's a dramatic move. It's planned for a limited two-year window starting in April of 2027, paired with a new income-linked benefit that rolls out first. Under a proposal shown to the Komeito party, households would need to earn at least around five hundred and sixty thousand yen a year to qualify, and a family of four could receive up to sixty thousand yen annually. The catch: when the rate returns to eight percent in the spring of 2029, the government will have to decide how large a benefit to offer to soften that blow. Cutting taxes is the easy part. Putting them back is where the politics gets hard.

And in a story about Japan's geography of power, the government has narrowed its shortlist for a so-called "second capital" to four regions: Hokkaido, Aichi, Osaka, and Fukuoka. The idea is to ease Tokyo's overwhelming dominance — too many people, too much economic weight, all concentrated in one place. The second capital would serve two purposes: anchoring a more spread-out economy, and backing up the functions of government if disaster struck the capital. A designation is expected before the end of this year, though Nikkei notes real questions remain about how this differs from existing special economic zones, and whether it could genuinely stand in for Tokyo in an emergency.

On defense spending, there's a notable gap opening up. The government is now discussing a five-year budget from fiscal 2027 somewhere in the fifty to sixty trillion yen range. That's well below the seventy to eighty trillion the Defense Ministry had been hoping for — and the defense industry is uneasy about the shortfall.

The Democrats have retaken both chambers of Congress — fifty-six Senate seats and more than two hundred fifty-seven in the House. For Trump's Republicans, this is a heavy defeat, and Nikkei suggests it may accelerate a kind of internal "purification" within the party.

And now, the one that caught my eye this morning. The broadcaster NHK has drawn up a formal code of conduct for drinking at work gatherings. The rules: dinners involving alcohol should wrap up by around ten p.m., and run no longer than three hours. This comes in response to a string of scandals. And there's something very Japanese, and very human, in this story. The after-work drinking session — the nomikai — has long been woven into professional life here, a place where real conversations happen and relationships are built. But it can also curdle into something harder to control. And so a national broadcaster finds itself writing down, in black and white, that the party should end by ten. Sometimes the most telling stories aren't about policy or markets. They're about an institution quietly deciding that the old ways need a bedtime.
