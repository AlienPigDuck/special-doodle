Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Friday, the 27th of November, 2026, and we've reached the end of another week. Let's get into it.

We begin this morning with a number that's genuinely hard to picture: ten trillion yen. That's what Tokyo Electric Power — TEPCO, the utility most of us associate with the Fukushima disaster of 2011 — plans to invest over the next nine years. Nikkei reports this morning that TEPCO will pour 10.2 trillion yen, through the 2035 fiscal year, into transmission networks and nuclear power, all of it aimed at one booming market: artificial intelligence.

Here's the logic. AI runs on data centers, and data centers are astonishingly hungry for electricity. TEPCO wants to attract the big American hyperscalers — the companies building these facilities — by promising them abundant, reliable power. And the growth targets are striking. The company plans to expand its electricity sales to data centers from 4.3 billion kilowatt-hours this fiscal year to roughly eight times that by 2035, and nearly sixteen times by 2040.

Now, why does a utility need to grow this aggressively? The answer sits in the background of everything TEPCO does: Fukushima. Decommissioning the Fukushima Daiichi plant costs enormous sums, and those bills will run for decades. Selling power to data centers is, in part, how TEPCO hopes to fund that cleanup. To raise capital, it's in talks with five outside investor groups — SoftBank, Japan Industrial Partners, and the American giants KKR, Blackstone, and GIP.

But here's the catch, and Nikkei flags it clearly. This whole strategy rests on restarting Japan's nuclear reactors. If those restarts don't proceed as planned — and nuclear restarts in Japan are slow, politically fraught, and locally contested — the entire plan wobbles. It's a big bet on an energy future that isn't guaranteed to arrive on schedule.

And that tension — AI's appetite versus the places that actually have to power it — runs straight through today's paper. Because over in Australia, Nikkei reports that opposition to new data centers is spreading fast. A plan by the AI company Anthropic has drawn objections running at ninety-nine percent of public responses. The worry? Electricity and water. These facilities consume staggering amounts of both, and the state of Victoria is now moving to tighten its rules. So the same technology TEPCO is betting billions on is meeting a wall of resistance elsewhere. Worth holding both of those in mind at once.

Let's turn to politics and trade, where the Takaichi government has a difficult morning.

First, a test of nerves in the upper house. Eight opposition parties have submitted a censure motion against Education Minister Yoshihiro Seki. Now, a censure motion is a formal rebuke — it carries political weight but doesn't force a resignation on its own. What makes this one tense is the arithmetic. Nikkei reports that neither side holds a clear majority, and the vote may come down to just one or two votes. That means independents and smaller conservative members hold the balance. Prime Minister Takaichi, for her part, is standing by her minister — she's signaled he'll stay. But a vote this close is the kind of thing that can rattle a government.

And Takaichi has a bigger headache coming from Washington. The U.S. Trade Representative has announced new tariffs: fifteen percent on Japanese goods, twenty percent on Chinese. Here's the sharp part — if Japan falls short on the investment commitments it's made to the United States, that fifteen percent could rise to twenty. The tariffs take effect after public hearings in January. And the Trump administration is openly criticizing Takaichi's 370-trillion-yen investment plan, calling it "overproduction." So a policy the prime minister has championed at home is now being used against Japan abroad.

Domestically, two quick notes. The government wants to revise its AI promotion law in next year's ordinary session — requiring companies developing the most powerful AI to report in, submit to inspections, and maintain security systems, with fines up to one hundred million yen for violations. And here's a creative one: the ruling Liberal Democratic Party is looking at expanding how much revenue from public gambling — think the keirin bicycle races and the national lottery — gets paid into the treasury. The goal is to secure around ten trillion yen, partly to fund a consumption tax cut. An old revenue stream pressed into new service.

Now, defense — and a development with real regional weight. South Korean President Lee Jae-myung has announced plans to build nuclear-powered submarines. The aim is deployment in the latter half of the 2030s, eventually running six of them. The driver is North Korea, which is developing its own nuclear submarines. And Nikkei notes Japan is watching closely — the question of whether Japan itself should possess nuclear submarines is now part of the debate over revising its three core security documents. For a country with Japan's postwar constraints, that's a significant conversation.

Speaking of North Korea — Pyongyang has demanded that Switzerland close its office there, retaliating after the Swiss sanctioned a North Korean group tied to Russia's abduction of Ukrainian children. And Kim Jong-un has reportedly ordered an expansion of nuclear material production. A hardening posture all around.

To the corporate world, where the electric vehicle story keeps getting more complicated. Toyota has, for the third time, delayed its EV battery plant in Fukuoka. Production was meant to begin in 2028; now it's pushed back again. The reason is simple — demand uncertainty, made worse by the Trump administration scrapping EV subsidies. Toyota is waiting to see where the market actually lands.

But while Toyota hesitates, China's BYD is surging. In the July-to-September quarter, BYD rose to fourth place in global sales — 1.32 million vehicles, up nineteen percent. The real story is overseas: sales abroad more than doubled to 540,000 vehicles, with rapid share gains in Europe and Australia. A genuine milestone for the company.

And Suzuki is adjusting to the geopolitics of batteries. Nikkei reports it will begin domestic production of lithium iron phosphate batteries — a type that uses no rare metals — from October 2029, at a new site in Kosai, Shizuoka. The goal is to reduce exposure to the risk of sanctions on Chinese products. A theme we keep returning to: companies quietly building insurance against a fracturing world.

Two more quick ones. Kioxia, the Japanese memory maker, is working with Nvidia to see whether its SSDs can take over part of the job currently done by high-bandwidth memory in AI systems. And the Bank of Japan's holdings of exchange-traded funds have swelled to 113 trillion yen — up eighteen trillion in just six months, driven by the roughly thirty percent rise in the Nikkei average. The central bank plans to accelerate its sales from January.

And that brings me to the one that caught my eye this morning — and it's about the quieter battlefields of modern politics.

Taiwan holds local elections this Sunday, and Nikkei reports that in the run-up, cyberattacks from China have surged past 180 million suspicious access attempts in a single month. Let that figure sink in — 180 million. But it's not just the volume. Investigators have found AI-generated fake videos and impersonation campaigns — sophisticated information warfare aimed at shaping how Taiwanese voters think and feel.

What strikes me is how ordinary this has become. These are local elections — mayors, councilors, the unglamorous machinery of democracy. And yet they've become a proving ground for the next generation of influence operations, where the weapon isn't a missile but a convincing fake. For every country holding elections in the years ahead, Taiwan this weekend is a glimpse of what's coming. Worth paying attention to how it holds up.
