Let's begin with a milestone that's been twenty-six years in the making.

For the first time ever, Japan's corporate pension funds are in the black. According to the Nikkei this morning, the country's defined-benefit pension plans — these are the traditional company pensions that promise retirees a fixed payout — posted their first-ever asset surplus in the fiscal year that ended in March. The numbers are striking. These funds now hold ¥51.5 trillion in assets, against ¥47.5 trillion in obligations. That's a surplus of four trillion yen. And it's the first time this has happened since Japan introduced retirement benefit accounting back in the year 2000.

Now, why does this matter, and why now? For most of the past two decades, these pension funds were chronically underfunded. In a world of ultra-low interest rates and a sluggish stock market, companies struggled to grow their assets fast enough to cover what they'd promised their retirees. That was a drag on corporate balance sheets for years.

Three things changed at once. First, the weak yen boosted the value of foreign assets these funds hold. Second, the stock market soared — the Nikkei Average is up more than forty percent over the past year. And third — this one's a bit counterintuitive — rising interest rates actually shrank the present value of future pension obligations. When rates go up, future liabilities are worth less in today's money. So all three forces pushed in the same direction.

And here's the interesting part: companies are now deciding what to do with the surplus. Nippon Yusen Kaisha — the big shipping line — along with Mitsubishi Paper and Sojo Electric, are redirecting those freed-up funds toward mergers, acquisitions, and growth investment. Others are raising their payout rates, so retirees actually get more. It's a quiet but real structural shift in how corporate Japan handles its obligations to employees.

Now, to politics, and a vote that came down to the wire.

The Upper House of the Diet voted Thursday to reject a no-confidence resolution against Education Minister Yoshihiro Seki — but just barely. The count was 124 to 120, out of 244 votes cast. That's a margin of four. One of the closest ever recorded. Seki had come under fire over political fundraising parties where, Nikkei reports, bureaucrats delivered policy pitches to company executives who had each paid ninety thousand yen to attend. That raises uncomfortable questions about neutrality — whether paying your way into a room buys you influence over policy.

Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi chose to keep Seki in his post. She cited the absence of any legal violation and pointed to party stability. But a four-vote margin tells you something about the mood in the Diet right now. It's fractious, and the opposition isn't finished scrutinizing this one.

Staying with the government for a moment — there's a clear theme running through the policy pages this morning, and it's young families. The ruling coalition is planning to expand gift tax exemptions in next year's tax reform. Right now, parents can gift up to ten million yen toward a child's home purchase tax-free. The plan would raise that cap to fifteen million yen for child-rearing households. The logic is straightforward: housing costs have climbed, and younger buyers are being priced out. This is one lever to help older generations pass wealth down to help their kids buy a home.

Now, onto trade — and a genuinely big story that hasn't gotten the attention it deserves.

The CPTPP — that's the Trans-Pacific trade bloc Japan helped build after the United States walked away from it — has decided to begin formal membership talks with three new countries: Indonesia, the Philippines, and the United Arab Emirates. And just look at that list. This stretches the bloc well beyond the Pacific — into Southeast Asia and, for the first time, the Middle East.

If these talks succeed, the Nikkei reports the CPTPP would cover roughly ten billion people and about seventeen percent of global GDP. That starts to look like a free-trade zone rivaling the European Union. It's a notable bet on open trade at a moment when much of the world is turning inward.

From trade to energy — and here's a story that matters quietly but enormously to Japan. Australia's parliament has passed a law requiring gas exporters to reserve twenty percent of their exports for domestic supply, starting in 2028. Japan relies on Australia for about forty percent of its liquefied natural gas imports. So on paper, that sounds alarming. But here's the key detail: Western Australia — which is where most of Japan's LNG actually comes from — won an effective exemption. So the disruption risk to Japan is limited. A reminder of how much Japan's energy security depends on the fine print of another country's laws.

A few quick notes from overseas.

Europe has chosen its next central banker. Pablo Hernández de Cos, a Spaniard who currently runs the Bank for International Settlements, will become president of the European Central Bank in February, when Christine Lagarde steps down. He's a return to the technocrats — a career central banker rather than a politician. Markets expect what the Nikkei calls a "dovish-leaning consensus" — meaning he'll favor coordination over aggressive rate hikes.

Taiwan, meanwhile, has raised its growth forecast for this year to a remarkable 13.28 percent — up from eleven percent. The driver is the artificial intelligence boom: semiconductor exports from TSMC and AI servers from Hon Hai. That is an extraordinary pace of growth, and it tells you just how central Taiwan has become to the global AI supply chain.

And a sobering note from North Korea. Kim Jong Un held a rare public inspection of nuclear weapons facilities, with state media releasing photographs of seven warheads of different sizes — and equipment analysts believe is a small reactor suited for submarine deployment. South Korean experts read this as a message: a diverse arsenal, and submarine-launched capability edging closer to reality.

Now, to the corporate pages, where cybersecurity is the common thread.

Two Japanese financial firms are still dealing with the fallout from a major data breach that hit 4.41 million foreign-exchange trading accounts back in October. GMO Internet Group has baked a three-billion-yen loss from that breach into its forecast — and yet it still expects operating profit of sixty-seven billion yen, up nine percent. And Rakuten Securities has postponed its launch of 23-hour US stock trading, citing the same breach, while competitors like SBI and Monex press ahead on schedule. A reminder that in finance, trust is the product — and once it's shaken, everything slows down.

And that brings me to the one that caught my eye.

Netflix is making a bigger bet on Japanese anime. The company announced expanded long-term investment in Japan, and it's launching something called "Anime Home" — a dedicated global showcase, going live in December across more than a hundred and ninety countries.

I find this quietly significant. Anime has gone from niche to mainstream to, now, a pillar of global streaming strategy. A dedicated front door, in nearly every country on earth, built around Japanese animation. It's a remarkable example of soft power — Japan exporting not cars or electronics, but stories and imagination. And for the creators here in Japan, a global audience of that scale changes what's possible.
