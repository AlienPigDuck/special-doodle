Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Saturday, the eighth of August, 2026, and I'm glad you're with me.

We begin this morning with a number that would have seemed almost unthinkable a generation ago. According to the Nikkei, for the first time ever, both South Korea and Taiwan have out-exported Japan. In the first half of this year, South Korea's exports came to $496 billion. Taiwan's, $416 billion. And Japan? Three hundred and eighty-four billion dollars — trailing both.

Now, to understand why this matters, you have to understand what's driving it. And the answer is chips. Specifically, the advanced semiconductors that power artificial intelligence. South Korea has Samsung and SK Hynix. Taiwan has TSMC — the company that makes the most sophisticated chips on Earth. For both countries, semiconductors now account for roughly thirty percent of everything they sell abroad. The AI boom has been, quite literally, an export boom.

And here's the uncomfortable part for Japan. Japan is actually very good at semiconductors — but at making the *equipment* that produces them, not the chips themselves. That equipment is just five percent of Japanese exports. Japan's export economy still leans, heavily, on cars. Meanwhile, South Korea and Taiwan each poured more than twenty trillion yen into chip production.

So the Nikkei frames this not as a bad quarter, but as something deeper — a challenge to the post-war manufacturing model that made Japan rich. The question hanging over Tokyo is whether the country that once defined the electronics age can adapt to the AI one. It's a big question, and it sets the tone for much of this morning's paper.

Because the AI story runs right through the business pages, too. Look at Toshiba. The company reported a net profit of four-point-four-six *trillion* yen for the quarter — an eye-watering figure. But most of that isn't really operating money. It comes from revaluing Toshiba's stake in Kioxia, the memory chip maker. As AI demand inflated chip valuations — nearly fivefold between March and June — Toshiba's stake ballooned, delivering a special gain of over six trillion yen. It's a paper profit driven by the same boom that lifted Korea and Taiwan.

And it's the same story at the GPIF — that's Japan's Government Pension Investment Fund, the enormous pool of money that backs public pensions. The GPIF just posted record quarterly gains of twenty-four trillion yen, riding the global rally in AI and semiconductor stocks. Its total assets have now, for the first time, passed three hundred trillion yen. So the boom that's reshaping Japan's place in world trade is also, for now, quietly padding its retirement accounts.

Let me turn to the yen, because it was a turbulent week and the paper keeps circling back to it.

The trigger came from Washington. The U.S. Labor Department reported that American employers actually *cut* twenty-three thousand jobs in July — a five-month low, when economists had expected gains of eighty to ninety thousand. Much of it came from state government education cuts. That surprise sent investors scrambling to reprice how quickly the Federal Reserve might cut rates, and the yen jumped to 156 to the dollar on speculation that Japan might intervene.

But here's where the Nikkei's analysis gets genuinely interesting — and it's my most important story of the morning after the chip news. Japan and the United States actually did coordinate an intervention, on the thirty-first of July, to prop up the yen. And it didn't hold. By the sixth of August, the yen had slid right back to 164.

The paper argues this isn't really about speculators. It's about trust. There's a phrase in the piece — the "bone-breaking shock," in Japanese *seisaku no honebuto shokku* — describing the market's sense that the government is leaning on the Bank of Japan, pressuring it over policy. When investors doubt a central bank's independence, when they can't tell whether the government will tighten its belt or keep spending, a currency loses what the Nikkei calls its "soft power." Its global appeal.

The conclusion is sobering. Japan's monetary influence, once formidable, has eroded — and intervention only works for a moment when the deeper credibility isn't there. That's a structural problem, not a market blip, and it's worth keeping an eye on.

Closer to home, there's movement on taxes. The government wants to trim the consumption tax on food by one percentage point, and the debate is scrambling the opposition. The Democratic Party's leader, Tamaki Yuichiro, is signaling he might *support* the cut rather than oppose it outright — weighing sustainable public finances against the politics of the local elections coming next spring. Komeito, too, is wavering. It's a reminder that tax policy in Japan is never just about economics.

And a quiet but striking figure on food. Japan's food self-sufficiency rate has fallen to a record low — just thirty-seven percent. The paper links this to what it calls the "Reiwa Rice Shortage," a bout of rice price volatility that pushed shoppers toward cheaper imports. The government's target for 2030 is forty-five percent, so this is moving in the wrong direction.

A few business notes before we go. Kirin Holdings — yes, the beer company — is buying Canada's Jameson Wellness for two hundred and eighteen billion yen. It's a bet on health science: probiotics, cognitive-health ingredients, beauty products. Kirin wants out of pure beer dependence, and it's buying its way into thirty-two thousand North American retail outlets to do it.

Fujitsu, meanwhile, is exploring spinning off its copier business — calling this its "third founding" as it pivots toward semiconductors and biotech manufacturing. The shares fell eighteen percent on the news, but the Nikkei compares it to the painful but necessary shift out of film photography twenty years ago.

Two quick international items worth flagging. India has committed one-point-four trillion yen to deep-sea oil and gas exploration — a plan called *Samudra Manthan* — opening its waters to foreign drillers with fifty-percent cost subsidies, all to reduce its dangerous dependence on Middle Eastern crude. And Saudi Arabia, Pakistan, and Turkey have signed a NATO-style mutual defense pact: an attack on one is an attack on all three. That's a significant new Sunni bloc forming amid Israeli-Iranian tensions.

And now, the one that caught my eye — tucked away in the front-page reporting on regional economies. It's about a small idea with a big result. Six Japanese prefectures have now reached what economists call a near-perfect "circulation rate" — meaning the money they earn stays home, rather than leaking off to Tokyo or Osaka. Mie Prefecture actually tops one hundred percent.

But the star is Oita, on the island of Kyushu. Back in 2010, Oita's circulation rate was ninety-four percent. Today it's over ninety-nine. And the reason is charmingly practical: wood biomass power. Oita started burning its discarded timber — scrap wood that would otherwise rot — to generate electricity. That meant less money flowing out to import energy, and more of it staying in the local economy.

It's a small story about firewood, really. But it points to something hopeful — that regions willing to use what they already have can build economies that sustain themselves.
