We begin with a story that marks a real shift in Japanese thinking. The government is planning to set up specialized defense research institutes at universities and national research organizations by fiscal 2031. The focus will be on cutting-edge technologies — artificial intelligence, space systems, the kind of foundational work that could feed into future military equipment.

Now, why does this matter so much? To understand it, you have to understand the history. For decades after the Second World War, Japanese academia kept defense research firmly at arm's length. Many universities had explicit policies against any research with military applications. It was a point of principle, rooted in the postwar pacifist consensus. So the idea of the government building defense research centers inside universities — and asking scholars to work with a clear pathway toward weapons development — is genuinely significant. It's not just a budget line. It's a cultural change.

And the thinking behind it is spelled out in a related item in today's paper. A senior official warns that, in their words, "the distance between science and security must narrow." The argument is that Japan can't rely only on its traditional defense contractors anymore. The real innovation — in AI, in dual-use technology — is happening in the civilian sector. The government wants defense money flowing into basic research, not just into finished equipment. Whether researchers themselves embrace that is a very open question, given that historical reluctance I mentioned. This is a story to watch over the next several years.

Staying with security policy, the government is also reviewing its active cyber defense law, and this one raises some real constitutional questions. The review would allow monitoring of domestic communications — and that bumps up against Japan's constitutional protection of the secrecy of communications. It's the familiar tension between security and privacy, and in Japan the privacy protections are written right into the constitution. Expect pushback.

And Prime Minister Takaichi spoke again about the abduction issue — the decades-old question of Japanese citizens kidnapped by North Korea in the 1970s and 80s. Takaichi said, "I will open a breakthrough in my administration," though she acknowledged that any progress depends heavily on the Trump-Kim dialogue channel. On that note, North Korea's Kim Jong-un was characteristically defiant this week, warning the Trump administration against resuming military action and claiming the United States had, in his words, "lost" both the conflict and the election.

There's also some friction inside Takaichi's own party. A consumption tax reduction bill is facing scrutiny in the Diet, and four LDP lawmakers actually boycotted the vote — they say the fiscal planning and the implementation details just aren't there yet. A small rebellion, but a telling one.

Let's turn to the Japanese economy, where the big front-page number this morning is about office space. Tokyo office rents have jumped twenty percent over the past year — the highest level since the bubble era burst more than three decades ago. In the prime Yaesu-Kyobashi-Nihonbashi district near Tokyo Station, rents are up a staggering ninety-seven percent.

What's driving this? In a word: workers. Japan's chronic labor shortage has flipped the old logic on its head. Companies used to treat office space as a cost to be minimized. Now they're treating it as a recruitment tool. In a tight labor market, with people coming back to the office after the pandemic, firms are upgrading to attractive buildings in prime locations to win and keep talent. It's a small window into just how competitive hiring has become in Japan.

And speaking of confidence in Japan's future, Nikkei reports that Sakana AI is preparing for an IPO by autumn 2027. Sakana is a generative AI company, backed by Google and NVIDIA, and only founded in 2023 — yet it reached unicorn status, a billion-dollar valuation, within a single year. The target now is a valuation north of four hundred billion yen, with Goldman Sachs advising. If it lands, this could be the largest domestic startup IPO in Japanese history, surpassing Mercari's 680-billion-yen debut back in 2018. It's a vote of confidence in Japan's homegrown AI ambitions, at a moment when the rest of the world is nervous about an AI bubble.

The confidence shows up in the markets too. The Nikkei average rebounded sharply, up 1,340 points — about two and a third percent — to close at 58,775 yen, lifted by strong earnings. Mitsubishi Heavy Industries surged after issuing its highest-ever profit guidance and announcing a 300-billion-yen share buyback. Semiconductor equipment makers also posted strong numbers, riding the wave of AI datacenter demand — JX Metals up fifty-three percent in profit, Kokusai Electric raising its guidance. And SoftBank is projecting a record profit of 6.1 trillion yen for the coming fiscal year, driven by its PayPay payments business and asset sales — hitting that target two years ahead of schedule.

A couple of big corporate moves to flag. Mitsui Chemicals is acquiring JSR's semiconductor materials division for 1.84 trillion yen, buying it from a government fund. Nikkei calls it Japan's largest-ever acquisition — a decisive bet on the semiconductor sector. And NTT is selling its entire 4.9 percent stake in NEC, worth around 290 billion yen, unwinding a capital alliance the two struck back in 2020. That partnership was built around next-generation telecom equipment — 5G and 4G — and NEC is now exiting that business, so the alliance has run its course.

One more I want to mention, because it speaks to a broader shift in Japanese corporate culture. Panasonic Holdings plans to appoint managers in their forties to sixty percent of new executive roles by 2028 — up from thirty-five percent today. In a country long defined by seniority-based promotion, deliberately pushing younger leaders into the top ranks is a meaningful break with tradition.

And now, the one that caught my eye — and this is a big one, buried a little deeper in the paper. NVIDIA's board has confirmed it is investigating its own chief executive, Jensen Huang. The subject: Huang's relationship with a woman allegedly connected to Chinese intelligence. The board is examining whether any sensitive chip technology may have been compromised.

Now, NVIDIA says there is no evidence of any breach. But just the existence of the investigation is extraordinary. Huang is arguably the most important executive in the world right now — NVIDIA's chips are the engine of the entire AI boom, and the company sits at the absolute center of the US-China technology rivalry. Huang himself has been doing a kind of personal, high-stakes diplomacy, courting governments and customers around the world. A scandal like this — espionage, a Chinese connection, the integrity of the most coveted technology on earth — could undermine all of that. And it lands at the worst possible moment, with Washington and Beijing locked in a battle over who controls the future of AI. China, incidentally, just extended its tariff truce with the US through January 2027, so the truce holds for now — but trust between the two remains paper-thin. Whatever this investigation finds, the implications reach far beyond one man's personal life.
