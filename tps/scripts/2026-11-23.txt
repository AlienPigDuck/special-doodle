Let's begin under the sea — specifically, beneath the waters of the Western Pacific, where a quiet but consequential shift is underway.

The Nikkei leads this morning with China's rapid expansion of its submarine fleet, and the challenge it poses to American naval dominance. According to the U.S. Navy's Office of Naval Intelligence, China could field as many as eighty submarines by 2035. Half of them would be nuclear-powered. Today, China has sixty-one, fifteen of them nuclear-armed. And here's a detail worth sitting with: Russia is reportedly helping China make its submarines quieter and faster — stealthier, and harder to track. For a submarine, stealth is everything. A quiet submarine is a submarine you can't find.

The forecast is that by 2040, China could threaten American supremacy in these waters outright. And that's prompting a regional response. Australia, South Korea, and others are accelerating plans to acquire nuclear-powered submarines.

And then there's Japan. Japan has twenty-two submarines — all diesel-electric, all conventional, none nuclear. That reflects a deep postwar commitment to a non-nuclear posture, rooted in the only country ever to suffer atomic attack. But the Nikkei frames today's story around a hard question: in a Taiwan contingency, would conventional submarines be enough? As the gap widens, Japan is under growing pressure to at least reconsider. That's a profound thing for this country to be weighing, and we'll be watching it closely.

Staying with the geopolitics, a pointed moment from the recent APEC summit in Shenzhen. North Korea's Kim Jong Un declined to meet President Trump — and chose instead to keep touring the countryside on inspections. The Nikkei reads this as a deliberate snub, and a telling one. Kim, the paper argues, simply doesn't feel the pressure anymore. He's got fifty-plus nuclear warheads, Russian military backing, and what's been described as a "blood alliance" with Vladimir Putin. Meanwhile, Trump has two years left in his term — and Kim has, well, indefinitely. That asymmetry of time favors Pyongyang, which can now set preconditions: recognition as a nuclear weapons state, and a reversal of what it calls hostile U.S. policies.

The ripple effects of a second Trump presidency run through several stories this morning. NATO has pushed its 2027 summit in Tirana, Albania, back to 2028 — officially to avoid friction. Alliance sources tell the Nikkei the real worry is that an in-person summit, with Trump pressing hard on European defense spending, could do more harm than good to the alliance's unity.

And Europe is hedging. On Saturday, the EU signed a comprehensive economic partnership with Indonesia, aiming for provisional implementation in January. At its heart: critical minerals — especially nickel, the stuff of EV batteries. This follows EU deals with South America's Mercosur bloc, with India, and with Australia. Brussels is quite deliberately diversifying — away from both Washington's unpredictability and Beijing's grip on raw materials.

Now, two major stories from the climate and legal fronts, and neither brings cheerful news.

COP31, the UN climate conference, collapsed — and I mean that literally. For the first time in the history of these summits, it ended with no consensus document at all. The sticking point was language on phasing out fossil fuels, which was stripped out on the nineteenth at the insistence of India, oil-producing nations, and others. The European Union and Pacific island nations — the countries most at risk of disappearing beneath rising seas — walked out in protest. The talks ran forty-four hours over schedule, the longest climate negotiation ever. All of this as the world approaches the 1.5-degree warming threshold. The divide between developed and developing nations, the Nikkei notes, has rarely looked wider.

And a landmark ruling from The Hague. The International Court of Justice found that Myanmar's 2017 military crackdown on the Rohingya Muslim minority constitutes genocide. This is the first time an international court has ever made such a finding of state-sponsored genocide. The court ordered Myanmar to prevent further killings and to allow refugees to return safely. It's a historic verdict — but enforcement is another matter. Myanmar's current leader, Min Aung Hlaing, was the general in charge in 2017. And with China and Russia holding vetoes at the Security Council, and a civil war still raging in Rakhine State, the path from judgment to justice remains deeply uncertain.

A grim footnote connected to that crisis: Malaysia's deputy prime minister announced plans last week to resettle ten thousand Rohingya refugees in Turkey — then quietly deleted the post after Ankara said no such agreement existed. A small, awkward illustration of how hard it is to share the burden of a displaced people nobody seems to want.

Let's turn to business, where the story is Japan losing ground it long held without question.

Chinese automakers now outsell their Japanese rivals in thirty-nine countries worldwide. And fourteen of those reversals happened just this year — including Germany, Italy, and Brazil. Think about that. These are serious car markets, and in some cases, car-making nations in their own right. BYD and the newer player Li Auto have ridden rising oil prices and surging EV demand straight into territory Japan once owned. Even in Southeast Asia, Japan's traditional stronghold, its lead shrank over the year from sixty-four points to fifty-four. Chinese carmakers lifted overseas sales fifty-one percent in the first nine months of 2026 — and they're building locally, too. BYD's new Indonesian factory can produce 150,000 vehicles a year, its largest in the region. The era of unquestioned Japanese dominance in the global car market is, this morning, visibly ending.

And a staggering number from the AI world: the American startup Anthropic raised around ninety billion dollars in its IPO on the nineteenth. That brings its cash reserves to two hundred thirteen billion dollars — four times what Alphabet, Google's parent, holds, and more than six major tech peers combined. A startup, sitting on a war chest larger than the giants. Whatever you think of the AI boom, that's a remarkable concentration of capital.

Before we close, two stories from Japan that balance the heavier news.

Hitachi has begun field trials of something it calls "structured radio waves" — a technology to detect the early signs of natural disasters from space. In plain terms: instead of flat, two-dimensional satellite images, these controlled waves can capture precise three-dimensional readings of terrain and atmosphere. Hitachi hopes to spot landslides, floods, and crumbling infrastructure before they fail — bridges, railways, power grids. Airborne tests come in 2027, satellites by 2035. More than twenty companies are already interested. In a country that lives with earthquakes and typhoons, the appeal is obvious.

And the story that caught my eye this morning — and warmed it, honestly — is Shuri Castle.

On Saturday, in Okinawa, the main palace of Shuri Castle officially reopened. If you don't know it, Shuri was the seat of the old Ryukyu Kingdom, the vermilion-red heart of Okinawan identity. It was destroyed in the Second World War, rebuilt in 1992, and then — devastatingly — burned again in a 2019 fire. This weekend marks the completion of a seven-year reconstruction.

But here's the part I love. The restoration leaned heavily on research archives compiled a century ago by a scholar named Yoshitaro Kamakura, who documented Okinawan heritage so meticulously that his work had already, once before, saved the castle from demolition during Japan's imperial period. A man who studied a place with such care that, generations later, his notes helped raise it from the ashes — twice. Artisans from across Japan contributed, blending historical accuracy with modern technique. It's a quiet testament to the idea that knowledge, carefully kept, outlasts the people who gather it.
