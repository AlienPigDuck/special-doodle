Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Saturday, the twenty-ninth of August, 2026, and I'm glad you're with me.

We begin this morning with a story that says a great deal about where the world's cars are being built, and by whom. Nikkei reports that Toyota — yes, Toyota, the company synonymous with Japanese manufacturing — will begin building its most advanced electric vehicles not in Japan, and not in the West, but in China. Production is set to start in the fall of 2027, at a new factory in Shanghai. The first vehicle off the line will be a luxury Lexus SUV, starting at a modest thousand units a month and scaling to tens of thousands a year by 2028.

Now, why does this matter? Because it's a quiet admission of something profound. China now accounts for sixty percent of all electric vehicle sales on the planet — some 8.57 million cars last year, dwarfing Europe. And Toyota is saying, in effect, that if you want to build the best EVs, you build them where the customers, the suppliers, and increasingly the innovation are. The company is bringing its cutting-edge techniques to Shanghai — including something called "gigacast," which casts large sections of the car's aluminum body in single pieces, cutting complexity and cost. Toyota developed these advanced electric vehicles specifically for Chinese consumers. So the world's largest automaker is following the market east, and taking its crown jewels along with it.

And Toyota isn't the only Japanese carmaker rethinking the future this morning. Nikkei also reports that Honda and Nissan have agreed to jointly develop the operating system and computers for what the industry calls software-defined vehicles — cars whose features are shaped by software, updated over the air, more like your phone than the car your parents drove. Together with Mitsubishi, these three companies sell more than 7.3 million vehicles a year, and pooling their efforts gives that shared platform real scale. It's a sign of how the car business is being reshaped — the value moving from the engine to the code.

Let me turn to money, because it was a rough day for the yen. In New York trading on Thursday, the Japanese currency slipped past 160 to the dollar — the first time it's breached that level since Japan and the United States stepped in together, back in late July, with coordinated intervention to prop it up. What pushed it there? American interest rates. The two-year U.S. Treasury note climbed to 4.3 percent, and behind that lies a message from the Federal Reserve.

At the annual gathering of central bankers in Jackson Hole, Wyoming, Fed Chair Jerome Powell said "there is work to be done" on inflation — and hinted the Fed might actually raise rates further if prices stay hot. And they are hot: the Fed's preferred inflation gauge stood at 3.7 percent in July, unchanged from June, well above the two percent target. Powell admitted the underlying trend "has not improved materially." Higher American rates draw money toward the dollar and away from the yen — which is why a speech in Wyoming ends up weakening a currency in Tokyo.

Here's a wrinkle worth knowing. For years, a weak yen was good news for Japanese stocks — it fattened the earnings of exporters like Toyota. But Nikkei reports that old relationship has fractured. Markets are now fixated on the artificial intelligence story rather than export profits, and experts warn that without genuine gains in productivity, an extremely weak yen stops being a tailwind and becomes a drag — raising the cost of everything Japan imports.

Now, three stories this morning that together tell a hard truth about Japan's population. Births in the first half of the year rose just eight-tenths of a percent, to 342,000. That's an increase, technically — but a tiny one, even as marriages recover. Nikkei points to the reason: the career penalty women still pay for raising children. Women's real wages have stagnated, childcare remains expensive, and that dims what the paper bluntly calls the "desire" to have a first child.

And the childcare system itself is buckling in strange, uneven ways. The waiting list for daycare has hit a nine-year high — 2,435 children with no place as of April — and Tokyo accounts for more than half of that, after the city expanded its subsidies and demand surged. Meanwhile, rural areas have the opposite problem: too many facilities, running at eighty percent capacity, built for a population that is simply shrinking away. It's a portrait of a country whose people are concentrating in the capital, leaving the countryside with empty rooms.

There's also a labor story here. Job openings fell in July, as restaurants and shops accelerate their turn to self-checkout and mobile ordering — replacing workers with software. Small retailers' software holdings jumped nearly five percent to record highs. Fewer workers, more code — a theme, you'll notice, running right through today's paper.

Abroad, a sobering item. U.S. intelligence suspects Vladimir Putin may be planning limited strikes on Baltic members of NATO — not a full war, but a test, to see whether the alliance would truly respond. CIA Director Krap has traveled to Moscow to discuss de-escalation. The deeper question, Nikkei notes, is credibility: NATO's promise that an attack on one is an attack on all only holds if everyone honors it — and there are doubts about whether the United States under President Trump would.

In China, General Zhang Youxia, one of the military's most senior officers, has been removed on corruption charges — a purge that reaches into the very top of the armed forces. And there's tragic news from the Himalayas: the U.S. Geological Survey has confirmed that the catastrophic flood that killed more than 550 people along the Nepal-China border on the twenty-sixth was triggered not by an earthquake but by a collapsing glacier. Global glacier loss has nearly quadrupled since the 1990s — around 390 billion tons a year now. A warming planet, made suddenly, terribly concrete.

And in Norway, King Harald the Fifth has died at 89 — Europe's longest-reigning monarch, on the throne for thirty-five years. His son, Crown Prince Haakon, becomes king.

One more from the world of business, because the number is almost hard to say aloud. Anthropic — the American AI company founded by Dario Amodei — is preparing a public offering in September that could value it at two trillion dollars. Its revenue jumped 150 percent in the last quarter, to 11.5 billion, and unlike some rivals, it's actually turning a profit. It would stand shoulder to shoulder with Amazon. Whether that valuation is genius or delusion, history will decide — but it captures the moment we're living through.

And now, the one that caught my eye. It comes from India, and it's about motorcycles. Yamaha has unveiled a new premium bike, the YZF-R2, priced at about 230,000 rupees — roughly 390,000 yen. Why does this matter? Because India is the largest motorcycle market on Earth — more than twenty million sold a year — and it's changing. For decades, Indians bought small, cheap, practical commuter bikes to get to work. But as middle-income households rise, they want something more: lifestyle bikes, sportier machines, motorcycles you buy for pleasure, not just necessity. The segment above 150cc grew a remarkable 33 percent last year. It's a small story about a single motorcycle, but it's really a story about a country growing richer — and beginning to buy for joy rather than just for need. There's something quietly hopeful in that.
