Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Tuesday, the twenty-fifth of August, 2026, and I'm glad you're with me.

We're going to start this morning with a story that sounds small and technical but is actually about something big — who gets to own a piece of Japan's economy.

The Nikkei reports that the government is going to abolish the requirement for companies to mail out shareholder meeting materials on paper. By 2027, all of that goes digital. Now, on its own, that's a rounding error — fewer envelopes, lower postage. But here's why it matters. Right now, Japan has something called the unit stock system. If you want to buy shares in most Japanese companies, you have to buy them in blocks of a hundred. A hundred shares, minimum. And for a lot of well-known companies, that means you need tens of thousands of yen just to get in the door. Compare that to the United States, where you can buy a single share — or even a fraction of one — and you can see the problem. Japanese stocks have long been harder for ordinary people to actually own.

Part of the reason companies liked those big blocks is cost. More shareholders means more paper mailings, more expense. So by taking the paper out of the equation, the government removes one of the excuses for keeping that hundred-share minimum. The real prize, the Nikkei says, is eventually scrapping the unit stock system altogether — and opening Japanese equities to a much wider group of small, individual investors. This fits squarely into Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi's push to get more Japanese households investing rather than leaving their savings sitting in the bank. Japan has famously cautious savers. This is one more nudge toward the market.

And speaking of getting money to move, there are two related stories this morning about exactly that problem in different countries. Mitsubishi UFJ Trust is heading into Vietnam. It's setting up a joint venture with BIDV, a state-owned Vietnamese bank, to launch wealth management services by 2027, aiming to manage some three hundred billion yen. Here's the interesting part — Vietnamese personal wealth is expected to hit six hundred billion dollars by 2027, but almost all of it sits in bank deposits and gold. MUFG wants to coax that money into stocks and bonds as Vietnam's economy grows at roughly eight percent a year. It's the same idea we just heard about Japanese households — get the money working — just in a faster-growing market.

Also on the front page, a Taiwan connection. Itochu's IT subsidiary, CTC, has formed a joint venture with Systemics, Taiwan's largest systems integrator, and they're setting up shop in Fukuoka. The goal is to help Taiwanese companies move into Kyushu. And why Kyushu? One word: TSMC. The Taiwanese chip giant's operations there have created a whole gravitational pull — suppliers, partners, and now the IT services they all need. The partners are targeting ten billion yen in sales by 2030. Kyushu is quietly becoming Japan's semiconductor heartland, and this is another sign of it.

But there's a sobering counterpoint on the same page. It's been one month since the Kumamoto earthquake, and the Nikkei looks at what it exposed: Japan's aging factories. At Japanese Pulp and Paper's Yashiro plant in Kumamoto, the quake caused major damage and killed nine workers. And the paper points to a startling number — Japan's manufacturing equipment has an average age of thirteen-point-seven years. That's the second oldest among twenty-three major countries. Older machines, older buildings, more vulnerable to seismic stress. Mitsui Metals has been hit repeatedly, in 2025 and again in 2026. The dilemma is painful and familiar: companies know they should invest in safer, newer facilities, but the money is tight. Safety versus cost, in a country that will always, eventually, shake.

Now let's turn to politics and economy here at home, where I want to spend a moment on rice — because it's turning into a genuine mess.

The Nikkei describes a rice crisis made worse by government policy that keeps zigzagging. Back in March 2025, officials released sixty percent of the national rice reserves to fight price spikes. Stockpiles have now fallen to thirty-two million tons — against a minimum of a hundred million. And the messaging to farmers has been all over the place. In August of last year, then-Agriculture Minister Shigeshige Ishiba said, produce more rice. By October, his successor, Norikazu Suzuki, pivoted to "demand-responsive production" — in other words, don't overdo it. Imagine you're a farmer trying to plan a season around that. And here's the gut punch: in some regions, rice is now selling at twenty to forty percent below what it costs to produce. That's not a market correction. That's a structural collapse in the making, and the people caught in the middle are the growers.

A couple of other domestic notes. The Takaichi government, through the trade ministry, is pushing tax incentives to get companies to transform themselves — letting firms defer capital gains taxes when they sell off a business division, as long as they reinvest the proceeds in growth areas within five years. The problem they're trying to fix is stark: the Nikkei says sixty-five percent of Japan's invested capital sits in what's bluntly called "value-destructive" segments. The idea is to make it easier to shed the dead weight and put money where it grows.

And the Defense Ministry has signed on with Sakana AI, a Tokyo startup, to use artificial intelligence for real-time intelligence gathering and analysis — speeding up command and control across the Self-Defense Forces. A homegrown AI company working on national defense; one to watch.

Overseas now, and the shadow over all of it is Washington.

President Trump is exploring talks with Kim Jong-un, apparently with an eye on the November midterms. And the Nikkei flags the danger for Japan and South Korea. Trump has been framing North Korea as a "nuclear-armed state" and talking about reducing military tension — not disarmament. That's a crucial difference. Tokyo and Seoul want actual denuclearization. If Washington settles for a "deal" that simply accepts North Korea's arsenal, it could split the US-Japan-Korea alliance and hand leverage to Beijing.

Trump is also swinging the tariff hammer at Canada — announcing a fifty percent duty on all Canadian vehicles, trucks and parts from January 2027. That shatters the framework of the USMCA trade pact, and Japanese automakers with big Canadian operations, Toyota and Honda among them, are bracing for a supply-chain scramble.

Two quick ones from Europe. Germany's Rhine River has dropped to sixty or seventy centimeters after record heat, forcing ships to lighten their loads. BASF is warning of shortages, and ING estimates it could shave three-tenths of a point off German growth. And New Zealand's Prime Minister Christopher Luxon is moving to ban under-sixteens from social media entirely, with fines of up to ten percent of global revenue for platforms that don't comply.

Before we go, the one that caught my eye — and it's a striking one. Switzerland, one of the wealthiest countries on earth, is facing what the Nikkei calls a "drug loss." Swiss pharmaceutical companies are increasingly skipping the local market — not applying for Swiss insurance coverage for their new drugs — and going straight to the US FDA instead. Why? Because Swiss prices lag American ones by as much as three times. Under Trump's "Most Favored Nation" pricing policy, Swiss drugmakers worry their margins will get squeezed if they're forced to match lower prices. The result is remarkable: thirty percent of newly approved drugs now lack Swiss insurance coverage. So in the home country of some of the world's biggest pharma giants, ordinary patients — the ones who aren't wealthy — can find themselves shut out of the very medicines their neighbors are making. A reminder that even in the richest places, access is never guaranteed.
