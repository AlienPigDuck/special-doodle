Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Sunday, the first of November, 2026, and we begin November with a story about money — specifically, where the world's money is going, and why so much of it is flowing through Tokyo.

Here's the headline. Foreign companies are borrowing yen at a pace Japan has never seen. The paper calls these "samurai bonds" — that's yen-denominated debt issued not by Japanese firms, but by overseas companies and institutions. In the six months from April through September, samurai bond issuance hit two-point-five-five trillion yen. That's a record for any half-year — and two-point-six times what we saw in the same stretch last year.

So who's borrowing, and why? The poster child here is Alphabet — Google's parent company. Back in May, Alphabet made its debut in the yen bond market with an issue worth five hundred and seventy-six-point-five billion yen. And the purpose of all this borrowing comes down to two letters: A, I. Artificial intelligence. The world's biggest cloud operators — what the industry calls "hyperscalers" — are spending staggering sums building data centers and developing AI, and they need capital wherever they can find it. Right now, Japan has it.

Why does this matter beyond the headline number? Because Japan's financial system has traditionally run on banks. Companies borrowed from banks; that was the model. Now foreign giants are coming directly to Japanese investors for their yen, and Nikkei reports a real concern: that Japanese companies themselves could find it harder to compete for funding at home, in their own currency. When Alphabet is in the room, you're bidding against Alphabet.

And that global appetite for capital connects to our second big story, because it's about the price of money in Japan — and a milestone thirteen years in the making. The Bank of Japan has announced that underlying inflation has reached its two percent target. For the first time since that goal was set, back in January of 2013.

Think about what that means. For a generation, Japan fought deflation — falling prices, a stagnant economy, the sense that tomorrow would be a little cheaper and a little smaller than today. The BOJ threw everything at it. Remember Governor Haruhiko Kuroda and his "quantitative easing" — essentially, the central bank printing money to buy government bonds on a massive scale. And here's the honest, slightly humbling part Nikkei points out: the two percent target wasn't really reached because of all that easing. It arrived on the back of external shocks — the COVID pandemic, and Russia's invasion of Ukraine, which drove up prices worldwide.

So the current governor, Kazuo Ueda — in office since April 2023 — inherits both a victory and a problem. The bank is now sitting on four hundred and seventy trillion yen in government bonds. That's roughly half of all the government debt outstanding. Unwinding that pile, without wrecking the stability he's just achieved, is a task with no instruction manual. Nobody has done this before, at this scale.

Which brings us neatly to the government's spending plans. Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi is pursuing what Nikkei describes as a three-front financial strategy, all to be settled by December. First, funding a cut to the consumption tax — bringing it from ten percent down to eight on food, for two years starting in April 2027. The price tag? About nine-point-six trillion yen a year. Second, accelerating defense spending. And third, backing growth investment in AI and semiconductors. Takaichi calls this "responsible expansionary fiscal policy" — and insists it can be done without new deficit spending. How you cut taxes and spend more without borrowing more is the question, and December will tell us her answer.

There's a sharp disagreement brewing over who pays for all this. Tokyo's governor, Yuriko Koike, has come out swinging against a national plan to redistribute more of Tokyo's wealth to other prefectures. She called that redistribution model "deflationary thinking" — her words. Her argument: Japan should grow the economic pie, not divide a shrinking one. She pushed back on the idea that Tokyo has a two-trillion-yen surplus to share, calling it "a number without substance." Tokyo, she notes, pays for security around national institutions and infrastructure for three million daily commuters. It's a real fault line — the capital versus the regions — and we'll be hearing more of it.

One more domestic note. The Takaichi government is eyeing a tax change aimed at overseas residents who own rental property or run parking lots here in Japan. Right now, if you don't live or keep an office in the country, you can earn real-estate income here and avoid a certain business tax. The plan, for the 2027 reform, would close that gap for larger operators — think ten or more apartment units. A fairness measure, modest in scale, but telling about the mood.

Now, a cautionary tale from the corporate world. GMO Internet Group — a major Japanese tech firm — suffered a serious cyberattack. And here's the sting: its own security subsidiary had warned management about the exact vulnerabilities, two years earlier, in April 2024. The flaws were in an FX autotrading service. The fixes weren't made in time. Attackers broke into nearly twenty-seven thousand servers, manipulated customer accounts, and demanded ten million dollars in ransom — about one-point-six billion yen. GMO refused to pay. And executives later admitted why they couldn't simply shut the system down to fix it: the whole selling point was twenty-four-hour, always-on trading. A warning, heeded too late.

Overseas now, briefly. In China, the Communist Party's 5th Plenum wrapped up on October 29th with thirty-three officials stripped of party membership — more than double the previous record. Sixteen of them were former military figures, and that detail has Japan and other neighbors watching closely, because purges in the military can mean instability. This comes as Xi Jinping consolidates power ahead of the 2027 Party Congress.

Diplomatically, a notable meeting: Japan's foreign minister, Toshimitsu Motegi, sat down with Russia's Sergey Lavrov in Atlanta — the first in-person talks between the two since before the Ukraine invasion. Motegi demanded the immediate release of a Hokkaido Shimbun reporter detained by Russia's security service. Lavrov gave no clarity, and Moscow repeated its line that Japan must drop its "non-friendly" stance first.

And a genuine bright spot from Southeast Asia. In the southern Philippines, the Bangsamoro autonomous region formally launched its government on October 30th — a peace milestone after nearly fifty years of separatist conflict in Mindanao. Japanese firms, including Sumitomo Metal Mining, are already eyeing nickel projects there. Though fourteen thousand armed combatants still await disarmament, so the peace is real but fragile.

And now, the one that caught my eye — tucked into the international pages. The United States, Japan, and South Korea have signed a cooperation pact on the Arctic.

The Arctic. Think about that for a moment. These are three Pacific nations, and yet here they are signing a framework on Arctic maritime cooperation and economic links. Why? Because as the ice retreats, new shipping lanes and new resource opportunities are opening at the top of the world — and Russia and China are both staking claims up there. For Japan, a country with no Arctic coastline, to be writing itself into the future of that frozen frontier tells you how much the map of global competition is being redrawn. The same agreement reaffirmed the goal of North Korea's complete denuclearization and renewed cybersecurity cooperation. But it's the Arctic line that stayed with me — a reminder that the race for the planet's resources is heading to places that were, until recently, simply too cold to matter.
