Our lead story this morning takes us to Russia. President Putin has signed a decree placing four Russian subsidiaries of Japan Tobacco — JT, as it's usually known — under what the Kremlin calls "temporary state management." Now, let me explain what that actually means, because it's a clever and rather brutal piece of leverage. JT still technically owns these businesses on paper. But it no longer controls them. Russia has effectively seized the keys while leaving the nameplate on the door.

And the stakes here are enormous. JT holds a 37 percent share of the Russian cigarette market — more than a third. Nikkei reports that these Moscow operations account for roughly a quarter of JT's adjusted operating profit, and about ten percent of its total sales. So this isn't a side business. It's one of the company's crown jewels. Investors understood that immediately — JT's stock crashed 19 percent on the news, a fall that reflects the loss of its single most profitable division.

Why is this happening now? This looks like part of a widening pattern of Russian pressure on Japan over Tokyo's support for Ukraine and its compliance with Western sanctions. Russia has been seizing the assets of European companies since 2023, and now a major Japanese firm has been pulled into that same machinery. It's a reminder that for companies that stayed in Russia after the invasion, there was never really a safe middle ground. JT bet it could keep operating quietly. That bet has now, in large part, failed.

Let's turn to the United States, because the political ground there has shifted dramatically, and the aftershocks are being felt in Tokyo.

The Democratic Party has won a comprehensive victory in the American midterm elections. President Trump now enters what we call a lame-duck phase — weakened, without a cooperative Congress for his remaining time in office. And Nikkei's analysis this morning is cautious about what that means for Japan. Without legislative support, Trump may lean harder on executive authority — and that could mean using trade negotiations and regional conflicts as leverage with partners like Japan. So the defeat offers Tokyo some relief, but no certainty. Japan's diplomatic planners are left watching an unusually unpredictable Washington.

And there's a fascinating subplot. Vice President Vance has publicly broken with the President over the Iran war. You'll recall the American military strikes on Iran back in February. Vance is now openly arguing that the economic fallout from that war — especially the inflation it unleashed — is what cost Republicans the election. That's a rare public disagreement, and Nikkei reads it as the opening move of 2028 succession politics. Vance appears to be repositioning himself as the true keeper of Trump's original "America First" message — distancing himself from the very policies the administration pursued.

Markets responded to all of this with real conviction. The S&P 500 jumped two percent. Investors are betting that a weakened Trump means smaller tax cuts and less defense spending — so healthcare and renewable energy stocks led the rally, while defense contractors fell.

But here's where it gets complicated for Japan — and it brings together several threads in today's paper. In the midterms, Texas, Ohio, and other states elected Democratic governors who campaigned on halting or restricting the construction of AI data centers. These are the vast facilities that power artificial intelligence, and they consume staggering amounts of electricity and water. If that building slows — by as much as 30 percent, according to Nikkei — the ripple reaches straight back to Japan's chipmakers. Japanese semiconductor stocks plunged ten percent on the news. Kioxia, the memory chip maker, dropped 18 percent. The logic is simple: fewer data centers, less demand for the chips that fill them.

Which makes the contrast with Arm Holdings all the more striking. The British chip design company posted record quarterly sales, up 27 percent, with licensing revenue up a full 50 percent — driven by exactly that AI data center boom. So you can see the tension in the market right now: the AI build-out has been a tremendous engine of growth, and investors are suddenly nervous about whether American politics is about to put sand in the gears.

Let's stay with corporate earnings, because it was a big day for Japan's biggest names.

Toyota raised its operating profit forecast for next fiscal year by four percent, to 3.9 trillion yen. The strength is coming from hybrid vehicles — those sell beautifully in the United States, and they've helped offset the pain of automotive tariffs. But there's a shadow on the horizon: a looming Canadian tariff increase of 50 percent that Nikkei warns could cut profits by 400 billion yen. Trade walls, again, are the recurring villain of today's paper.

Sony had a spectacular quarter — a record pure profit of 1.33 trillion yen, up 29 percent, powered by gaming and movies. And the company is pinning hopes on the nineteenth of this month, when "Grand Theft Auto 6" finally launches. That's one of the most anticipated video games in history, and Sony expects it to keep the momentum going — though PlayStation 6 development costs are set to rise.

Nintendo tells a more mixed story. Sales of its Switch 2 console fell 37 percent year on year, even with price increases. But software saved the day — titles like "Tomodachi Collection" and "Rhythm Heaven" kept profits growing. It's the classic Nintendo pattern: the magic is in the games, not the hardware.

Now, before I close, I want to spend a moment on the story that caught my eye — and it's one that sits quietly deep in the paper, far from the headlines about hacks and earnings.

Nikkei reports that uninsured individual deposits in Japan have ballooned to 140 trillion yen. Let me explain. In Japan, your bank deposits are protected up to 10 million yen if your bank fails. Anything above that threshold is uninsured. And the amount of money sitting above that line has surged over five years — it now represents a full quarter of all personal deposits in the country.

Why? An aging, increasingly wealthy population. Inheritance passing down, real estate being sold, cash concentrating in fewer and older hands. And here's the thing that struck me: that 10-million-yen protection ceiling hasn't changed in 40 years. Four decades of inflation, and the safety net has stayed exactly the same size.

In the old days, the worry was moral hazard — that protecting deposits too generously would make banks reckless. But the world has changed. Today, with digital banking, money can move instantly. We saw what that looked like in 2023, when Silicon Valley Bank collapsed in America — a mid-size bank undone by a wave of deposits fleeing at the speed of a smartphone. Nikkei's point is sobering: Japan now has a growing pool of nervous, unprotected money, concentrated in the hands of the elderly, that could move just as fast if confidence ever cracked. And almost no policymaker seems to be watching it. It's the kind of risk that stays invisible — right up until the moment it isn't.
