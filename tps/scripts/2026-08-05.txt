Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Wednesday, the fifth of August, 2026, and there's a lot moving in the world of money this morning, so let's get right into it.

Our lead story is a big one, and it's about the yen. Late last week — on the thirtieth and thirty-first of July — Japan and the United States carried out what the Nikkei is calling a historic joint intervention in the currency markets. Japan deployed somewhere between twelve and thirteen trillion yen, buying up its own currency to push back against a slide that had become genuinely alarming. The yen had weakened to 163.5 to the dollar. That's near a forty-year low.

Now, here's what makes this remarkable. This is the first time in roughly twenty-eight years that Washington and Tokyo have acted together on the currency. Japan intervenes on its own fairly often, but the United States joining in — that's rare, and it sends a message. And there's a clever mechanism underneath it. The US activated something called the FIMA Repo Facility — that's a Federal Reserve tool that lets foreign central banks borrow dollars using their holdings as collateral. In plain terms, it means Japan could keep buying yen without burning through its foreign reserves to do it. The dollars were on loan.

So why now? A few reasons all landing at once. There's the sheer scale of the yen's weakness. There's rising tension with Iran — and we'll come back to that. And there's the American political calendar: US midterm elections are coming in November, and a spike in oil prices, or a currency in freefall, is exactly the kind of thing an administration wants to head off. Both governments are signaling they're serious about halting this.

And that yen weakness shows up everywhere in today's paper — including, interestingly, as good news for one very large company. Toyota has revised its profit forecast upward. For the fiscal year, it now expects net profit of 3.25 trillion yen, up from three trillion. The reason is largely the weak yen. Toyota had been assuming an exchange rate of 150 to the dollar; it's now assuming 160. That single change adds an estimated 480 billion yen to operating profit, because Toyota earns so much abroad and books it back in cheaper yen.

The underlying business is holding up too — strong North American sales, robust demand for hybrids. China, though, is a soft spot, with sales down twenty-eight percent. And analysts are cautious for another reason: the United States accounts for about a quarter of Toyota's sales, which means any revision to the USMCA trade deal — the North American trade pact — could cut both ways.

Let's stay with money a moment longer, because there's a fascinating story on the front page about Japan's banks competing for your deposits. For years, Japanese savers earned essentially nothing. That's changing. Mizuho will raise deposit rates to 1.5 percent for large customers — specifically people who've recently sold real estate. That's triple its standard rate. MUFG is offering bonuses on big inheritance and retirement deposits; Sumitomo Mitsui is offering three and a half percent on short-term deposits.

But here's the catch, and it matters. This competition is really about the big accounts. Rates on deposits over a hundred million yen have climbed to around 0.89 percent, while small savers get less than half a percent. That gap has nearly doubled. And the money is flowing toward Tokyo and the major financial centers, which leaves regional banks squeezed. So we're watching a two-tier system emerge — good news if you're wealthy and in a big city, harder if you're a small saver at a regional bank.

Now to politics and the economy here at home. Prime Minister Takaichi will attend the atomic bomb memorial ceremonies — Hiroshima tomorrow, on the sixth, and Nagasaki on the ninth — reaffirming Japan's commitment to nuclear non-proliferation. The cabinet has also approved 24.2 billion yen in emergency funding for earthquake recovery in Kumamoto, with the aim of resolving water shortages there by the end of the month.

And a notable move on taxes: the government is expected to approve a cut to the consumption tax on food, bringing it down to just one percent, effective April 2027. Formal party approval is targeted for September. That's a meaningful step, given how much cost-of-living pressure households are feeling.

Speaking of which, there's encouraging news on wages. The Keidanren — Japan's big business federation — reports that spring wage negotiations delivered an average increase of 5.37 percent, nearly twenty thousand yen more per worker. This is the third year running above five percent, and the paper notes that's the first such streak since the bubble years, 1989 to 1991. Tight labor supply is doing the work here. The information and communications sector led the way at over eight percent.

But — and there's always a but — the government's own Economic and Fiscal White Paper flags a worry. The ability to pass rising costs through into prices is faltering in the public sector. Government price pass-through has fallen to 48.4 percent, now below the private sector. And unsuccessful public works bids are climbing — 336 last fiscal year — because contractors, facing higher costs and labor shortages, simply can't make the numbers work at the offered price. It's a quiet sign of strain underneath the good headline wage figures.

Now let's turn abroad, and to that Iran tension I mentioned. President Trump claims negotiations with Iran are ongoing and, in his words, "just beginning" — pointing to mediation from Saudi Arabia, the UAE, and Qatar. But the picture is murky. The CIA reportedly says the US military is exploring what it calls "unconventional" attack methods, which hardly suggests talks are going smoothly. Iran, for its part, denies there are any talks at all. Trump appears keen to avoid an oil price spike before November — which, again, ties right back to why we're seeing all this currency intervention.

And you can see the effect in the oil market itself. Saudi Aramco reported that its production fell twenty-eight percent in the second quarter — down to 7.56 million barrels a day — because of a blockade in the Strait of Hormuz. And yet its profit rose forty-four percent, to 32.6 billion dollars. How? Prices. Crude averaged over 108 dollars a barrel, up from around 67 a year earlier. Less oil, far more money.

One more from Europe before I finish. In Germany, the far-right Alternative for Germany party is exploiting years of economic malaise and high energy costs by calling to repair the Nord Stream pipeline and bring back Russian gas. It's a striking reversal. Germany's growth has stalled near zero, and the old reliance on Russian energy — championed under Angela Merkel — now looks, in hindsight, like a strategic mistake. Poland, once fiercely opposed to Nord Stream, is quietly cooling its investigation into the 2022 sabotage, which only muddies the water further.

And now, the story that caught my eye. It's tucked into the security pages, and it's about artificial intelligence going to war. Japan's Self-Defense Forces will begin adopting American AI systems for command and control — specifically Palantir's Maven and Anduril's Lattice platforms — to close a decision-making gap with the US military. But here's the elegant part. To avoid becoming dependent on any single American system, Japan will use several of them, and at the center — acting as what the paper calls a "control tower" — will sit a homegrown Japanese system from a company called Sakana AI. The Japanese system routes decisions to the right American tool. It's a very Japanese solution, really: embrace the foreign technology you need, but keep your own hand firmly on the wheel.
