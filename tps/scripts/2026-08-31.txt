It's Monday, the thirty-first of August, 2026, and we've got a rich paper today — a big shift in how Uniqlo wants to sell you clothes, a strong new poll for the prime minister, and a warning, buried deep in the pages, about the slow-motion collapse of Japan's fishing industry. Let's get into it.

We'll start with a company almost everyone listening has probably bought something from. Fast Retailing — the parent of Uniqlo — is rethinking what a store is even for. Nikkei reports this morning that over the next ten years, Uniqlo plans to halve the number of conventional stores it opens in Japan while doubling its flagship stores, up to around twenty. These are the big, showcase locations in major cities.

Now, why does that matter? Because it's a bet on a very different future. For years, Uniqlo built its name on cheap, mass-produced, well-made basics — the kind of thing you grab and go. But Chairman Yanai Masayuki wants to move the brand upmarket, toward being a premium lifestyle destination. Something you visit, not just shop at.

And there's hard logic behind it. Japan's population is shrinking. E-commerce has more or less saturated the market — everyone who wants to buy online already is. So if you're going to keep a physical store open, that store has to offer something a website can't: an experience. Uniqlo has already seen this work abroad. In North America and Europe, flagship stores generate about twenty percent of the company's global sales, and they're growing at double-digit rates. So the question the Nikkei raises is whether this becomes a template — not just for Uniqlo, but for Japanese retail as a whole, which has been searching for a way forward as customers dwindle.

From the shopping street to the halls of government. Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi is having a good week, at least by the numbers. Her cabinet's approval rating jumped four points to sixty-two percent in the latest Nikkei-TV Tokyo poll, conducted over the weekend. That's the first time she's been back above sixty since July. The most striking detail is where the support came from — non-partisan voters, the people who don't align with any party, surged eleven points, up to forty-seven percent. And it was especially strong among younger voters.

But hold that good news next to a warning sign in the very same poll. Sixty-four percent of respondents say they're worried that cutting the consumption tax will end up hurting social security. And that's the exact fault line Takaichi is walking. Her government is planning to cut the consumption tax on food to one percent, starting in April 2027, for a two-year period. Popular, certainly. But there's a catch — ten trillion yen in funding to pay for it that's still, as of now, unaccounted for.

That's an enormous hole. And Nikkei notes that bond markets are watching with growing alarm. If the government fills that gap by borrowing, investors get nervous about Japan's already-heavy debt. So Takaichi has strong public support — but she's promised a tax cut people like, funded by measures people fear could weaken the safety net they rely on. That tension is going to define her government in the months ahead.

Staying with the government and something that touches every dinner table: rice. The Agriculture Ministry will repurchase around two hundred thousand tons of stockpiled rice by September — more than the hundred and fifty thousand tons originally planned. Here's the background. Japan keeps an emergency rice reserve, and it's fallen to a record low of three hundred and twenty thousand tons. That's raised real food-security concerns.

But there's a wrinkle worth understanding. Earlier, the government released rice from its reserves to calm prices — and that pushed down prices for the 2025 crop. So now there's plenty of rice sitting in private hands, even as the official reserve runs thin. By buying rice back, the ministry is trying to do two things at once: rebuild its cushion, and steady prices for the new harvest so farmers don't get squeezed. A balancing act.

Let's take a quick tour abroad, where there were a few genuinely surprising results. In Iceland, voters rejected opening negotiations to join the European Union. Fifty-two point eight percent said no, on a remarkably high turnout of eighty-two and a half percent. What's notable is that Iceland's own government called this referendum — and now has to respect a no. Icelanders chose sovereignty and control over their fisheries over the economic case for joining.

Meanwhile, Vietnam is reshaping its map. Two northern provinces, Quang Ninh and Bac Ninh, are being elevated to directly-controlled cities. It's part of a bigger plan to streamline administration and pull in foreign investment — Nikkei reports that by 2030, up to a third of Vietnam's provinces could become direct municipalities.

And a heartbreaking one from the Himalayas. China and Nepal are coordinating disaster recovery after catastrophic floods and landslides. The toll so far — eight hundred and four dead, more than three thousand still missing. Chinese Foreign Minister Wang Yi has pledged cooperation on rescue and reconstruction.

Back home, a couple of quick business notes that speak to how Japan is adapting to fewer workers. FamilyMart — one of the big convenience-store chains — is rolling out switchable registers across all roughly sixteen thousand of its stores starting in September. These can flip between self-checkout and staffed checkout depending on how busy things are. The company says it cuts employee checkout time by about twenty percent. In a country short on labor, that flexibility is the whole point.

And Japan has raised its mandatory disability-employment rate, from two-and-a-half percent to two-point-seven percent. Companies are now required to hire more people with disabilities — and Nikkei highlights firms, like Bolashare in Miyazaki, investing in real workplace support and career paths, not just meeting a quota.

Which brings me to the one that caught my eye — and it's a serious one, tucked deep in the paper. Japan's fishing industry is in structural collapse. Professor Katsukawa Toshio, of Tokyo University of Marine Science and Technology, lays out the grim numbers: fishery output has fallen seventy-five percent since its peak in 1984. And here's the crucial insight — it's not primarily about a shortage of fishers. It's resource depletion. Japan has simply overfished.

The professor's argument is that government policy is stuck in what he calls "patch economics" — endless subsidies for diesel, loan forgiveness, hatchery restocking — all propping up a fleet that's too big for the fish that remain. What Japan avoids is the painful medicine: firm, science-based catch limits. He points to Norway, which in the 1970s imposed a near-total moratorium — brutal at the time, but it let stocks recover. Japan passed a Fisheries Law in 2018 promising exactly that kind of science-based management, but Katsukawa says implementation remains toothless. The one bright spot? Pacific bluefin tuna, which has recovered thanks to international discipline — proof that restraint works when it's actually enforced.

And the deeper point is this: depopulation lets overcapacity linger, while that same overcapacity keeps the industry from ever becoming sustainable enough to hold rural communities together. A quiet crisis, worth watching.
