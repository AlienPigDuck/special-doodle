Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Friday, the thirteenth of November, 2026, and we have a genuine earthquake out of Silicon Valley to begin with. So let's get right to it.

The headline everyone is reading this morning: Jensen Huang has resigned as chief executive of NVIDIA. If that name doesn't mean much to you, let me put it plainly — NVIDIA makes the chips that power artificial intelligence, and it controls more than seventy percent of that global market. Huang co-founded the company. He built it. And for the last several years he has been, arguably, the single most important person in the entire AI boom.

And now he's gone, and the circumstances are extraordinary. Nikkei reports that NVIDIA's board announced the resignation on November twelfth, following revelations of alleged affairs — one of them, reportedly, with a woman suspected of Chinese espionage, and another with an executive inside the company. The board had been investigating whether Huang violated company policy and whether sensitive information may have leaked. The chief financial officer, Colette Kress, steps in as interim chief executive while the search for a permanent successor begins.

Now, why does this matter so much? Markets gave their answer immediately. NVIDIA stock dropped fourteen percent on the news. Huang wasn't just a manager — he was a dealmaker. He personally brokered the flow of AI investment, met directly with customers, and in many ways he was the face that reassured the world that this enormous, expensive build-out of AI infrastructure would pay off. Take him out of the picture, and you introduce uncertainty into the whole edifice.

And here's what's fascinating in the Nikkei's reporting. While NVIDIA fell, its rivals — AMD and Broadcom — rose. Investors are already reallocating. And this comes at a delicate moment for American technology more broadly. The so-called "Magnificent Seven" mega-cap tech giants have, for the first time in four years, seen their profit growth fall behind the rest of the S&P 500. The big tech names grew profits around twenty-three percent; the other four hundred and ninety-three companies in the index grew thirty-three percent. And Nikkei flags something called "shadow debt" — some three-point-two trillion dollars in unfinalized contracts and leases sitting beneath these companies' balance sheets. Translation: the market is beginning to ask whether the AI spending spree has run ahead of itself.

That question has a political dimension, too. In last week's US midterms, Democrats took control of both chambers of Congress and several key states. And one of the surprising grassroots stories is a backlash against data centers — the vast warehouses of computers that AI requires. Communities are objecting to how much energy and water these facilities consume. So the regulatory tailwind that AI has enjoyed may be turning into a headwind, just as its most famous champion exits the stage.

Let me turn now to Japan, and to a government that is thinking very hard about security — energy security, economic security, and national defense.

First, energy. Nikkei reports this morning that Japan is leading an effort to build a shared oil stockpiling system across Southeast Asia — across the ASEAN bloc. Storage facilities are planned in Indonesia and Thailand by 2027, using what's called a "ticket mechanism" — essentially, member states can buy the right to purchase emergency oil at pre-set rates. The goal is to reduce the region's dependence on Middle Eastern supplies. And the context here is sharp: back in February, military clashes between the United States and Iran disrupted shipments. Prime Minister Takaichi will promote this framework at the upcoming ASEAN summits.

Second, economic security — and this one is striking. The Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry is studying a plan to, in effect, nationalize aging private factories that produce materials vital to the economy — things like chemicals and metal castings. The government would buy the equipment through independent agencies, pay to modernize it, then hand the facilities back to private operators. This follows a model used in the US and UK called "government-owned, contractor-operated." The worry it addresses is this: Japan's supply chains sometimes depend on a single, aging factory — and if that one plant goes down, so does the supply. A related economic security bill will tighten government oversight of critical supply chains at high-tech startups and mid-sized manufacturers, guarding against both China's export controls and broader geopolitical shocks.

And third, defense. Takaichi is preparing to meet President Trump — at the APEC summit in Manila next week, and possibly again at the G20 in December. Nikkei reports she's bracing for pressure to sharply raise Japan's military spending, and she intends to explain Japan's own defense strategy to preempt those demands.

All of this unfolds against a weakening yen. The Japanese currency is edging toward a hundred and sixty per dollar, with the dollar near eighteen-month highs on expectations that the US Federal Reserve will raise rates again in December. At home, the Bank of Japan's deputy governor, Asada — appointed by Takaichi — is urging caution. He warns against raising Japanese rates too quickly, arguing the current policy rate of one-point-two-five percent is already within a reasonable range, and that moving faster could choke off economic momentum.

A quick turn now through the corporate pages, where there's a story of reinvention. The restaurant chain Watami — known for its izakaya pubs, those casual Japanese bars that serve food and drink — is going private. Founder and chairman Yoshiki Watanabe is leading a management buyout valuing the company at around three hundred and sixty-six billion yen. Here's the interesting part: food delivery now makes up forty-four percent of Watami's sales, and the company bought the Subway sandwich franchise in Japan back in 2024, with plans to reach a thousand Subway outlets by 2034. Going private, Watanabe says, lets him invest for the long term without the pressure of quarterly earnings.

Not every corporate story is so upbeat. Sony's financial arm is feeling the drag of an internal fraud investigation — new policy sales at Sony Life fell eleven percent year-on-year as the probe into improper money-handling disrupts the sales teams.

Internationally, a few quick notes. The Ukraine peace talks remain stalled — a US envoy met Putin in Moscow and Zelenskyy in Dublin, but the territorial disputes haven't budged. China's Xi Jinping, marking the hundred-and-sixtieth anniversary of Sun Yat-sen's birth, again called unification with Taiwan a "sacred historical task." And in Indonesia, Vice President Gibran — the son of former president Joko Widodo — has resigned amid a constitutional audit over, of all things, missing proof of his high school diploma. That deepens the rift between the old and new administrations in Jakarta.

And now, the one that caught my eye this morning — tucked into the markets pages. The Japanese news app SmartNews is heading to the stock market on December fifteenth. And it is a sobering debut. SmartNews will list at one thousand three hundred and ninety yen a share, valuing it at around forty-three billion yen. Now, hold that number in your mind, because back in 2021, SmartNews was valued at more than two hundred billion yen. That's a so-called "down round" — the company is going public worth roughly eighty percent less than it was at its peak just five years ago.

I find this quietly telling. SmartNews was a darling of Japan's startup scene. And here it is, listing at a fraction of its former glory, in an era when all the money, all the hype, has rushed toward artificial intelligence. It's a small story, but it captures something real about where we are — the money moves in waves, and the last wave's champions can be left high and dry. Which, on the very morning Jensen Huang stepped down, feels like more than a coincidence.
