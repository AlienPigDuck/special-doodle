Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Thursday, the thirtieth of July, 2026, and there's a heavy story leading the paper today, so let's begin there.

Two days ago, on the twenty-eighth of July, a magnitude seven earthquake struck Kumamoto Prefecture, on the island of Kyushu in southwestern Japan. At least twelve people have died, and an extraordinary twenty-five million people are now under evacuation orders. For anyone who follows Japan, the location alone brings back painful memories. Kumamoto was hit by a devastating earthquake almost exactly ten years ago, in 2016. And now it's happened again.

The human toll is what matters first, and it's serious. But Nikkei this morning is also tracing the ripples this quake sends far beyond Kyushu — because Kumamoto isn't just any prefecture. Over the last decade, it's become one of the most important places on Earth for making computer chips. TSMC, the Taiwanese semiconductor giant, built major facilities there. So did Sony and Renesas Electronics. And all three have now halted production.

Here's why that reaches into your life, wherever you're listening from. This is happening at the peak of global demand for chips to power artificial intelligence. When TSMC stops in Kumamoto, the effects don't stay in Kumamoto. Toyota and Honda have already suspended car production through tomorrow, the thirty-first. And there's a quieter problem too. The port of Yatsushiro suffered what engineers call liquefaction — that's when the ground turns almost liquid during violent shaking, and everything built on it sinks or tilts. Yatsushiro is where Japan imports the high-pressure gases and specialty chemicals that chipmaking depends on. Damage there could stretch these disruptions out well beyond the factories themselves. So this is a natural disaster and a supply chain story at the same time, and we'll be watching both.

Let me turn now to a very different kind of slow-moving story — but one that may matter even more over the long run. For the first time in forty-two years, Japan's population has fallen below 120 million. As of the first of January, it stood at 119.74 million. The last time it was this low was 1984.

Dig into the numbers Nikkei gives us and you find two Japans moving in opposite directions. The number of Japanese nationals fell by nearly 917,000 people — that's seventeen straight years of decline, and the steepest drop ever recorded. But foreign residents surged to a record 4.03 million, up more than 350,000. Foreign arrivals hit an all-time high. And here's the part worth sitting with: while the Japanese population is aging, more than eighty-six percent of foreign residents are of working age. Foreigners now make up 3.26 percent of everyone living in Japan. That's still a small share by the standards of most Western countries — but for Japan, it represents a real transformation, and it's the reason the total population isn't falling even faster than it is.

That demographic picture connects to a lot of what else is in the paper. Prime Minister Takaichi's government is trying to keep the economy growing in the face of a shrinking, aging workforce, and today it laid out the details of a policy meant to ease the squeeze on households. Nikkei reports the consumption tax on food will be cut to just one percent for two years, starting in April 2027. Japan's consumption tax is like a national sales tax, and food is a big part of every family's budget, so this is real relief for people who've been living with inflation. The government had been vague about the timing before — now it's on the calendar.

On wages, though, the news is more modest. The minimum wage will rise to 1,176 yen an hour next year — about a five percent increase, and the smallest in six years. Inflation is cooling, and with it, the pressure that the Takaichi administration had been putting on employers to race toward 1,500 yen an hour has eased somewhat.

And where the government is spending freely is on the future. Nikkei reports a new investment framework — they're calling it "Strong and Prosperous Japan" — worth 2.7 trillion yen for the coming fiscal year, with no upper limits on money going into AI, semiconductors, and next-generation energy. The message is clear: this government is choosing growth and strategic bets over trimming the national debt.

Which brings us naturally to China, and a striking front-page piece. Nikkei's annual survey of world market share finds Chinese companies gaining ground in twenty-five of sixty-seven key products. CATL now makes more than forty percent of the world's EV batteries. BYD has overtaken Tesla in electric vehicle sales. And here's the thing that jumps out: the Trump administration's tariffs — some as high as 145 percent — haven't stopped this. Chinese firms simply redirected their exports toward Southeast Asia. China's trade surplus last year hit a record 1.2 trillion dollars. The United States still leads in twenty-three product categories, but that's down from twenty-seven. Japan holds ten.

The rest of the international news is dominated by the Middle East, and it's grim. A four-day ceasefire brokered by the Trump administration collapsed on the twenty-eighth, when Iran's Revolutionary Guard fired ballistic missiles at a U.S. base in Jordan. The U.S. says all were intercepted, but Washington is signaling major retaliation. Iran is now demanding complete control of shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. And Yemen's Houthi militia attacked Saudi oil tankers, enforcing what they're calling a maritime blockade. Oil has spiked above eighty dollars a barrel — and you can already see that flowing into Japanese corporate earnings. Chubu Electric cut its profit forecast by thirty percent this week, citing exactly that Middle East fuel volatility.

There's also a story here that touches Japan directly. Under U.S. pressure, five countries — Chad, Mali, Niger, Burkina Faso, and Venezuela — have announced they're withdrawing from the International Criminal Court, with Secretary of State Rubio vowing to, in his words, "dismantle" it. Japan is the court's single largest financial backer, contributing 4.5 billion yen a year, and Tokyo firmly opposes any withdrawal. So this is a moment where Japan and Washington are quietly pulling in different directions.

A couple of quick ones before I close. Peru swore in Keiko Fujimori as president — the daughter of jailed former leader Alberto Fujimori, a name with deep resonance in Japan given his Japanese heritage. And in France's Gironde region, historic wildfires have grown so intense they're generating their own storm clouds — pyrocumulus, they're called — which whip up unpredictable winds and spread the flames even faster. More than 300,000 people have been displaced.

And now, the one that caught my eye. Tucked into the space news, Nikkei reports that Mitsubishi Heavy Industries flew a stripped-down version of its H3 rocket last month — the "30-configuration," they call it. What makes it remarkable is what's missing: the solid rocket boosters. This version flies on its main engine alone, and by doing so, it cuts the cost of a launch roughly in half, from around ten billion yen down to a target of five billion. Commercial flights could begin as early as next month.

Why does that matter? Because for years, the story of space has been SpaceX doing what everyone else said was too expensive. Japan has been playing catch-up. But a launch at half the price starts to make Japan genuinely competitive — and there are customers waiting. The lunar startup ispace is already planning to ride the H3 to the moon in 2028. Not every Japanese rocket program is thriving — IHI's Epsilon-S has suffered two test explosions and long delays. But the H3 quietly turning the corner on cost? That's the kind of unglamorous engineering win that changes an industry.
