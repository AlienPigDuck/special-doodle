We begin this morning with a story about money, trust, and how you pay the people who sell you a promise.

Prudential Life Insurance in Japan has announced a sweeping overhaul of how it pays its sales force. And this isn't a minor tweak — it's the company dismantling the very model it was built on. Nikkei reports that Prudential is effectively abolishing what's called its "full commission" system. That's a setup where salespeople earn primarily by landing contracts — the more policies they sell, the more they make.

Now, why the sudden change? Because of a serious scandal. More than a hundred employees and former employees are alleged to have misappropriated around 3.1 billion yen — that's roughly twenty million dollars — from customers. And the company has come to a difficult conclusion: that its own pay structure may have driven the misconduct. When your entire income depends on closing deals, the temptation to cut corners — or worse — grows.

So here's the fix. Prudential will introduce a guaranteed monthly income of about 300,000 yen — a base salary, essentially, where there wasn't really one before. It will spread out the bonuses for winning contracts over several years, rather than paying them all at once. And crucially, it will tie compensation to compliance and customer satisfaction — not just sales volume.

This matters beyond one insurer. Japan's financial industry has been wrestling with a series of trust scandals in recent years, and the question underneath all of them is the same: what behavior are we actually rewarding? Prudential is still waiting on a third-party committee's final report before locking in every detail. But the direction is clear, and it's a significant admission.

From corporate accountability to the national budget — and a policy that will touch every grocery bill in Japan.

Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi says she'll lay out her position on a food consumption tax cut within the week. The plan on the table: a one percent reduction in the consumption tax on food, starting in April of 2027, lasting two years. For listeners abroad, the consumption tax is Japan's version of a national sales tax, and food is a sensitive spot — it hits lower-income households hardest.

But this is proving politically messy. Cross-party talks at the Social Security National Council broke down, with opposition parties rejecting the government's framework. The Democratic Party argues that a delayed, temporary cut isn't good enough — they want immediate relief instead. And Takaichi has told her own Liberal Democratic Party to sort out two things fast: where the money will come from, and whether the party even agrees internally.

The politics here aren't happening in a vacuum. A new Nikkei–TV Asahi poll shows the Takaichi cabinet's support has dropped ten points, down to 58 percent. Voters cite confusion over policy and a sense that the administration isn't prioritizing the squeeze on ordinary household budgets. Fifty-eight percent is still a healthy number, but a ten-point fall in one stretch is the kind of thing that concentrates a politician's attention.

Now, a story from the front page that I want to sit with, because it's genuinely sobering.

Nikkei carries a major analysis of the recent U.S. air campaign against Iran — and it argues this was the first large-scale military operation where artificial intelligence played a central role in targeting. The AI didn't just assist. It recommended attack routes. It recommended which weapons to use.

And it made a catastrophic mistake. The system targeted what turned out to be a primary school, killing more than 150 people. The cause? It relied on satellite imagery that was seven years old. The building's use had changed. The machine didn't know that — and the humans didn't catch it.

There's a term for this that the piece highlights: "automation bias." It's the very human tendency to trust the machine's recommendation without questioning it. When a system is right most of the time, we stop double-checking. The analysis recalls warnings from figures like Stephen Hawking and Elon Musk, who described autonomous weapons as potentially the "third revolution in warfare," after gunpowder and nuclear arms. This story is a grim, concrete illustration of why they worried.

And there's related news: Iranian officials have told Reuters that Tehran will hold off on further retaliation, provided Washington stops its attacks. U.S. forces have suspended the air campaign after thirteen straight days — reportedly citing ammunition running low and diminishing returns. So a fragile pause, at least.

Let's turn to the global economy, where one theme keeps surfacing: artificial intelligence, and who's paying for it.

Alphabet — Google's parent company — reported something remarkable. Its free cash flow went negative for the first time ever, to the tune of 5.8 billion dollars in the second quarter. Now, this is a company that also posted record profits of 112 billion dollars. So how does that square? The answer is spending. Alphabet is pouring enormous sums into AI infrastructure — the data centers and chips that power these systems — and investors are starting to ask whether that pace is sustainable. The result was a selloff in tech stocks worldwide.

Meanwhile, the companies selling the picks and shovels are thriving. China's CATL, the world's largest EV battery maker, posted a 42 percent jump in profit, to 43.2 billion yuan — driven partly by demand from AI data centers, which are hungry for power storage. And in Taiwan, nineteen major IT firms saw combined sales grow 57 percent in June, with server makers leading the way. So the AI boom is very real for suppliers — even as the buyers face harder questions about the bill.

It's a striking sign of how deep frustration over jobs and inequality runs.

And a heartbreaking one: an Ebola outbreak in eastern Democratic Republic of Congo has killed more than a thousand people in just three months — a faster pace than the devastating West African crisis a decade ago. In a conflict zone, 80 percent of transmission routes remain unknown, and most deaths are happening before people ever reach a hospital.

Closer to home, two quick items. Kirin Holdings is bringing its probiotic functional drink to Singapore, with plans to expand across Southeast Asia — part of a push to double its health science business by 2030. And now, the one that caught my eye — buried deeper in today's paper.

DeepSeek — the Chinese AI startup that made waves not long ago — has quietly suspended its fundraising. And here's what's interesting: it wasn't for lack of ambition. The company had been aiming to raise up to 16 billion yuan, on a valuation of 71 billion dollars, right after closing a seven-billion-dollar round in June. That's a company with the wind at its back. And yet it paused.

Nikkei frames this as a reflection of China's strategic uncertainty around AI. When a red-hot startup with a soaring valuation chooses to stop taking money, it tells you something — about caution, about political weather, about a sense that the ground may be shifting. In a week where Alphabet's spending spooked markets, DeepSeek's restraint is the flip side of the same anxious coin. Everyone is asking how much AI is really worth, and how fast is too fast.
