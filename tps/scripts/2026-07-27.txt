We begin this morning with a story that says a great deal about who really lives in Japan's most glamorous addresses — and the answer, it turns out, is often nobody.

The Nikkei conducted an investigation into roughly 300 high-rise residential buildings in the centers of Tokyo and Osaka. These are the tower apartments — in Japanese, "tower mansion," though "mansion" here just means a large apartment building — the kind of buildings that dominate the skyline, the ones with the panoramic views and the sky-high prices. And the paper looked specifically at the top-floor units. The penthouses.

What they found is striking. Of 1,867 top-floor units examined, 895 — that's 48 percent — showed a mismatch between the property and the owner's registered address. Nearly half of these penthouse owners live somewhere else. Many are registered overseas, or in another region of Japan entirely.

In other words, these homes aren't homes. They're investments. And the reason isn't hard to find. The yen remains weak, which means that to a foreign buyer, Japanese property looks like a bargain — prime real estate in a world-class city, discounted by the exchange rate.

Now, why does this matter beyond the obvious? Here's the thing. When you buy into one of these buildings, you're not just buying an apartment — you're joining a community that has to make decisions together. Repairs, renovations, long-term maintenance funds, building management. All of that requires owners who show up, who care, who can be reached. When half the owners are absentee investors scattered across the globe, that consensus-building becomes very difficult. And over time, that can erode the value of the very asset the investors came for. It's a slow problem, but a real one — and it strikes at the heart of what it means to live in Japan's most desirable neighborhoods.

Let's turn to politics, where Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi is having a difficult week.

According to a Nikkei-TV Tokyo survey conducted over the weekend, her cabinet's approval rating has fallen sharply — from 68 percent in June down to 58 percent now. That's a ten-point drop, and the Nikkei notes it's the steepest single decline since September of 2022. Disapproval climbed ten points, to 37 percent.

The most telling number is among independent voters — the unaffiliated, roughly a fifth of the electorate, the people who don't reliably back any party. Among them, disapproval now outpaces approval, at 51 percent. That's a fifteen-point swing against her. Even within her own Liberal Democratic Party, support slipped seven points, though it remains strong at 87 percent.

So what happened? The government's own read is that voters see its priorities as disconnected from daily life. Two issues come up specifically: proposed measures on subsidiary capital, and reforms to imperial succession. These are the kinds of debates that occupy Nagatachō — Japan's political center — but feel remote to households worried about the cost of living. And 58 percent is still a healthy number in absolute terms. But the direction of travel is what will worry Takaichi's team.

Now, from politics to industrial strategy — because Japan is making a serious bet on the future of artificial intelligence, and it's placing that bet in Kumamoto.

Mitsui Fudosan, one of Japan's largest real estate developers, will establish what it's calling the Physical AI and Semiconductor Foundation Center — PASTEC — in Kumamoto Prefecture by 2030. The idea is to bring together Japanese and Taiwanese semiconductor firms to develop the next generation of AI chips — specifically for autonomous machines and industrial robots. So-called "physical AI" — intelligence that acts in the real world, not just on a screen.

Kumamoto is a deliberate choice. This is where the Taiwanese chip giant TSMC has been building its Japanese operations, and PASTEC would combine TSMC's expertise with Japanese equipment makers and Kyushu manufacturers like Yasukawa Electric, a leader in industrial robotics. The subtext here is economic security — securing critical technology at home, so Japan can compete with China and the United States rather than depend on them.

And staying with the theme of Japan's technological sovereignty — the Swedish enterprise software firm IFS has announced partnerships with NEC and IBM Japan to build a domestically controlled cloud platform called "Kaname," launching in November. The name means something like "linchpin" or "keystone" in Japanese. The purpose is data sovereignty — giving Japanese companies AI infrastructure that keeps their data under domestic control. It's part of a broader anxiety, felt worldwide, about where exactly your data lives when you hand it to an AI system.

Two more from the world of business, briefly. Honda and Nissan — Japan's storied rivals — will jointly develop the operating system for future software-defined vehicles, and they'll build it on Nissan's technology. Sharing the enormous cost of software development is how they hope to compete against fast-moving Chinese EV makers. And Coursera, the online education platform, has begun offering AI-dubbed Japanese versions of university lectures — translated and even lip-synced — from partners like the University of Michigan and Google, with plans for more than 500 courses by year's end.

And now, the one that caught my eye — a story buried deeper in the paper that I think deserves far more attention than it's getting.

Japan is quietly running out of nurses.

The Nikkei reports that over the past decade, applicants to nursing programs have collapsed by nearly 31 percent — down to 141,234 in 2025. Now, Japan's population of 18-year-olds shrank too, but only by about 9 percent. So this is not just demographics. This is young people looking at nursing and deciding, no thank you.

And you can understand why. The curriculum is grueling. Wages have stagnated. The working conditions are hard. Compare that to the United States and Australia, which have roughly doubled their nursing pipelines by offering better pay and real career pathways — like becoming a nurse practitioner, with more autonomy and higher status. Japan hasn't done that. Instead, schools are compressing their curricula and dangling full tuition forgiveness in exchange for years of service.

The trouble is, that's a band-aid. It papers over the deeper problem — that Japan hasn't made nursing a competitive profession. And the people who will feel this first are in the countryside, where rural hospitals already run on thin margins. When those programs close, regional healthcare doesn't just strain. It collapses. In an aging society that needs more care than ever, that's a quiet crisis worth watching closely.
