We begin this morning with money — specifically, the question of whether Japan can afford the tax cut its Prime Minister just delivered.

Sanae Takaichi has pushed through what the Nikkei describes as her "personal aspiration" — cutting the consumption tax on food all the way down to one percent. Now, for context, the consumption tax is Japan's version of a national sales tax, and food has long been taxed at a reduced rate to soften the blow for households. Takaichi has now taken it nearly to zero. It's popular. It's also, according to Japan's own economists, a serious gamble.

And I want to sit with just how striking that economist reaction is. The Nikkei reports on an Economist Panel survey in which eighty-eight percent of Japanese economists believe this policy will do net economic harm. Eighty-eight percent. You rarely see a consensus that lopsided among economists, who famously agree on very little.

Here's the worry. This is a two-year measure, but the government has delayed disclosing how it will pay for it until December. That's the phrase everyone's fixated on — the "hidden deficit." The fear is that the funding will come, quietly, through deficit bonds — borrowing, in other words, without saying so upfront. And the Nikkei explicitly invokes Britain's "Liz Truss shock" of a few years back, when an unfunded tax-cutting budget spooked markets so badly it toppled a prime minister within weeks.

Japan is doing this at a delicate moment — energy prices rising, the central bank tightening. The concern is that unfunded tax cuts on top of inflation could test the market's patience with Japan's finances. And it's already drawing political fire. An unlikely coalition of opposition parties — the Public Welfare Reform League, the Constitutional Democratic Party, and Komei — plans joint counter-proposals challenging how Takaichi intends to pay for all this. So watch December. That's when the bill comes due, literally.

Now, staying with money flowing through Japan — but this time flowing in. The Canadian investment giant Brookfield has announced it's buying more than one hundred billion yen worth of rental apartments across Japan's four major metropolitan regions. The Nikkei calls it the largest residential real estate transaction of 2026. And it fits a bigger pattern: foreign money is pouring into Japanese housing. Investment last year hit around nine hundred and eighty billion yen — a record since 2005. This is happening even as Tokyo apartment prices climb twelve to sixteen percent in the single-occupancy market. The takeaway is that global investors see Japanese property as a bargain and a safe harbor, weak yen and all.

There's a related theme here — the world betting on Japan's future. The Nikkei reports that SRI International — the storied American research institute — is launching a venture fund in Japan through its VC arm, teaming up with LINE Yahoo's Z Venture Capital. They're aiming for a thirty to fifty billion yen AI deeptech fund by the end of the year. And here's the fun detail: SRI is where the technology behind Apple's Siri was born. They'll license that intellectual property to Japanese startups, backing twenty to twenty-five companies in AI, space, and robotics. It's an attempt to import a proven American innovation model onto Japanese soil.

Let me turn now to something more anxious — the Middle East, and Japan's oil.

Following escalated military conflict between Iran and the United States, Japanese crude imports through the Strait of Hormuz have plummeted. That strait is the narrow chokepoint through which a huge share of the world's oil passes — and much of Japan's. The government's response, according to the Nikkei, is unusual: it will draft legislation this autumn allowing the state to assume the reinsurance costs for oil tankers operating in those high-risk waters. In plain terms, the government would backstop the insurance so tankers can keep sailing — because private insurers may not cover the risk anymore. It's a quiet but telling measure of how fragile Japan feels about its energy lifeline.

And yet — here's the paradox — the oil market itself has stopped panicking. Nikkei traders report that statements from Trump and from Iran no longer move crude prices much at all. The market has, in effect, gotten used to the drama. The geopolitical premium is fading as investors simply reroute toward supply outside the Strait of Hormuz. A ceasefire accord between the two sides is eroding as a sixty-day negotiating deadline approaches on the seventeenth — that's Monday — with minimal progress. But the market has moved on. Which tells you something about how conflict becomes background noise.

When two of the planet's biggest sugar nations both have a bad harvest at once, the ripples travel far. It's a small, tangible lesson in a world where a drought becomes an economic force — and where, sometimes, the most global story starts with rain that didn't fall.
