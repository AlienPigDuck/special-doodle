Good morning, and welcome to The Papers Say — Japan's Nikkei newspaper, in English, every morning. It's Monday, the tenth of August, 2026, and if there's one thread running through the paper this morning, it's oil. Or rather, the lack of it, and what happens to a country like Japan when the world's energy arteries start to close.

Let's start with the lead story, because it's a fascinating one, and it's about a kind of Japanese company that outsiders often struggle to understand.

The Nikkei calls them "the backstage enablers" — Japan's great general trading houses. You'll know the names: Mitsubishi Corporation, Marubeni, Mitsui, Sumitomo, and Itochu. These are the "sogo shosha," the trading companies that buy, sell, ship, and finance nearly everything Japan needs from the outside world. And this morning, the Nikkei argues that during the recent Middle East energy crisis, these five firms functioned as something close to national infrastructure.

Here's what happened. When the Strait of Hormuz — the narrow waterway through which so much of the world's oil passes — was effectively blockaded, Japan's supply of naphtha started to dry up. Naphtha is the raw material behind plastics and chemicals, so this matters. The trading houses moved fast. They released emergency stock from domestic reserves, and they scrambled to find alternative supplies — from Peru, and from India.

Now, to understand why this is such a big deal, you need to sit with a couple of numbers. Japan depends on the Middle East for ninety percent of its crude oil. And its own energy self-sufficiency? Just fifteen percent. That is an extraordinarily exposed position for the world's fourth-largest economy. These trading houses have earned more than three trillion yen in annual profits over the past five years by knitting together supply chains across the globe — but the Nikkei's point is that in a crisis, they're not just profit-makers. They're a strategic asset, a role they've played, in one form or another, since the merchant houses of the nineteenth century.

And here's a telling detail buried further in the paper: European carmakers, BMW among them, are now urging their own governments to study this Japanese model — the long-term resource partnerships, the supply-chain orchestration — as a blueprint for securing critical minerals. So a distinctly Japanese institution is suddenly looking like something the rest of the world wants to copy.

Now, why was Hormuz closed in the first place? The international pages fill in that picture, and it's a tense one. On the ninth, Yemen's Houthi militia — backed by Iran — struck Saudi Aramco's Jizan refinery, setting off a fire and continuing a grim tit-for-tat that's tangling up U.S.–Iran ceasefire talks. Iran's crude exports have collapsed by seventy percent after Washington re-imposed a blockade in July — from more than a million barrels a day in June down to just three hundred thousand by late July. And now Tehran is making a demand: it wants the U.S. to lift its port blockade, stop the attacks, and pay reparations before it will reopen the Strait. The catch, as the Nikkei notes, is that Iran's proposed route would effectively hand it control of maritime traffic through Hormuz. So this is far from resolved.

China is watching all of this and drawing a lesson. According to the Nikkei, Beijing is pouring fourteen trillion yen into coal-to-chemicals projects — turning coal into the products it would normally make from imported oil. The projects are concentrated in Shaanxi and other regions, and they'll require thirty million tons of coal a year by 2027. It's a bet on energy security that sits uncomfortably with China's climate pledges, but that tells you how seriously Beijing takes the risk of another Hormuz.

And there's a warning sign in China's own economy this morning. Consumer prices rose just half a percent in July, year over year — down from one percent in June, and below one percent for the first time in six months. Core inflation, stripping out food and energy, also slipped under one percent. That's weak demand, weak confidence — a Chinese consumer who isn't spending, against the backdrop of all this turmoil.

Two more from the international desk, briefly. President Zelensky has disclosed that North Korea and Russia have agreed to send thirty to fifty thousand North Korean troops to fight for Russia — on top of forty ballistic missiles and ninety missile personnel already deployed. The worry here isn't only Ukraine; it's that North Korea's army is gaining real, modern combat experience, which is unsettling for the whole Indo-Pacific. And on the diplomatic side, the Russian agency TASS reports that Trump's peace envoy Keith Kellogg, along with Jared Kushner, may visit both Kyiv and Moscow within the next week to ten days — the first Moscow trip since January.

Let's come home now, to a debate that touches almost every Japanese family. The Nikkei's front page carries a striking survey. Among Japanese physicians — doctors themselves — sixty-four percent support raising out-of-pocket healthcare costs for the elderly. Right now, people over seventy-five pay just one to two percent of their medical bills. The idea on the table would move them toward the thirty percent that working-age people pay. Seventy-eight percent of the doctors surveyed said they're worried about whether the system can survive. This is the arithmetic of an aging society colliding with a shrinking tax base. The governing Liberal Democratic Party and the Japan Innovation Party are discussing reform by the end of this year. The hard part, of course, is protecting low-income seniors who might simply stop seeking care.

On defense: the Defense Ministry will start installing cybersecurity software directly onto Air Self-Defense Force radars and fighter jets by 2027. The technology comes from a Tokyo startup called NodeX. It verifies whether a piece of equipment is genuine, watches for intrusions, and can automatically cut communications to stop an attack spreading. What's notable is the shift in thinking — from protecting computer networks to protecting the weapons themselves.

A few quick notes from the business pages. Tesla is expanding in Japan, going from seven delivery centers to eleven by year's end, with new sites in Yokohama, Kobe, and Nagoya, after sales jumped to around twelve thousand cars in the first half of this year. Mitsubishi Electric is launching something called "Executive Seeds 30" — picking thirty employees in their thirties and early forties for intensive training, which quietly breaks Japan's tradition of choosing leaders late in their careers. The Ohi nuclear plant's Unit 3 in Fukui shut itself down automatically on the ninth after a magnetic-field anomaly — no radiation released, cause still under investigation. And the fintech firm Digital Garage begins offering stablecoin payments to retailers today — that's digital currency pegged to the dollar or yen — with lower fees than credit cards.

And out in Las Vegas, the AI world is arguing with itself. Geoffrey Hinton — the Nobel laureate often called a godfather of AI — warned about systems developing a kind of self-preservation instinct. Stanford's Andrew Ng pushed back, saying defensive AI will keep us safe. And Fei-Fei Li urged everyone to stay scientific and skip the science-fiction dread.

Now, the story that caught my eye — and it's tucked away in the business pages, which is exactly why it deserves the light.

JR East, the big railway operator, is closing nine kilometers of the Kururi Line in Chiba — the stretch between Kimitsu and Kazusa-Kameyama. That may sound minor. It isn't. This is the first time since Japan privatized its railways in 1987 — thirty-seven years — that a line has been closed purely because too few people ride it. And how few? Daily ridership on that segment fell ninety percent, from eight hundred and twenty-three passengers down to seventy-six.

Here's why this haunts me. Japan's population has only just begun to shrink. The trains, the water pipes, the power lines — all of it was built for a country that was growing. Now a smaller number of people must pay to maintain infrastructure built for a bigger nation. The Kururi Line is a test case for something much larger: how does a country gracefully let go of what it can no longer afford to keep? Nostalgia won't lay the tracks. This little branch line in Chiba is, quietly, a glimpse of Japan's future.
