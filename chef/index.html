Chef

On-demand meal plans that hit your macros

Chef is one tab of Fit, a self-hosted app that Claude made to help me track my 3 month coaching block with Sam. It's a bit overkill :) but was fun to make. The rest of the app sets the day's targets for calories, protein, fat and carbs. Chef turns those numbers into food. It does three jobs.

Tool 1

Give me a meal plan for today

Tap Simple or Fancy and a full day appears. No AI here: the plan is assembled from the recipe library by a scoring routine.

Gives me...

  • Lunch and dinner (I rarely have breakfast but that could be added), each a real recipe with macros, prep time and a fold-out recipe.
  • Sides: shakes, yoghurt, rice, fruit, almonds or olive oil. These pull the day onto target.
  • A scoreboard: total against target, green when close, orange when not.

Simple versus Fancy

Lunch is always a quick recipe. Simple makes dinner quick too. Fancy picks dinner from the recipes that take more effort. This is just the instruction I gave Claude when building it

Then

  • New plan rerolls without repeating the mains just shown.
  • Keep dinner holds the dinner and rerolls the rest.
  • Log this day writes every item into today's food diary on the nutrition tab.

How it decides

  1. ShortlistLunch-or-dinner recipes with a known protein source and macros, not flagged as hard to shop for in Japan. 45 of the 89.
  2. Draw a dinner, then a lunchRandom picks. Lunch is steered to a different protein source from dinner.
  3. Fill with sidesUp to two whey shakes for protein, yoghurt if still short; rice then fruit for carbs; almonds or oil for fat. Stops if calories are already over.
  4. ScoreProtein shortfall costs most, then carbs, then distance from the calorie target. Missing the fat or protein floor adds a flat penalty.
  5. Repeat 140 timesKeep the best version of each lunch-and-dinner pair, then show a random one of the top six. Variety without losing accuracy.
Protein and fat are treated as floors, not targets. Carbs and calories are the flex.
Tool 2

Fill the rest of my day

Lunch is already logged. What does dinner need to be?

The app does the sums

It reads today's food log and shows what is left of each target. A negative number flips to over and turns orange.

The AI picks the food

Suggest what to eat sends the AI the targets, everything eaten so far with its macros, and the exact remaining numbers. It is asked for three options with amounts in grams and an estimated total each.

Its rules

  • Protein first, even if calories run slightly over.
  • Specific amounts: "180 g chicken breast + 200 g cooked rice".
  • If fat is already over its floor, go lean and keep added fat minimal.
Tool 3

Ask the chef

"How do I make this?", "What can I do with chicken and rice?", "A swap for the salmon?" Short answers, macros attached.

The persona

A practical, upbeat chef for someone busy, cutting, and wanting tasty, simple, varied food. Its standing orders:

  • Keep it quick unless asked for something involved.
  • Stick to staple foods and a normal Japanese supermarket.
  • Give macros with any dish or swap, and say they are estimates.
  • Metric units, short ingredient list, numbered steps.
  • Sweet things are budgeted, not banned.

What it knows today

Body weight, diet phase, the four targets, and the eating pattern: no breakfast, lunch and dinner, dinner biggest, training at six.

Memory

The conversation is kept, so follow-ups work. clear wipes it.

The PDFs

A collection of fitness recipe books in PDF form that I've gathered over the years was read page by page and each recipe converted into the same structure: name, meal, simple or fancy, prep time, servings, macros, ingredients, method. Two of the books supplied most of the library; a third added a few. The rest were taken from named recipe sites, mostly macro-focused ones, plus a handful of Japanese dishes (gyudon, oyakodon, miso salmon, mapo tofu).

Where the macros come from

Each recipe's calories, protein, fat and carbs are per serving, taken from the book's or site's own nutrition figures. Where a source gave no figures, they were estimated from the ingredient amounts; 32 recipes are flagged this way and the plan marks them with "≈ est". The side items use fixed values for one fixed portion. The AI's figures are always estimates and it is told to say so.

Japan check

Each recipe was checked against what a normal Japanese supermarket stocks. Twenty-four got a substitution note (ground turkey is scarce, use chicken or beef). Ten were flagged impractical and the planner skips them.

Under the hood

The AI and the API key

Tools 2 and 3 are Claude, called with an Anthropic API key that lives in the app's server config.

  • Every tap on Suggest or Send is one paid request to the Anthropic API, made by the app's own server. Nothing runs in the browser.
  • The model is set to low effort, which keeps answers quick and cheap. Plenty for food.
  • Without a key, the AI buttons disappear and the page says so. Tool 1 and all the arithmetic keep working.
  • The AI never sets targets, never reads the diary directly and never logs anything. It is handed numbers and asked to write around them.
