Chef

On-demand meal plans that hit your macros

Chef is one tab of Fit, a self-hosted fitness app that tracks a diet and training plan. The rest of the app knows the day's targets: calories, protein, fat and carbs. Chef turns those numbers into actual food, so the question "what do I eat today?" never has to be answered from scratch.

It does three jobs, shown below in the order they appear on the page. One is pure arithmetic over a recipe library. The other two hand the arithmetic to an AI and let it do the talking.

Tool 1

Give me a meal plan for today

Tap Simple or Fancy and a full day appears a moment later. No AI is involved here. The plan comes from a library of 89 recipes and a handful of fixed side items, assembled by a small scoring routine.

What you get

  • A lunch and a dinner, each a real recipe with calories and macros, a prep time, and a fold-out recipe (ingredients, numbered method, and a note on substitutions for Japan where needed).
  • "On the side": protein shakes, Greek yoghurt, rice, fruit, a few almonds or a teaspoon of olive oil. These are the levers that pull the day onto target.
  • A scoreboard at the top: the day's total against each target, with the difference in green when it is close and orange when it is not.

Simple versus Fancy

Lunch is always drawn from the quick recipes, because lunch on a work day has to be quick. Simple draws dinner from the same quick pool. Fancy draws dinner from the recipes that take more effort, for an evening when cooking is the point.

After the plan appears

  • New plan rerolls the whole day and will not repeat the mains you were just shown.
  • Keep dinner holds the dinner you liked and rerolls everything around it.
  • Log this day writes every item straight into today's food diary, so a day that was planned here counts as eaten once you follow it.

How the planner decides

  1. Shortlist the mainsOnly recipes that can be a lunch or dinner, have a named protein source, have macros, and are not flagged as impractical to shop for in Japan. That leaves 45 of the 89: 38 quick ones and 7 for Fancy.
  2. Draw a dinner, then a lunchRandom picks from the right pool. The lunch is steered away from the dinner's protein source, so you are not eating chicken twice.
  3. Fill the gaps with sidesA fixed sequence: up to two whey shakes until protein is close, then yoghurt if still short; rice portions and then fruit until carbs are close; then almonds or olive oil until the fat floor is met. Every step stops if the day is already well over its calories.
  4. Score the dayProtein shortfall is punished hardest, then carbs, then distance from the calorie target. Landing under the fat floor or the protein floor adds a flat penalty. Going far over calories adds another.
  5. Repeat 140 times, keep the best of each pairingEvery lunch-and-dinner combination keeps only its best-scoring version. The plan shown is a random pick from the top six, so repeat taps give variety without giving up accuracy.
Protein and fat are treated as floors, not targets. The diet method behind the app says protein protects muscle while cutting and a minimum of fat protects hormones. Carbs and total calories are the flex. An earlier version ignored fat entirely and most generated days came in well under the floor, which is why almonds and olive oil now appear on the side.

Tool 2

Fill the rest of my day

For the more common situation: it is mid-afternoon, lunch is already in the diary, and the question is what dinner needs to be to finish the day on target.

The app does the sums

The card reads today's food log and shows what is left of each target: calories, protein, carbs and fat. If a number has gone negative it flips to over and turns orange. This part is plain arithmetic and works whether or not the AI is configured.

The AI picks the food

Tapping Suggest what to eat sends the AI a tightly written brief: the daily targets, the list of what has been eaten with its macros, the totals so far, and the exact remaining numbers. It is asked for three distinct options, each with specific metric amounts and an estimated total, and told to say in one line if an option cannot hit every macro.

The rules it is given

  • Protein is the priority. Get close to the remaining protein even if that runs calories slightly over.
  • Adjust serving sizes freely to hit the numbers. "180 g chicken breast + 200 g cooked rice", not "some chicken".
  • If the day is already over the fat floor, the brief changes: favour lean protein and low-fat carbs and keep added fat minimal, because the fat calories have already been spent. The card warns about this too.
  • If fat is still short, top up toward the floor.
Tool 3

Ask the chef

Free-form chat. "How do I make this?", "What can I do with chicken and rice?", "Is there a swap for the salmon?" The answers are short and come with macros attached.

Who you are talking to

The AI is given a persona and a set of standing instructions before every message. In short: a practical, upbeat personal chef for someone who is busy, wants tasty, simple, varied food, and is currently cutting.

  • Keep it simple and quick unless asked for something involved.
  • Prefer the staple foods and things buyable in a normal Japanese supermarket.
  • Give calories and macros with any dish or swap, and say they are estimates.
  • Metric units. Ingredients as a short list, method as a few numbered steps.
  • Sweet things are budgeted rather than banned. If the day's numbers leave room, a treat is fine.

What it knows about today

Current body weight, the diet phase, the day's four targets, and that a recipe library of 89 dishes exists in the app. It also knows the eating pattern: breakfast skipped, lunch and dinner the two meals, dinner the bigger one, training around six in the evening.

Memory

The conversation is stored, so follow-up questions work ("make that spicier", "and without the rice?"). The clear link at the top of the card wipes it and starts fresh.

Under the hood

What is AI and what is not

A deliberate split. Anything that must be exactly right is computed by the app. The AI is only ever asked to choose foods and write sentences around numbers it has been handed.

JobDone byWhy
Daily targets (calories, protein, fat, carbs)The app, from the current diet blockThey are the plan. Nothing in Chef can change them.
Building a day from the recipe libraryThe app, scoring routineRepeatable, instant, and it never invents a recipe or a macro.

Cooking questions, swaps, recipesAI (Claude), with the chef personaOpen-ended, and the answers are advice, not records.
Writing a plan into the diaryThe app, on "Log this day"Only a tap commits anything. Nothing is logged automatically.
Every macro figure that comes from the AI is labelled an estimate, and the chef is instructed to say so itself. The recipe library's numbers are fixed; 32 of the 89 recipes carry an "estimated" flag where the source did not give full nutrition data, and the plan marks those with "≈ est".

The AI calls go straight to the Anthropic API from the app's own server, with the model set to run at low effort. That keeps each answer cheap and quick, which is right for meal suggestions. If no API key is configured, the page says so, and Tool 1 and the arithmetic in Tool 2 keep working without it.

The whey-based entries are mostly breakfasts, bars and shakes. They are in the library for "Ask the chef" to draw on but are not used as mains by the planner.

Japan notes. The app is used in Japan, so each recipe was checked against what a normal supermarket there stocks. Twenty-four carry a substitution note (ground turkey is hard to find, use chicken or beef and fresh chilli; that kind of thing). Ten are flagged as impractical and are excluded from the planner altogether, though they remain readable.

Some of the Japanese dishes in the library: Gyudon, Oyakodon, Teriyaki Salmon Bowls, Miso Salmon, High Protein Mapo Tofu, Korean Ground Beef with gochujang, Shrimp Poke Bowl, Air Fryer Teriyaki Chicken.
